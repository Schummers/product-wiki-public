# Product Management

## Definition

Product management is the discipline of guiding a product through its lifecycle:
defining strategy, prioritizing work, and ensuring the product delivers value to
both users and the business ([[2024-10-25_product-ux-glossary]]). Christian
Crumlish compresses the role into one word, value: the product manager is
"responsible for value, through the coordination and delivery of customer
experiences," making sure the experience delivered to customers and other
stakeholders provides enough value to be "hired" by the user and developed as a
sustainable concern, ideally in service of a broader vision
([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_04-chapter-1-what-exactly-does-a-product-manager-do]]).
Sustainability, in his reading, means finding repeatable cycles of inputs and
outcomes with the people and money inputs at least steady; the value sustained
by the organization is "a fair share of the value created for the customer."

What *value* itself means, Crumlish does not define himself: he quotes Jay
Zaveri, his chief product officer at CloudOn and later head of a product
incubator at Social Capital, for whom value is "something special that a person
or customer experiences that never existed in the same way for them in the
past" — useful, usable and desirable, and apparent when something is
technologically differentiated, abundantly available, and changes human
behavior. Zaveri warns against mistaking the scoreboard for the thing: "No true
value is created by just financial and growth metrics." LinkedIn product lead
B. Pagels-Minor supplies a shorter test of the same idea: "Something the user
values and repeatedly uses" (same chapter; see [[Value Proposition]]).

*User Story Mapping* offers a matching refusal to reduce product management to requirements-gathering, from a different angle. In his foreword, Marty Cagan contrasts the practices of strong and weak product teams: good teams pursue a compelling product vision "with missionary-like passion" and draw ideas from KPIs, customer observation, and data, while bad teams are "mercenaries" who passively gather requirements from sales and stakeholders and build whatever is on the roadmap without expecting to iterate [[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_03-foreword-by-marty-cagan]]. Patton's own preface positions story maps as something product managers need alongside UX practitioners, POs, business analysts, and Agile coaches, to bridge from high-level vision to executable detail and shared team understanding — the recurring point being that stories are "not a way to write better requirements, but a way to organize and have better conversations" [[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_04-preface]].

The corpus also holds a deliberate refusal to define the role at all. In the
book's introduction, Crumlish writes that what a product manager is "depends on
who the person is, where the job is, and how the role is being executed," and
that the correct relationship between product and UX likewise "depends"
([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_03-introduction]]). He also flags
a shorthand the book uses throughout: practitioners say *product* on its own for
*product management*, a deliberate choice not to lean into the ambiguous
*management* part, and also to mean product thinking in general, covering
product design, development and marketing.

## Practice

### What the role is not

Crumlish separates the product manager from two neighbours it is routinely
confused with
([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_04-chapter-1-what-exactly-does-a-product-manager-do]]):

- **Not a project manager.** A project manager's core expertise is keeping
  projects on track, on time and on budget, not defining value or driving
  strategy. Project management can legitimately be one of a PM's
  responsibilities, but Crumlish says it should be "only a sliver of their time
  and attention." He quotes Matt LeMay of Sudden Compass on the difference:
  "Product managers have both the opportunity and the responsibility to ask
  'why?'" See [[Project Management]].
- **Not a product owner.** In the definition the book adopts, a product owner
  shapes and guides the engineering team, is primarily focused on tracking
  tasks, comes out of the engineering pool, and was "invented in the absence of
  true product managers." Crumlish notes the title is used many other ways in
  practice, including for a primary business stakeholder or, in some government
  contexts, for a role closer to head of product. See [[Agile Development]].
- **Not a design job.** "Product management is not a design job. It does require
  some familiarity with design and a deep sensitivity to user experience
  concerns, but the day-to-day tasks and craft work of product management rarely
  involve pushing pixels"
  ([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_05-chapter-2-do-you-want-to-be-a-product-manager]]).
  The book's FAQ makes the same point from the other end: product managers are
  not designers, and the day-to-day work of the two roles differs significantly
  despite large overlapping areas of shared concern
  ([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_01-frequently-asked-questions]]).

### Five archetypes the role descends from

Rather than one origin story, Crumlish traces five, and says the role is
assembled from all of them
([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_04-chapter-1-what-exactly-does-a-product-manager-do]]):

1. **Business manager** — responsible for the business case, strategy and
   financial viability.
2. **Marketing manager** — the historic twentieth-century origin, and the source
   of the obsession with customer needs and product-market fit.
3. **Engineering manager** — technical PM interviews, and Google's Associate
   Product Manager program pioneered by Marissa Mayer.
4. **Experimental explorer** — Marty Cagan of Silicon Valley Product Group and
   *Inspired*, Rich Mironov, and the "Build, Measure, Learn" cycle from Eric
   Ries's *The Lean Startup*, brought into the UX community by Jeff Gothelf and
   Josh Seiden. Crumlish adds a caveat about the loop's name: despite the
   ordering and the fact that it is a cycle, "you generally do not start with
   building" — you start by learning or measuring something, then build.
5. **Creative artist** — design thinking, the Apple mythology, and UX leads
   moving onto the product track.

The strategic repertoire follows the same accumulation: market sizing from
marketing, technical depth from engineering, the lean loop, problem reframing
from design (see [[Product Strategy]]).

### Three traits

Crumlish says great product managers share three
([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_04-chapter-1-what-exactly-does-a-product-manager-do]]):

- **Curious** — "curious almost to the extent of feeling like a busybody, being
  nosy, wanting to 'know all the things,' keeping tabs on everything, and being
  incredibly 'high context' and thorough about understanding."
- **Connecting** — providing "the glue or lubrication" of emotional intelligence
  and soft skills that let teams thrive.
- **Courageous** — brave enough to take risks, evaluate failures coldly and
  learn from them, which sets a tone that encourages others.

### The work of a day

The concrete inventory, from
[[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_05-chapter-2-do-you-want-to-be-a-product-manager]]:
writing specification docs, decomposing epics into user stories, grooming the
backlog, planning sprints, answering developer questions on tickets, reviewing
demos and automated test data, reading burn-down charts, running retrospectives,
plus email, Slack, calendar work, biweekly Agile rituals, and roadmap reporting
to leadership quarterly if not monthly. Chapter 3 adds the shape of it: PMs
spend most of their time writing (email, specs, user stories, reports), in
meetings, and consuming data and information; they do not use design software or
produce visual work
([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_06-chapter-3-ux-skills-that-carry-over]]).

Crumlish also offers a "typical day," from a 4:30 a.m. wake-up to checking data
one more time before sleep — and immediately disowns it as a template, closing
with "that's just me" and promising other PMs' days later in the book. The
generic day is his, not the role's.

He frames data as the PM's raw material by analogy: as a potter develops a feel
for clay and a UX practitioner for digital materials, a PM's material is data,
requiring "deep immersion for sustained periods," until the PM has "very
sensitive antennae" that react when a morning North Star report shows an
unexpected dip in daily active users or a spike in sales (see
[[Business Metrics]]).

### Do you need to love numbers?

The book contradicts itself here on purpose, and the disagreement is worth
keeping
([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_05-chapter-2-do-you-want-to-be-a-product-manager]]).
Crumlish poses three screening questions to anyone considering the switch: do I
love spreadsheets, am I a good writer, am I deeply curious about interpersonal
dynamics. He then writes that you "really do have to like numbers, math,
analysis, spreadsheets, and databases to be a great product manager." The
sidebar on the same pages softens it immediately:

- **Clement Kao** (Principal PM at Blend, cofounder of Product Manager HQ) says
  truly amazing B2B PMs can get away without numbers, because they are so deeply
  locked in with each customer's priorities — though there is no escaping
  numbers in B2C.
- **Matt LeMay** (Sudden Compass) is blunter: "This is sometimes but not always
  true. I don't like any of those things ('numbers, math, analysis, spreadsheet,
  or databases'), but I have the three qualities you described earlier."

### Decision-making, and its limits

Chapter 3 makes decision-making the substance of the role: big strategic
decisions, medium ones about whether to ship, small ones about whether a bug
blocks a release, tiny ones about backlog sequencing — "an unending cascade of
decisions," and PMs do not get to say "it depends"
([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_06-chapter-3-ux-skills-that-carry-over]]).
The chapter then bounds that authority rather than celebrating it:

- UX leader **Peter Boersma** notes PMs have no monopoly on final decisions:
  designers own design choices, engineers own technical architecture.
- **Matt LeMay** warns that people who move to PM because they want to be "the
  decider" often lose their team's trust by deciding in a siloed,
  anti-collaborative way; he hopes people coming from UX will be more thoughtful
  partners, having felt what exclusion from a decision is like.
- "For some reason," Crumlish writes, there is a consensus among PMs that even
  when you are doing well you will be wrong 25–35% of the time; the job is to
  decide at the speed required, not to gather every possible data point.
  **Clement Kao** adds that not deciding is itself a decision with consequences.

See [[Decision Making]].

### Facilitation, not command

Product managers rarely have direct reports for the people who do the work —
designers, developers, customer support, marketing — so leadership is
demonstrated rather than bestowed, and runs through persuasion, influence, and
making everyone else's life easier
([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_06-chapter-3-ux-skills-that-carry-over]]).
Toward engineers specifically, the FAQ puts it as focus rather than authority:
PMs do not command engineers, they focus and organize their efforts, and
building that competency is essential to PM success
([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_01-frequently-asked-questions]]).
Good PMs stay one abstraction layer away from design: they care deeply about
design and advocate for UX, but provide requirements, guide and facilitate
implementation without treading on the design team's domain
([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_06-chapter-3-ux-skills-that-carry-over]];
see [[Design Leadership]]).

That role clarity is also what keeps the relationship healthy at the
organizational level. Whether product and UX teams experience turf wars or
partnership depends on clear role definitions and on leadership articulating
responsibilities, not on the disciplines themselves
([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_01-frequently-asked-questions]]).
Nielsen Norman Group reaches a compatible conclusion from a different angle,
recommending a RACI matrix so that each task has exactly one accountable person
and UX and product responsibilities stop colliding
([[2022-06-05_ux-roles-responsibilities]]).

### Patton's product owner: leadership, not task-tracking

*User Story Mapping* uses the title "product owner" for much of the part Crumlish describes for product management, and gives it a different shape than Crumlish's "not a product owner" bullet above. Patton's product owner keeps the team focused on business vision and strategy, makes the final call when tradeoffs are needed, and holds accountability for a cohesive product — leadership and orchestration, not writing every story or deciding everything alone [[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_17-12-rock-breakers]]. That reads close to what Crumlish reserves for the product manager, and sits in tension with his claim that a product owner is "primarily focused on tracking tasks" and was "invented in the absence of true product managers" ([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_04-chapter-1-what-exactly-does-a-product-manager-do]]); the two books use the same title for two different jobs, and neither corrects the other.

Patton's argument for why one person cannot do the job alone is structural: there are too many story conversations for a single product owner to attend, and no one person holds the business, user-experience, and engineering expertise needed at once to find a solution that is valuable, usable, and feasible [[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_17-12-rock-breakers]]. His answer is a small, cross-functional discovery team of two to four people (see [[Product Trio]]), and, during delivery, the "three amigos" — a tester, a member of the core discovery team, and a user experience designer or business analyst, with a developer from the building team also in the room — who finalize story details and acceptance criteria together in the last conversation before building [[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_17-12-rock-breakers]]. He also names the failure mode this guards against: the client-vendor anti-pattern, where conversation is replaced by specifications and handoffs, because clients cannot predict solutions and vendors lack the context to build the right one; he prefers a doctor-patient or producer-artist relationship instead, crediting the producer metaphor to a conversation with Agile practitioner David Hussman [[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_17-12-rock-breakers]].

Decisions themselves run through the same three perspectives across the whole product lifecycle, not only at the story-workshop level: the customer and user perspective (who they are, how they work, what they need), the technical perspective (what is feasible), and the business perspective (what outcomes matter), brought together through conversation from the earliest opportunity discussion through discovery, delivery, build, evaluation, and release [[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_16-11-rock-breaking]].

### Information architecture as product leadership

Crumlish identifies information architecture as perhaps the single most directly
applicable UX skill to the role: it supplies a toolkit for mapping the "meaning
layer" of a product, abstracted away from pixels and code, and its artifacts
(concept models, diagrams, journey maps, flow diagrams) act as edge artifacts
that several disciplines can rally around — maps are posters, and they make the
product visible to everyone
([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_06-chapter-3-ux-skills-that-carry-over]]).
The FAQ generalizes it into an organizational claim: an organization benefits
from having at least one person with real depth in information architecture
([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_01-frequently-asked-questions]]).
See [[Information Architecture]].

### From idealism to pragmatism

"UX is a role for idealists, purists, seekers, and revolutionaries. Compromise
is hard for such folks. Product managers do not have the luxury of purity or
single-minded idealism." They still have ideals, values and North Star goals,
but also many masters, and much of product is navigating balance between
competing priorities — sales, data, customer support, the CEO, the roadmap —
without losing sight of the mission
([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_06-chapter-3-ux-skills-that-carry-over]]).
Crumlish presents this as a different role, not a betrayal of UX values, and
names synthesis — holding multiple converging forces and cross-pressures in one
solution — as the greatest strength a UX person carries in, frequently producing
a better result than any single strong point of view would.

Matt LeMay's foreword sets the same expectation earlier and more bluntly. UX
professionals often arrive at product carrying a "redemption narrative" — that
becoming a PM will finally elevate UX and let them treat the team with respect —
and the deadlines, estimates, high-stakes decisions and high-pressure
stakeholders of the job "inevitably complicate your best-laid plans and purest
ambitions," until you start to understand why the previous PMs were "like that."
LeMay's conclusion is not to abandon the ambition but to adapt the user-centric
superpowers to product constraints, with real-world guidance rather than good
intentions alone
([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_02-foreword]]).

### Why UX people consider the move

Two sources in the corpus treat PM as a career destination for designers.
Artiom Dashinsky presents it as the full-time path with the biggest potential
for future growth, noting that director, VP and Chief Product Officer positions
are more common than their design equivalents, and quoting Intercom's definition
of the role
([[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_03-1-design-career-paths]]).
Crumlish sorts the motivations into three families — professional and career,
ambition, and evolving interests — calls all of them legitimate, and then tests
them: he warns against the grass-is-greener fallacy that romanticizes the road
not taken "and minimizes the mowing, weeding, and manure spreading that produced
your neighbor's green lawn," and names two unintended consequences of moving out
of ambition alone: perpetuating an inferior approach to product management that
dominates UX without using its value wisely, and losing what makes the work
meaningful
([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_05-chapter-2-do-you-want-to-be-a-product-manager]]).
One consequence is not a risk but a guarantee: the burden of decision-making.
The chapter also reprints John Cutler's essay "44 Signs You Are Becoming a
Product Manager" (Cutler was head of product research and education at
Amplitude), whose signs include being pressured for estimates you know are
worthless, building a roadmap everyone buys into just before everything changes,
shipping a dud feature nobody uses and an awesome one nobody notices, being "the
single wringable neck," and being empowered on paper while taking orders. See
[[UX Career]] and [[Trade-Off]].
