# Content Strategy

## Definition

Content strategy, across these sources, is the discipline of deciding what
information to publish, in what format, at what length, and in what structure,
so that people can complete the task that brought them there. It is not writing
alone: the same body of guidance covers whether an image earns its place
([[2017-05-21_big-pictures-small-screens]], [[2024-05-03_7-tips-memorable-imagery]]),
whether a document should be a PDF or an HTML page
([[2020-06-28_avoid-pdf-for-on-screen-reading]]), when a video is the right
medium ([[2020-01-19_instructional-video-guidelines]]), and where a piece of
content should permanently live ([[2020-05-31_covid19-intranet-ia]]). It sits
tightly against information architecture: overlapping category names and
scattered, undifferentiated content are treated as content-strategy failures
before they are navigation failures ([[2016-10-30_top-10-enduring]]), and
analytics signals about categories must be read against content strategy,
business goals and user needs rather than against traffic alone
([[2016-09-25_ia-warning-signs-analytics]]).

The empirical foundation is stable and, the sources insist, unchanging: people
scan rather than read, a behaviour documented across 23 years and five
eyetracking studies and found to be nearly identical between American and
Beijing participants ([[2020-04-05_how-people-read-online]]). Users read only
about 20 to 28% of a page's text ([[2026-05-08_ux-writing-faqs]]). What has
changed is what people come for: understand-oriented activity rose from 24% to
40% of critical internet use between 1997 and 2019 while compare-and-choose fell
from 51% to 36%, making comprehensive educational content — not visual design —
the reason people visit a site ([[2020-01-26_information-seeking-behavior-changes]]).
Technology changes quickly, humans do not
([[2020-04-05_how-people-read-online]]), which is why the same guidance now
extends to AI-generated answers ([[2026-04-17_less-chat-more-answer]],
[[2026-05-08_ux-writing-faqs]]).

## Practice

### Write for scanning

- Use clear headings to break content, front-load the important information,
  apply formatting such as bulleted lists and bold text, and write in plain
  language ([[2020-04-05_how-people-read-online]]).
- Aim for a 6th–8th grade reading level. Plain language serves all literacy
  levels, including experts, who are also busy, distracted and scanning; chunked
  content, descriptive subheadings and bulleted lists additionally help users
  with dyslexia, ADHD and other cognitive differences
  ([[2026-05-08_ux-writing-faqs]]). The same sixth-grade-level, short-sentence,
  bulleted approach is recommended for teenagers, who get enough reading at
  school and are frustrated by dense screens
  ([[2019-03-17_usability-of-websites-for-teenagers]]).
- Use jargon only when the audience expects and uses it
  ([[2026-05-08_ux-writing-faqs]]). Terms obvious to insiders — the example
  given is "in-state tuition" — need context or examples for first-time users
  ([[2024-08-23_explicit-differences]]).
- Chunk deliberately: group related information into meaningful units, mark
  their boundaries with headings, white space, highlighting and background
  colour, and keep line lengths around 50–75 characters with short paragraphs
  ([[2016-03-20_chunking]]). Note the correction to a common belief: Miller's
  "seven" describes how many chunks people retain, not a limit on interface
  options, and menus with more than seven items work fine when well structured
  ([[2016-03-20_chunking]]).
- Avoid walls of text and cluttered pages, which are among the most persistent
  web-design mistakes ([[2016-10-30_top-10-enduring]]).
- Be aware of what disrupts linear reading: pull quotes and inline elements
  cause users who had begun reading carefully to switch to light scanning
  ([[2020-04-05_how-people-read-online]]).
- Depth of reading depends on motivation, task type, focus and personal reading
  characteristics — not on the content alone
  ([[2020-04-05_how-people-read-online]]).

### Length, complexity and the mobile constraint

- The mobile comprehension picture is more nuanced than the older received
  wisdom. A study of 276 participants found no practical difference in
  comprehension between phone and computer for easy, linear articles — mobile
  was 3 points higher, statistically significant but not practically meaningful
  — which contradicts earlier findings that mobile reading impaired
  comprehension ([[2016-12-11_mobile-content]]).
- The caveat is complexity: with difficult articles, readers slow down on mobile
  to hold comprehension steady, re-reading and working harder. Brevity and clear
  organisation therefore still matter, and sites with financial, medical,
  scientific or B2B material should run their own mobile studies rather than
  assume desktop results transfer ([[2016-12-11_mobile-content]]).
- Short, easy passages are faster to read on any device, so the mobile
  constraint improves desktop content too ([[2016-12-11_mobile-content]]).

### Support the task, not just the page

- Anticipate what users do not know. A diary study found 21% of online activity
  is research-based, and users frequently fail because sites do not anticipate
  knowledge gaps; missing information derails the task or sends people elsewhere
  ([[2019-03-03_unbridged-knowledge-gaps]]).
- Structure information so alternatives can be compared side by side on
  consistent attributes, and provide tools — calculators, interactive
  comparisons — that reduce mental effort and external-memory load. Aim for a
  self-sufficient experience users can complete without opening other tabs
  ([[2019-03-03_unbridged-knowledge-gaps]]).
- Make differences between options explicit. When options differ on only a few
  attributes, surface those attributes at the top of a comparison table and
  merge cells for what is shared; when options differ on many, highlight only
  the few that matter and put the rest behind progressive disclosure
  ([[2024-08-23_explicit-differences]]).
- Left implicit, users either assume all options are identical or agonise over
  trivial distinctions, choose wrong, pay the interaction cost of fixing it, and
  lose trust in the site ([[2024-08-23_explicit-differences]]).
- Related information scattered across a site with no links between the pieces
  leaves users unable to find supplementary material; consolidate and cross-link
  ([[2016-10-30_top-10-enduring]]).

### Organise, label and maintain

- Category labels must be differentiated. Similar names force users to guess
  which path to take; card sorting and usability testing reveal a structure that
  matches mental models ([[2016-10-30_top-10-enduring]]).
- Read analytics contextually. Low category traffic, low conversion, high
  bounce, low entrance rates and high search-query volume each flag a possible
  IA or labelling problem, but each needs interpretation against strategic
  importance, visual prominence, layout and multi-stage journeys before you
  remove or rename anything; supplement with A/B tests or surveys when ambiguous
  ([[2016-09-25_ia-warning-signs-analytics]]).
- High search-query volume for terms that match no category is a vocabulary
  mismatch, and a candidate for a new or renamed category — if strategic
  importance and content volume justify it
  ([[2016-09-25_ia-warning-signs-analytics]]).
- Plan the permanent home of temporary content. During a crisis, consolidating
  everything into one prominent section is fastest to build and maintain, but
  the severe and common mistake is leaving news items, policies and tools there
  forever; decide when a temporary item becomes long-lasting and where it then
  belongs ([[2020-05-31_covid19-intranet-ia]]).
- Cross-link between related pages and improve metadata (keywords, tags, titles)
  when content necessarily lives in several places; mark deprecated procedures
  explicitly and show last-updated dates and version information so users can
  judge currency ([[2020-05-31_covid19-intranet-ia]]).
- In-page links (jump links) are now widely recognised, but they only work when
  the content is genuinely divisible into distinct sections; a cohesive
  narrative gains nothing, and a short page gains only extra length. Label them
  ("On This Page", "Table of Contents") and style them as links so users can
  tell they stay on the page ([[2023-10-01_in-page-links-content-navigation]]).

### Choose the right format

- Do not use PDFs for on-screen reading. Twenty years of research find them
  print-optimised, hostile to scanning, and slow; the belief that they are
  quicker to publish is wrong once updating, accessibility and link maintenance
  are counted ([[2020-06-28_avoid-pdf-for-on-screen-reading]]).
- Instead, publish an accessible HTML gateway page carrying the core message and
  enough detail to read online, with the PDF as an optional download. If a PDF
  is unavoidable, evidence that users need to print it, make it accessible,
  minimise file size, label the download with size and page count, and remove
  old versions ([[2020-06-28_avoid-pdf-for-on-screen-reading]]).
- Offer multiple formats — HTML, e-reader, audio — rather than a single one
  ([[2020-06-28_avoid-pdf-for-on-screen-reading]]).
- Video supplements text; it never replaces it. Users turn to video when content
  is complex, unfamiliar or multistep, and some never do. Keep overview videos
  under 30 seconds, break multistep processes into several short videos rather
  than one long one, place videos at the top of the page or of their section
  (never the bottom or right rail), show length upfront, use illustrative
  thumbnails, add chapters or timestamps, and include captions
  ([[2020-01-19_instructional-video-guidelines]]).
- Imagery is content, subject to the same value test: choose visuals that
  reinforce the text and its key messages, avoid generic stock photos, show
  products in realistic contexts, place visuals near the text they support,
  restrain quantity, use high resolution, and account for cross-cultural
  interpretation ([[2024-05-03_7-tips-memorable-imagery]]).
- On small screens, ask first whether an image earns its space at all. Remove
  images that are unclear when small, cannot be cropped without losing meaning,
  are obscured by overlay text, or force excess scrolling; otherwise resize or
  re-crop, and move text out of overlays. Being critical of imagery for mobile
  tends to improve the large-screen version too
  ([[2017-05-21_big-pictures-small-screens]]).

### Prioritise and personalise on the page

- Personalised recommendations outperform generic promotions and should be
  placed higher on the page — position materially affects discovery and use
  ([[2018-11-04_recommendation-guidelines]]).
- Split recommendations into specific genre- or behaviour-based categories
  rather than one undifferentiated block, state precisely what data drove each
  suggestion (avoid vague sources such as "and more"), let users rate, edit and
  dismiss, and reflect that feedback quickly so the system visibly learns
  ([[2018-11-04_recommendation-guidelines]]).

### Content in AI-mediated experiences

- Site chatbots are used like search bars, not conversation partners. Users type
  minimal, typo-ridden queries, get terser as trust builds, and abandon bots
  that waste their time ([[2026-04-17_less-chat-more-answer]]).
- Cut pleasantries and sycophancy; they lengthen replies without adding value.
  Keep sentences short and paragraphs to two or three sentences, use lists, bold
  text, headers and white space ([[2026-04-17_less-chat-more-answer]]).
- Follow the truncated-pyramid rule: essential answer first, follow-up prompts
  so users pull detail on demand. Prefer specific ranges or estimates over
  generic answers hedged across scenarios, and when the bot cannot help, say so
  plainly ([[2026-04-17_less-chat-more-answer]]).
- AI can draft — headlines, alternatives, alt text, brainstorming — but it
  drifts from brand voice and does not know your users or context, so always
  edit with the audience in mind ([[2026-05-08_ux-writing-faqs]]).
- Keep voice stable and let tone adapt to the situation (empathetic in errors,
  encouraging in onboarding), and document both in the style guide
  ([[2026-05-08_ux-writing-faqs]]).
- SEO, AEO and GEO rest on the same principles as good UX writing: clear
  structure, plain language, direct answers, genuine authority. Websites become
  sources that AI tools cite rather than destinations; write for people first
  and structure for machines second. Measure task completion rather than traffic
  ([[2026-05-08_ux-writing-faqs]]).
