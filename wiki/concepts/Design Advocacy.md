# Design Advocacy

## Definition

Design advocacy is the work of making design count with people who are not
designers. The sources use the term in two directions that meet in the same
skill. Inward, it is championing user needs and design decisions against
pressure for other goals, and doing it through research, education and
collaboration rather than authority or refusal
([[2017-12-10_bad-design-suggestions]]). Outward, [[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_23-21-advocacy-community]]
defines advocacy as promoting the company and the design team's message
externally, which attracts talent, improves how customers, investors and
vendors perceive the company, and builds the designer's personal brand.

What links the two is the same move: translating design into the terms of the
audience in front of you. [[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_13-11-communicating-design-communication]]
frames it as replacing preference and visual taste with evidence, and
[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_16-14-stakeholder-management-collaboration]]
as talking in terms of the other person's interests rather than design logic.
[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_23-21-advocacy-community]] notes
that advocacy is less critical than the other competencies the book covers,
while arguing that personal brand can still have an outsized effect on a
career, because it accumulates and follows you across roles where
company-specific visibility does not.

## Practice

### Advocating a design decision

- Ground the reasoning in evidence. [[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_13-11-communicating-design-communication]]
  recommends checking whether "I think/believe that" can be replaced with "We
  know that", "Data shows that" or "According to research", and lists the kinds
  of reasons that hold up: data and analytics, research, visual design
  principles, business objectives, technical feasibility, project context, and
  ethical or legal considerations. The chapter argues this sort of reasoning is
  harder to disagree with and, in most cases, leads to better solutions; it
  also challenges your own decisions, builds trust, and removes unnecessary
  feedback cycles.
- Adjust the evidence to the audience: different people respond to different
  types of evidence, and PMs, developers, executives and external parties each
  arrive with different priorities
  ([[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_13-11-communicating-design-communication]]).
- Frame the idea around what the other person already cares about.
  [[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_16-14-stakeholder-management-collaboration]]
  presents this as the most basic and important technique the corporate world
  uses to convince others: engineers value clean code and performance, product
  managers value shipping on time and roadmap alignment, so pitch there rather
  than on design principles alone, connecting the idea to speed of development,
  cost efficiency or business KPIs.

### Advocating against a bad suggestion

[[2017-12-10_bad-design-suggestions]] treats unsolicited design suggestions
from executives and colleagues as an advocacy problem rather than a nuisance:

- Lose the ego and hear the suggestion out. The article argues that a diversity
  of perspectives leads to more effective ideation, and that people with little
  design knowledge may bring expertise or experience you lack.
- Separate the solution from the problem. A poorly proposed solution may
  address a legitimate underlying problem; ask what the person is trying to
  achieve to tell the two apart.
- Acknowledge benefits as well as costs. Even bad ideas have some benefit, and
  naming both sides helps stakeholders understand design complexity.
- Buy time with research rather than an immediate "no": offer to research the
  idea, review usability guidelines and explore alternatives.
- When you do reject, explain the reasoning from UX research and principles.
  The article treats the moment someone is engaged enough to make a suggestion
  as the ideal time to spread UX knowledge through the team.
- With powerful stakeholders, the article recommends an aikido-like approach:
  agree to explore the suggestion, then present alternatives that reach their
  underlying goals without implementing the problematic design, avoiding direct
  rejection where it is politically risky.
- Prevent the problem upstream by seeking stakeholder input at scheduled,
  strategic times, which makes late-stage surprise demands less likely.

### Advocating externally

[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_23-21-advocacy-community]] covers
external channels: public speaking, writing, dedicated design team websites,
public mentorship programmes, and design awards.

- Speaking usually progresses from internal events to small meetups to
  conferences. The chapter calls being scouted rare and dependent on an
  established public presence, and presents submitting to a call for papers as
  the more realistic route. It relays the VP of design at Figma saying that
  publishing articles about their internal design work process brings design
  candidates to their door, and notes that Figma and Intercom position
  themselves as thought leaders by sharing their design work publicly.
- For writing, the chapter's idea-generation techniques include leveraging time
  already spent on a topic, revealing insider information or unique access,
  naming an emerging industry pattern, examining a convention that has fallen
  out of favour, going deep on a small neglected topic, defining your own way
  of doing something, and making internal documentation public. It quotes Linus
  Pauling, "The way to get good ideas is to get lots of ideas", and warns that
  no technique guarantees an article will be popular.
- The stated payoff is luck surface area: each article, talk or open source
  contribution raises the chance that a job offer, consulting gig or book deal
  lands in your domain, illustrated by the author being approached by InVision
  leadership.
- The same chapter argues that designers systematically underrecognise B2B
  opportunities. The author reports analysing 120 companies with open product
  designer positions in the San Francisco Bay Area and finding the majority to
  be B2B, and recommends showcasing more B2B work.

### Advocating for your own work

[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_24-22-getting-promoted]] treats
self-advocacy and visibility as a critical and often-overlooked competency,
distinct from the work itself: visibility determines whether people know about
your achievements and can champion them. It cites Shreyas Doshi's three work
types (producing, organizing, self-promoting) and Ray Dalio's observation that
in most companies people do two jobs, their actual job and the job of managing
others' impressions of it. The visibility-building strategies it lists include
mentorship, documentation, knowledge sharing, interest groups, progress
updates, peer feedback, participation in hiring, and external initiatives.
