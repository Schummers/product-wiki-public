# Innovation

## Definition

The sources use the word at several very different scales, and the page keeps them apart. In the Nielsen Norman Group articles, innovation is a property of a design: a novel interface pattern that departs from the conventions users already know, or a solution that emerges from a user-centred process. [[2016-07-31_design-thinking]] presents innovation as the payoff of design thinking, an ideology holding that a hands-on, user-centric approach to problem solving leads to innovation, and innovation to differentiation and competitive advantage. [[2016-10-30_power-law-learning]] treats innovation as the opposite of [[Design Consistency]]: a new pattern that forces users onto the steep part of a fresh learning curve while the familiar alternative is already at saturation, so it carries a real cost and must earn its place.

In the two studio books, innovation is a property of a venture-building organisation. [[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_01-foreword]] frames it as the core outcome a studio exists to produce: identifying trends and giving people the tools and space to create world-changing technology. [[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_03-startup-studios-in-a-nutshell]] locates the studio's innovation in process rather than in any single product, an "idea generation and validation engine" that professionalises discovery, ideation and traction. [[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_24-studios-and-innovation]] then narrows the claim sharply: studios are not technical innovation leaders, they design within the existing technology frontier and apply proven business models from other industries to new problem areas, because disruptive technology is too costly and slow for a model built on failing fast. Read together, the corpus agrees on one thing across both scales: innovation is expensive, and whether it is worth attempting depends on economics (learning cost for users, runway cost for a studio), not on novelty for its own sake.

A third scale sits between the two. [[2026-03-05_10x-method_02-finding-and-validating-the-idea]] makes innovation a property of a single startup idea, where the idea is itself a three-dimensional object — problem, solution, and go-to-market strategy — rather than a one-liner. In that framing, innovation is a threshold an idea has to clear: every successful startup innovates in at least one of those three dimensions, and excelling in only one can create substantial market value and differentiation. This is the one source in the page that treats innovation as a requirement rather than as a cost to be justified, and it grants innovation a broader address than the other sources do, since a new go-to-market strategy counts as fully as a new solution.

## Practice

### Scales, and one warning

The design sources and the studio books do not describe the same object. NN/g talks about innovation in an interface, where the unit is a UI pattern and the cost is paid by users in repetitions. The studio books talk about innovation in a venture, where the unit is a business and the cost is paid by the studio in time and capital. A founder's manual, [[2026-03-05_10x-method_02-finding-and-validating-the-idea]], talks about innovation in an idea, where the unit is a problem–solution–go-to-market triple and the question is whether the idea is worth pursuing at all. The guidance below is grouped by scale; the design and studio sources share the lesson that innovation is a cost-benefit decision rather than a virtue, while the idea-scale source treats it as a bar to clear.

### Interface scale: when to deviate from a standard

[[2016-10-30_power-law-learning]] is the only source in this page that gives an explicit decision rule, built on the power law of learning: the time to perform a task decreases with the number of repetitions, following a power-law curve, and different designs reach their performance plateau (saturation) after different numbers of repetitions.

- **Consistency is borrowed practice.** By using a standard pattern, a designer gives users one more repetition of an element they have already practised across dozens of other sites, so they start near saturation. This is why consistency is valued despite looking boring to designers; the article presents it as a consequence of how human memory and learning work, not an aesthetic preference (see [[Learnability]], [[Mental Model]]).
- **A novel pattern starts users at the steep end of a new curve.** Design A (familiar) may be learnable quickly; design B (novel) might offer superior performance once learned, but needs more practice to reach its plateau. Users must decide whether to invest that effort before they see any benefit, and occasional users may abandon the new design before it pays off.
- **Three conditions for a justified deviation.** The article states that departing from a standard should only happen if: (1) the new design performs substantially better once learned; (2) users will encounter it frequently enough to reach saturation; or (3) the brand's perceived value justifies the learning investment.
- **Frequency is the deciding variable.** Innovation works better when users interact daily, because practice accumulates quickly, or when a major company imposes an update on a captive audience. The article calls the path to innovation circuitous and costly, and warns that users with few learning opportunities may never reach the optimal-performance plateau.

### Interface scale: innovation as an outcome of process

[[2016-07-31_design-thinking]] approaches innovation from the other side: not as a pattern to be judged, but as what a user-centred process produces.

- **Solve the right problem first.** The article quotes Jakob Nielsen: "a wonderful interface solving the wrong problem will fail." Design thinking is credited with focusing creative energy on the right problem by defining it before jumping to solutions, and with solving real user needs rather than imaginary ones.
- **Explore several solutions.** Among the advantages listed for the process is that it encourages exploring multiple solution avenues, alongside being user-centred, data-driven and leveraging collective expertise. The six phases (empathize, define, ideate, prototype, test, implement) are iterative, and teams often return to earlier phases after prototyping reveals new learning.
- **Innovation requires implementation.** The sixth phase, implement, is described as the most important and the most often forgotten. Design thinking "is not magic": ideas only become innovation through actual design doing (see [[Design Thinking]], [[Design Process]]).

The two NN/g articles do not contradict each other, but they pull in different directions: one says a user-centred process leads to innovation, the other says an innovative result has to clear a learning-cost bar before it should ship. The corpus offers no source that reconciles them explicitly; a reader should take the first as the route to candidate solutions and the second as the filter on which candidates deserve to replace a convention.

### Idea scale: innovate in at least one dimension

[[2026-03-05_10x-method_02-finding-and-validating-the-idea]] argues against the "1% idea, 99% execution" formula and gives the idea itself a structure that can be examined dimension by dimension.

- **Three dimensions, and one is enough.** A robust idea combines the problem (a specific pain point or need), the solution (the product you will build) and the go-to-market strategy (how customers find and use it). Every successful startup innovates in at least one of the three — discovering an unaddressed problem, creating a significantly improved or new solution, or developing a new go-to-market strategy — and excelling in a single one can create substantial market value and differentiation (see [[Business Strategy]]).
- **Three categories of idea, each with its own search.** Category 1 is problem–solution fit found through personal frustration or industry expertise; category 2 is a market or technology shift where new capabilities enable a dramatically better solution to a known problem; category 3 is category creation through a behavioural shift. Each requires a different search strategy and different evidence.
- **Timing carries part of the innovation.** The chapter holds that ideas belong to their time, not to their creators, emerging at the intersection of cultural shifts, new technologies and evolving user behaviours; the best entrepreneurs are not necessarily the first to think of something but the ones who recognise when an idea's moment has arrived and move fast. A well-timed, well-formed idea can go far even in imperfect hands.
- **The innovation claim is validated, not asserted.** The chapter's three-step process — become an expert through 25–30 user interviews, scope the idea in writing across eight dimensions, then draw 3–4 low-fidelity wireframes — is what turns a claimed innovation into a plan (see [[Idea Validation]], [[Ideation]], [[User Interviews]]).

### Venture scale: innovation as the studio's mission

[[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_01-foreword]] (written by Michael Jones of Science Inc.) sets the ambitious version of the claim.

- The studio, a term borrowed from filmmaking for a structure where artists, operators and producers work, is described as "much more potent when applied to innovation."
- The studio's responsibility is to identify trends and provide people the tools and space to create world-changing technology, and its mission is to supply team support, engineers, marketing and a fertile environment so founders can focus on creation rather than operations.
- Speed and agility are the claimed advantage over traditional venture models: the ability to move quickly, learn from failure, and iterate for value, then scale proven ideas. The foreword expects the model to expand beyond software and commerce into other growth areas (see [[Venture Studio]], [[Studio Strategy]]).

### Venture scale: innovation as an engine, not a flash

[[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_03-startup-studios-in-a-nutshell]] shifts the emphasis from the outcome to the machinery.

- **Professionalise discovery, ideation and traction.** The studio recipe is to assemble a core team and entrepreneurs in residence, add shared infrastructure and in-house funding, generate ideas internally or co-found with external teams, build multiple startups in parallel, discard what fails, and spin off what succeeds.
- **Volume and selection.** The model is described as an "idea generation and validation engine" able to produce dozens of startup concepts per year and select only the most viable for full execution. Because resources are available in-house, the team can focus on the product and the customer.
- **Failure as an expected outcome.** Running startups in parallel lets a studio treat failure as normal: when one fails, the team and expertise move to another concept rather than being lost. The chapter also notes that studios motivate people to found companies who otherwise would not, for lack of an idea they believe in or of the means to forgo a salary (see [[Studio Economics]]).

### Venture scale: the limits of studio innovation

[[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_24-studios-and-innovation]] is the corrective, and it disagrees in tone, if not in substance, with the foreword's world-changing framing.

- **Studios operate within the frontier, not at it.** Studios "typically design within the tech frontier and apply existing business models or solutions from other industries to the problem area they are exploring." Creating disruptive technology is costly and time-consuming, which is incompatible with an economic model built on failing fast and preserving runway.
- **Structural, not accidental.** The chapter presents this as a feature aligned with studio economics rather than a shortcoming. Studios have inherent limits on ideation compared with traditional venture capital, which sources ideas globally, because a studio draws only on internal staff and a handful of entrepreneurs-in-residence.
- **Execution beats ideas.** Many successful studio exits generate strong returns without breakthrough technology; the chapter concludes that strong execution may be far more important than truly game-changing ideas, and that "studios tend to be a story of economic ownership levels, not superior ideation." High initial ownership at low cost means exits of only $50M or $100M may still return 50x or 100x.
- **Sector focus follows.** Research cited in the chapter shows studios concentrate on B2B SaaS and ventures serving specialised niche needs, not consumer-facing or bleeding-edge technology (see [[Portfolio Strategy]], [[Business Strategy]]).
- **Where the ceiling is.** As more studios enter similar sectors, generating novel ideas that fit the model gets harder, and the model already needs a very wide initial funnel to yield one viable company; the chapter judges this limit still distant. The binding constraint on the market is the supply of quality entrepreneurs, not studio capacity or ideation.

### Where the sources disagree

- **Foreword versus Kannan and Peterman.** [[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_01-foreword]] casts studios as builders of world-changing technology; [[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_24-studios-and-innovation]] says studios are not innovation leaders in a technical sense and succeed through execution and ownership economics. [[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_03-startup-studios-in-a-nutshell]] sits between them: its innovation is the engine that produces and filters concepts, which is compatible with either reading. The page does not resolve this; the later book is explicitly arguing against the more heroic framing.
- **Does the idea matter, or only the execution?** [[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_24-studios-and-innovation]] concludes that strong execution may be far more important than truly game-changing ideas, and that studios are "a story of economic ownership levels, not superior ideation". [[2026-03-05_10x-method_02-finding-and-validating-the-idea]] argues the opposite way round, that ideas matter more than the "1% idea, 99% execution" wisdom acknowledges — "Execution is the engine, but the idea is the map" — and that a well-timed, well-formed idea can go far even in imperfect hands. The two are speaking from different seats: the studio book is explaining where a portfolio's returns actually come from, the founder's manual is telling one founder what to do before writing code. Neither addresses the other.
- **Process leads to innovation versus innovation must be earned.** At interface scale, [[2016-07-31_design-thinking]] and [[2016-10-30_power-law-learning]] frame innovation as, respectively, a desirable output and a costly deviation. Both are NN/g positions, published three months apart, and neither cites the other.

### A shared thread

Despite the scale difference, the design and studio sources tie innovation to a cost that someone else pays back. At interface scale, the cost is users' repetitions and the payback is a higher performance plateau; at venture scale, the cost is the studio's runway and the payback is ownership on exit. The guidance that holds across both is that innovation is justified by a substantially better result once the cost is sunk, not by novelty, and that the decision has to account for how often the payoff will actually be reached.

The idea-scale source breaks that pattern rather than extending it: [[2026-03-05_10x-method_02-finding-and-validating-the-idea]] does not weigh innovation against a cost but requires it, as the condition for an idea to be worth pursuing at all. What it shares with the others is the refusal to accept novelty on its own terms — the innovation has to be located in a named dimension (problem, solution, or go-to-market) and then tested through interviews, written scoping and wireframes before anything is built.
