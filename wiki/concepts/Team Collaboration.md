# Team Collaboration

## Definition

Team collaboration is the normal working mode of UX and product work, not an
occasional supplement to it. A survey of 86 practitioners found that 82% of UX
professionals collaborate when producing deliverables, making collaboration five
times more common than working alone [[2016-01-03_ux-deliverables-collaboration]].
The people involved are rarely all designers: engineers contribute technical
feasibility, product and business stakeholders contribute requirements and goals,
and other UX professionals contribute a fresh perspective and discipline knowledge
[[2016-01-03_ux-deliverables-collaboration]]. Collaboration in this sense covers
both the act of producing work together (ideation, research, mapping, critique)
and the communication that keeps a distributed set of roles pointed at the same
problem.

The sources consistently locate its value in three places. First, better output:
multiple evaluators find more usability problems than one, divergent input from
different disciplines reduces bias, and showing work to a colleague before a
stakeholder catches mistakes cheaply — the "four eyes principle"
[[2016-01-03_ux-deliverables-collaboration]]. Second, shared understanding:
working through activities together builds a common vocabulary, tangible artifacts
that act as organizational memory, and a trust-based culture
[[2016-09-18_design-thinking-team-building]]; this is the psycholinguistic notion
of *common ground*, the mutual knowledge that lets colleagues use shortcuts
instead of re-explaining everything [[2021-05-09_common-ground]]. Third, buy-in
and ownership: people who took part in producing a decision defend and implement
it, which is why collaboratively created journey maps report higher success at
internal alignment than independently created ones
[[2021-12-19_journey-mapping-impact]] and why teams excluded from the design
process later derail critiques [[2021-05-30_derailed-design-critiques]].

Collaboration is not treated as an unqualified good. Cohesive teams drift into
groupthink, where consensus is prioritized over considering alternatives and
dissent is self-censored [[2023-01-15_groupthink-in-ux]]; unmanaged stakeholder
input becomes a source of conflict and poor decisions
[[2017-12-10_bad-design-suggestions]]; and stakeholders who observe research
without guidance can violate participant ethics and study validity
[[2016-05-29_team-members-user-test]]; and design by committee, where
stakeholders without design expertise contribute unchecked suggestions, spirals
into groupthink and mob mentality and yields compromised experiences of the
"CEO button" or "home page syndrome" kind
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_02-chapter-1-great-designers-are-great-communicators]].
Almost every source therefore describes collaboration as something to be
*structured* — through formats, artifacts, facilitation, and explicit
agreements — rather than something that happens when people are put in a room
together [[2020-02-16_workshops-vs-meetings]]. Patton frames the same principle
at the level of a single conversation rather than a whole format: a story card
is a token that starts a conversation, not a specification that replaces one,
and the collaborative work happens in the talk itself, not in what ends up
written down [[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_11-6-the-real-story-about-stories]].
One source states the dependency
from the other end: a design succeeds only if it solves a problem, is easy for
users, *and* is supported by everyone on the team, three conditions it treats as
equally necessary
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_02-chapter-1-great-designers-are-great-communicators]].

## Practice

### Workshops as the default collaborative format

Several sources draw a hard line between meetings and workshops. Meetings share
information passively across many topics; workshops solve one predefined problem
through hands-on activity and produce decisions and action items in the session
itself [[2020-02-16_workshops-vs-meetings]], [[2019-12-08_5-ux-workshops]]. That
depth is bought with preparation: a workshop brief, clear objectives, an
intentional activity sequence, a detailed agenda, materials, and stakeholder
alignment beforehand, against an agenda and invitations for a meeting
[[2020-02-16_workshops-vs-meetings]]. Simply gathering people creates nothing, and
a wasted workshop costs credibility with senior stakeholders who then decline the
next one [[2020-02-16_workshops-vs-meetings]]; teams are advised to define
explicitly what each word means in their organization.

Five recurring workshop types serve different collaborative goals: discovery
(consensus on plan and direction), empathy (shifting the team from features-first
to users-first), design (rapidly generating solutions), prioritization (consensus
on what matters), and critique (evaluating designs against user needs)
[[2019-12-08_5-ux-workshops]]. Others are documented for journey mapping, design
studios, service blueprinting, and roadmapping
[[2021-09-12_facilitating-ux-workshops-guide]]. Roadmapping workshops are given a
concrete shape: 2–4 hours, held at the theme-creation and prioritization steps,
with pre-work to set goals and gather inputs and post-work to visualize and share
— and a two-workshop variant (1.5h preliminary, 2.5h primary) when roadmapping is
new to the organization [[2021-05-23_roadmapping-workshop]].

A three-part model structures any single activity: **Explain** (describe steps,
roles and context without stating what will be learned, to avoid biasing
participants), **Execute** (step back and observe), **Examine** (present outcomes,
reflect, and connect the output to prior and future work)
[[2018-07-22_ux-workshop-exercises]]. The same source warns against playing
facilitator and participant at once, since a facilitator's contributions carry
disproportionate weight.

### Diverge then converge

The most widely repeated structural pattern is to work alone first, then together.
Individual work in the diverge stage avoids groupthink and lets introverts
contribute equally; the converge stage synthesizes findings into a collective
output [[2024-05-30_diverge-converge]]. Divergent activities (sketching,
brainstorming, brainwriting) are followed by convergent ones (affinity
diagramming, forced ranking) [[2020-02-16_workshops-vs-meetings]],
[[2021-09-12_facilitating-ux-workshops-guide]]. Affinity diagramming applies the
same three beats: generate independently on sticky notes for 5–10 minutes, group
collaboratively, then prioritize by dot voting and assign action items and point
persons — and the discussion while building the diagram matters more than the
final artifact [[2024-04-26_affinity-diagram]].

Survey evidence supports the pattern. Teams rating their ideation *very effective*
collaborate more than they ideate individually, ideate after research but before
prototyping (71% vs. 48% of merely effective teams), and use written techniques
such as brainwriting significantly more, which reduces groupthink and hierarchy
bias; 95% of ineffective teams rely on unstructured discussion
[[2017-10-29_ideation-in-practice]]. Notably, very effective teams spend *less*
time on ideation (77% under three hours) — structure, not duration, is what
correlates with effectiveness.

Group note-taking follows the same rule and states it as a caution: avoid shared
collaborative documents *during* research sessions; have each observer take
separate notes and combine afterwards, precisely to prevent groupthink and
distraction [[2017-04-30_group-notetaking]].

### Shared artifacts as the medium of alignment

Sources repeatedly treat artifacts as the thing that carries alignment forward
rather than as decoration. Tangible outputs — empathy maps, journey maps,
wireframes, storyboards, mockups — transcend language and culture, document team
history, and reduce ambiguity [[2016-09-18_design-thinking-team-building]].
Personas, journey maps, service blueprints, and roadmaps have a dual function:
they *establish* common ground and then *record* it, sparing future re-explanation
[[2021-05-09_common-ground]].

- **Empathy maps** — the mapping process itself, not just the artifact, is where
  the value sits; involving the team builds shared understanding and strips out
  bias-based assumptions, and a sparse map exposes what the team does not yet know
  [[2018-01-14_empathy-mapping]].
- **Journey maps** — 64% of practitioners create them collaboratively
  [[2021-12-19_journey-mapping-impact]], [[2023-01-08_journey-mapping-how]], with
  product (70%), marketing (46%) and customer service (42%) involved
  [[2023-01-08_journey-mapping-how]]. Involving customers and cross-functional
  teams ranked as the top two of eight success factors; visual design ranked last
  [[2021-12-19_journey-mapping-impact]]. 37% of reported success factors concern
  creating maps as a team and sharing broadly, and 32% of respondents named shared
  goals and vision as the primary benefit
  [[2016-10-16_journey-mapping-ux-practitioners]].
- **Service blueprints** — most often owned by design teams, yet fewer than half
  of organizations involve senior leadership, support, marketing, or sales; the
  most frequently reported benefit is a common language across departments, often
  becoming a single source of truth [[2019-10-20_service-blueprinting-practice]].
- **Roadmaps** — used to create a shared vision of *problems to solve* rather than
  execution plans [[2020-07-05_ux-roadmaps]]. Type follows collaborative purpose:
  product roadmaps (cross-department, shared vision), field roadmaps (UX-wide,
  educating stakeholders on the design process), specialty roadmaps (one
  discipline, workload balancing) [[2020-11-01_roadmap-types]]. A solo-created
  roadmap will likely go unused [[2021-05-23_roadmapping-workshop]].
- **Mood boards** — collaborative collages that align teams on emotional and
  visual direction before detailed design, reducing later miscommunication
  [[2023-02-26_mood-boards]].
- **Vision statements** — built through research sharing, dot-coding (agree,
  disagree, surprising, essential), affinity diagramming and dot voting so the
  whole team believes in the result; the vision then has to be actively
  communicated through documents, videos, boards or prototypes to keep momentum
  [[2022-02-06_ux-vision-statements]].
- **Post-its** — physical evidence of group effort in different hands and colours;
  identical size gives every idea visual equality and lets quiet members
  contribute without tool proficiency; limited space forces concision; organizing
  them forces the group to act rather than talk in circles. The same source warns
  they are not design in themselves and work badly for remote teams and colour-blind
  participants [[2018-03-25_post-it-in-ux]].

Artifacts are also worth testing on their own audience. The **sketch test** has a
colleague read a deliverable and sketch or summarize it back; the discrepancies
show what the audience misperceived or misunderstood, and the participant's
annotations matter more than their verbal suggestions [[2017-01-22_sketch-test]].

### Getting non-designers to participate

Lowering the cost of entry is a theme in itself. Collaborative sketching brings
buy-in, idea diversity, shared ownership and speed, but non-designers resist it
claiming they cannot draw; the counter is a four-variable formula — fat markers
(limit detail), tiny spaces such as index cards or 8-up sheets (shrink the space,
shrink the anxiety), time limits (the most important variable, preventing
overthinking and self-censoring), and deliberately ugly examples (a polished
example signals that sketching belongs to creative roles)
[[2019-12-22_how-to-get-stakeholders-to-sketch]].

Lightweight activities fold collaboration into existing Agile ceremonies:
**6-up 1-up** (five minutes of six individual sketches, group discussion of themes,
five minutes refining one, group hybrid selection), **whiteboard sessions**
(informal real-time alignment between design, development and product for
near-term sprints), and the **telephone activity** (a sketch passed around, each
person adding for 1–2 minutes), which builds comfort with building on others' work
[[2018-09-23_collaborative-agile-activities]]. Involvement should scale inversely
to urgency: one-on-one work with a developer for the current sprint, larger group
brainstorms during lulls for features further down the backlog. Introduce these
incrementally by carving time out of ceremonies already on the calendar.

For stakeholders more broadly: evangelize research by framing it as risk
reduction and rework saved, create standing space for interaction (UX office
hours, design clinics, open chat channels), align research goals with stakeholder
goals and give credit for wins, start with small pilots that produce quick wins,
make attendance logistically easy with personal invitations, and produce
accessible deliverables (annotated prototypes, bullet lists, screenshots,
stories) rather than dense academic reports [[2017-02-26_collaborating-stakeholders]].
Involving developers, product owners and stakeholders in usability sessions and
debriefs shifts decisions from opinion to data [[2016-08-28_ux-success-agile]], and
full-team participation in discovery research produces firsthand empathy and
consensus [[2024-09-27_7-tips-discovery]].

Participation has guardrails. Observers should be reminded that testing is about
receiving feedback, not giving it, and that they stay silent until the end; in-room
observers should be capped at three so the participant does not feel outnumbered,
with overflow broadcast to another room; managers should not observe their direct
reports; and when a user struggles the team must attribute it to the interface,
not the person [[2016-05-29_team-members-user-test]]. Rotating stakeholders through
sessions, with 4–10 observers and everyone seeing at least two sessions, prevents
judgments formed from a single observation [[2017-04-30_group-notetaking]].

### Facilitation, group dynamics and dissent

The risk that collaboration degrades into conformity is treated directly.
Groupthink symptoms include collective rationalization of warnings, an illusion of
unanimity produced by silence, self-censorship, and direct pressure on dissenters
[[2023-01-15_groupthink-in-ux]]. Countermeasures: state group norms that value all
ideas and explicitly invite disagreement, ideate solo before sharing, pair solo
and group activities in a diverge-converge sequence, use dot voting so the
facilitator does not decide, recruit diverse focus-group participants and warm
them up, and test design trends with actual users instead of assuming a
competitor's adoption is evidence. Virtual settings make it worse, so rotate
meeting hosts, use anonymous follow-ups, and give each channel an explicit purpose
[[2023-01-15_groupthink-in-ux]]. Ground rules for the diverge stage — enforced
quiet time, banning "no" and "but", anonymity for sensitive topics — serve the same
end [[2024-05-30_diverge-converge]].

Democratic mechanics recur: sticky notes and anonymous idea cards keep hierarchy
out of evaluation, and dot voting produces a visible heat map that makes consensus
transparent [[2016-09-18_design-thinking-team-building]]. Voting tools for remote
workshops should be democratic in the same sense — not weighted by role or
seniority [[2020-03-08_tools-remote-ux-workshops]].

Standard facilitation challenges are encouraging full participation, managing
dominating participants, and facilitating with stakeholders in the room;
intentional silence, parking lots and explicit guidance are the named techniques
[[2021-09-12_facilitating-ux-workshops-guide]]. A **parking lot** is a visible,
shared artifact that defers off-topic items without dismissing them. Five
practices keep it from becoming a graveyard: introduce its purpose and ground
rules up front, cluster items by affinity as they arrive, apply "W.W.W." (who does
what, when) to each item, include every item in the written meeting notes for
public accountability, and actually follow up — parking an idea and abandoning it
insults the contributor and damages team dynamics [[2019-09-08_parking-lots]].

The facilitator's own posture gets a treatment of its own. A designer in a
feedback meeting is there to facilitate, not to defend the work or pitch it like
a salesperson: guide the conversation toward a good solution, represent
stakeholders' needs back to peers, and check the ego
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_06-chapter-5-get-in-the-right-mindset]].
Three mental shifts support that role — understand it as guide and advocate for
the user experience, give up ultimate control in exchange for influence (since
accepting that you do not have the final say is what removes the
defensiveness), and prepare stakeholders to represent the work by giving them
the vocabulary and confidence to explain the thinking in the meetings you are
not in. Vocabulary is part of the mechanism: lead with "yes" to keep the
conversation open and reinforce a team mentality, avoiding "yes, but", which
negates the affirmation; drop "you're wrong", "from a design perspective" and
"like / don't like" in favour of language about effectiveness and function; and
adopt the words stakeholders already use rather than jargon that separates
expertise levels
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_06-chapter-5-get-in-the-right-mindset]].

Management support is a precondition rather than a nicety: 15% of practitioners
name lack of managerial backing as the primary barrier to ideation, because
without top-level clout no one can pull people off other teams or allocate the
time [[2017-10-29_ideation-in-practice]].

### Critique and feedback

A design critique is a group conversation analyzing whether a design meets its
objectives — distinct from a design review, which evaluates against heuristics and
approves work to move on [[2016-10-23_design-critiques]]. Its three conditions are
a clear scope, agreed design objectives, and conversation rather than
command-and-control feedback. A designated facilitator sets scope, chooses
participants, reformulates feedback back to design goals, documents the
discussion, and follows up with action items. Round-robin gives everyone a turn;
quota-based feedback asks for a set number of positive and negative comments.
Presenters restate objectives, tell the story of the work, and avoid
over-explaining. Start small and soon, with current projects and immediate
teammates, rather than waiting for the next project. Common pitfalls: no agreed
personas or objectives, over-long sessions, taking feedback personally, solving
problems during the critique, and focusing only on negatives.

Critiques derail in three recognizable ways: hypothetical scenarios presented as
evidence (devil's advocate pseudoevidence, often from developers and PMs trying to
cover every case), vague and subjective feedback, and drift into premature
implementation detail [[2021-05-30_derailed-design-critiques]]. The tactics are to
capture hypothetical scenarios on sticky notes and rate them by likelihood, target
persona and business objective so design focuses on the top 1–3; publish feedback
guidelines stating what kind of feedback is wanted; log questions and unverified
assumptions on a persistent knowledge board (unlike a temporary parking lot); and
sort feedback afterwards into "To Do", "To Persuade" and "To Clarify". The root
cause named by that source is exclusion: team members who feel shut out of the
design process are the ones who derail its critiques.

Unsolicited suggestions from colleagues and executives get a parallel treatment:
drop the ego and accept that non-designers may see something real, hear the
suggestion out, separate the underlying problem from the proposed solution,
acknowledge both the benefit and the cost of the idea, buy time by offering to
research it and explore alternatives, explain rejections in terms of UX research
and principles so refusal becomes education, and use an aikido move with powerful
stakeholders — agree to explore, then present alternatives that reach their goal.
The preventive measure is scheduling stakeholder input at strategic moments so it
arrives structured rather than as a late-stage surprise
[[2017-12-10_bad-design-suggestions]].

Artiom Dashinsky treats the exchange itself as a skill with two sides, and starts
from the observation that design may be the role that has to deal with the most
feedback inside the organization
[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_14-12-feedback-communication]]. On giving feedback, the structure is to
describe the behaviour with concrete examples, articulate its impact, and then
either encourage continuation (positive) or position a solution as a question
(negative), opening with a request such as "Can we talk about X?" rather than
launching in. On design feedback specifically, ask clarifying questions like
"Could you explain the reasoning behind…?" before proposing anything, so a
solution is not suggested before the designer's intent is understood
[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_14-12-feedback-communication]]. He cites a study featured in Harvard
Business Review reporting that no one likes to give negative feedback but
everyone wants to hear it
[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_14-12-feedback-communication]].

On receiving it, he adds the step the NN/g sources leave implicit: prioritisation
before response. Understand the root cause, evaluate relevancy against the
project's context (readiness level, scope, original request), then attach a
severity — by asking "How strongly do you feel about it?", by using severity
phrases, or by adopting a tagging system such as HubSpot's FlashTags (#fyi,
#suggestion, #recommendation, #plea) — and respond in one of four categories:
yes, yes but later, requires research, or no. Without such a system, he argues,
designers are simply overwhelmed
[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_14-12-feedback-communication]]. Feedback is also a growth signal: document
what you thought was right to ship against what was eventually shipped, and the
delta between the two indicates where to improve; categorising feedback and
scoring it by frequency and severity reveals patterns more reliably than
individual incidents
[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_14-12-feedback-communication]].

He also treats the recurring critique meeting as one of the highest-impact growth
activities available, because it builds presentation, feedback, confidence and
oral communication at once — and gives solo designers a fallback: find a sparring
partner or set up a mutual weekly critique with another designer, following a
simplified structure of explaining why you designed it, stating what feedback you
want, then walking through the solution
[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_15-13-written-and-oral-communication-communication]].

### Roles, ownership and shared accountability

Ambiguity about who does what is documented as a concrete collaboration failure.
A survey of 372 PM and UX professionals found statistically significant
disagreement across nearly every responsibility area: 73% of UXers versus 19% of
PMs claimed discoveries for UX, only 46% of PMs considered user testing a UX
activity, 75% of UXers assigned information architecture to UX while 50% of PMs
assigned it to development, and the two groups disagreed sharply about who
explains designs and secures stakeholder buy-in
[[2021-05-02_pm-ux-different-views-of-responsibilities]]. The prescription is
explicit, organization-level role definition, since the alternative is duplicated
work, confusion and dissatisfaction.

Frameworks proposed for that alignment:

- **RACI** — Responsible (does the task), Accountable (final review, exactly one
  per task), Consulted (provides input), Informed (kept aware). Assignments should
  follow skills, relationships, product history and individual ambitions rather
  than job titles, and vary by phase, being most useful in strategic and discovery
  phases where roles are least clear. Visualizing roles this way pushes teams
  toward design-as-process collaboration and away from design-as-service handoffs;
  it can be dropped once involvement becomes obvious
  [[2022-06-05_ux-roles-responsibilities]].
- **Product triad** (design, product, engineering, rooted in IDEO's
  Desirable/Viable/Feasible) — its purpose is explicitly *not* to give each member
  one leg of DVF but to make all three a joint responsibility; one member may lead
  a decision while ownership stays collective. The designer becomes the authority
  on user needs, exposes conflicts between stakeholder assumptions and business
  goals early, and involves the engineering lead directly in the design process so
  the vision is achievable [[2025-07-18_the-product-triad-designs-role]]. Patton
  describes an earlier version of the same structure as a two-to-four-person
  "cross-functional discovery team," "dinner-conversation-sized," led by a
  product owner who orchestrates the work rather than monopolizing it
  [[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_17-12-rock-breakers]].
- **Coownership of design and development** — designers optimize usability,
  developers identify constraints, both own the product outcome. Root causes of
  the classic conflict are named as historical trauma from toxic environments,
  power struggles over final say, low psychological safety, and processes that
  exclude developers early. Practices: build one-to-one relationships outside
  meetings; communicate to understand rather than to convince, repeating back what
  you heard; agree on shared vocabulary early, since the two disciplines use
  different words for the same thing; and acknowledge invisible work. Hearing
  feasibility concerns early lets designers iterate faster, and developers who see
  designers committed to outcomes accommodate more
  [[2025-09-26_developer-designer-relationship]].
- **Team charters** — shared agreements on purpose, roles, goals and values, plus
  how decisions get made (e.g. "disagreements resolved by revisiting user goals"),
  communication protocols and feedback norms; they give the team a reference point
  when process breaks down [[2025-04-18_design-guidance]].
- **Discovery norms** — clarify task ownership and agree collaboratively on
  meeting frequency, research participation and documentation before starting;
  this prevents confusion and boosts morale [[2024-09-27_7-tips-discovery]].
- **What each role values** — a complementary angle: rather than assigning
  responsibilities, learn what the other roles care about, because each needs a
  different approach. Executives care about vision and reporting, developers
  about quality and feasibility, product owners about innovation and
  simplicity, project managers about schedule and scope, and marketers about
  their own concerns; stakeholders themselves sort into team influencers,
  executive influencers and external influencers
  [[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_03-chapter-2-stakeholders-are-people-too]].

Ownership shows up in artifacts too. Most journey maps omit Zone C — opportunities,
ownership and metrics — which is the section that turns a narrative into an action
plan: without ownership there is no accountability, without metrics no measurable
improvement [[2016-10-16_journey-mapping-ux-practitioners]]. Roadmap themes are
built to carry the same information: beneficiary and need, business outcome, and
who does the work with what kind of effort [[2020-07-05_ux-roadmaps]].

### Story conversations: Patton's collaboration model

Jeff Patton frames all collaboration in software product work as a matter of
conversation rather than document handoff, an argument several forewords to
*User Story Mapping* extend into their own domains. Martin Fowler argues that
programmers must collaborate on stories from the start, not receive them as
one-way requirements, because they are a vital source of ideas about what
software can do [[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_01-foreword-by-martin-fowler]].
Alan Cooper argues that design and development are distinct disciplines that
"lack a common tongue" without a bridging tool such as story mapping, and that
building software one feature at a time, a valid development strategy, produces
a "Frankenstein monster" when used as a design method
[[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_02-foreword-by-alan-cooper]]. Marty Cagan
contrasts strong product teams, where product, design and engineering work side
by side with mutual influence and engineers review discovery prototypes daily,
against weak teams that work in silos and communicate through formal requests
[[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_03-foreword-by-marty-cagan]].

Patton traces the practice to Kent Beck's original insight: requirements
documents fail because different readers imagine different solutions, so teams
should talk together to arrive at shared understanding instead of writing a
precise spec [[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_11-6-the-real-story-about-stories]].
He formalizes this as Ron Jeffries's "3 Cs": Card (a minimal written prompt),
Conversation (where shared understanding actually happens), Confirmation
(agreed acceptance criteria). The Connextra "as a... I want... so that..."
template, which came out of Connextra, is a conversation starter, not a
specification; teams that try to write perfect stories instead of having rich
conversations become "template zombies," a term Patton takes from Tom DeMarco
et al.'s *Adrenaline Junkies and Template Zombies*
[[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_12-7-telling-better-stories]]. His own checklist
for running these conversations covers who, what, why, context, failure modes,
assumptions and better solutions, and he states a rule of etiquette for them:
"be respectful of the expertise of others in the conversation. Don't tell a
highly trained technical person how to do her work"
[[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_12-7-telling-better-stories]].

Patton also argues that a story card is a token, not a container: like a
library catalogue card, it identifies what is being discussed but holds none of
the real information, which lives in conversation notes, on walls, in
wireframes. Different roles — product manager, business analyst, tester, UX
designer, project manager — each need a legitimate but different conversation
about the same story, so teams must deliberately design multiple conversation
channels: physical and face-to-face for building shared understanding, digital
for tracking progress once decisions are made
[[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_13-8-its-not-all-on-the-card]]. He credits
Alistair Cockburn with the term *information radiator* for information posted
visibly on a wall, as against information kept only in tools, which goes stale
"like frost in the back of a freezer"; and cites Cardboard, co-created by David
Hussman, as an example of a tool that lets remote participants build the same
map concurrently with people mapping on a physical wall
[[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_13-8-its-not-all-on-the-card]].

On team composition, Patton argues directly against the Agile assumption that a
single product owner can write all stories and lead all conversations: this is
a bottleneck, and no one person holds the business, UX and engineering
expertise needed to find a valuable-usable-feasible solution. He advocates a
small, cross-functional discovery team, a "triad" of two to four people,
"dinner-conversation-sized," led by a product owner who orchestrates rather
than monopolizes [[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_17-12-rock-breakers]]. Inside
story workshops specifically, he names the "three amigos" (a tester catching
edge cases, a member of the core discovery team, and a user experience designer
or business analyst, with a developer from the building team present too); the
name is shared with a 1986 film, but he says he does not know who first applied
it to Agile
[[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_17-12-rock-breakers]]. He is explicit that this
is not design by committee: "design by community is not design by
committee… design is never democratic" — the product owner keeps the team
pointed at business vision and makes the final call after incorporating diverse
expertise [[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_17-12-rock-breakers]].

Patton also names and rejects a specific collaboration failure mode: the
**client-vendor anti-pattern**, where conversations degrade into a client role
stating requirements and a vendor role estimating delivery, a breakdown he
attributes to clients being unable to predict solutions and vendors lacking
context. He proposes reframing the relationship as doctor-patient (an expert
who listens deeply) or, attributing the image to a conversation with David
Hussman, producer-artist: the business analyst or product owner acts like a
producer helping artists succeed, sometimes saying no, rather than an
order-taker executing stated requirements verbatim
[[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_17-12-rock-breakers]]. In story workshops that
finalize stories before building, three to five people, a product-minded
person, one or two developers, a tester, dig into who the user is, how she uses
the feature, what it looks like, how it behaves, and roughly how to build it,
concluding once the group agrees on acceptance criteria
[[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_21-16-refine-define-and-build]]. Patton frames
the underlying shift here as one from **intermediaries to facilitators**: away
from business analysts handing down specifications, toward people who bridge
business, users and delivery through collaborative workshops rather than
lengthy written narratives
[[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_21-16-refine-define-and-build]].

This sits alongside, rather than against, the corpus's existing treatment of
workshops as the default collaborative format: Patton's contribution is to
locate the collaborative act specifically in conversation design, who talks to
whom, about what, through which medium, rather than in workshop logistics
alone.

### Where product ends and UX begins

*Product Management for UX People* devotes a chapter to the same boundary the
survey above measures, and refuses to draw it once and for all.
[[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_12-chapter-9-healthy-collaborative-tension-on-the-product-ux]]
starts from the premise that collaboration and clarity are not opposites: teams
should work shoulder to shoulder on diagrams, interviews and design exploration
while still establishing clear ownership, the product manager deciding readiness
to ship, the UX strategist owning the navigation taxonomy. Lines exist to clarify
who makes which decision, not to limit collaboration; without them the real
overlap between the disciplines (both research, both model, both specify)
produces conflicts, duplication, wasted cycles and tug-of-war loops that end only
when someone higher up forces a resolution. Adam Connor, VP Design at Rocket
Mortgage, supplies the framing Crumlish builds on: the relationship is symbiotic,
design and research answering "what are the audience's needs and possibilities?",
product management answering "which opportunities make sense to focus on given
business priorities and capacity?", engineering answering "how do we build stably
and scalably?", each discipline an active participant in the others'
conversations rather than a constraint on them. "It isn't an assembly line of one
passing decisions on to the next."

The chapter then sorts real relationships into three states, and is explicit that
the healthy one is the exception. The good: clearly defined roles, well
understood ownership, negotiated final say, with the standing challenge of
sustaining that culture as the team scales. The bad, which it calls the majority
state: well-meaning teams that have simply not done the work of understanding
each other's discipline, miscommunicating and springing deliverables on one
another. The ugly: toxic feature factories where a product title functions as
project manager or overseer in a command-and-control environment, leaving no room
to reframe a question or appeal to vision. Each state gets its own move. In the
bad, the repair is working agreements: rough consensus on ground rules, treated
as testable hypotheses and iterated through retrospectives, which requires
individual practitioners to name uncomfortable truths and leadership to commit to
facilitating the conversation at every level. In the ugly, the moves are survival
ones: pick your battles rather than declaring war on every design violation, get
data that has not been cherry-picked to an agenda, define goals and metrics, and
track outcomes and demand accountability when decisions fail, buying integrity
and breathing room until a better environment turns up. Reporting structure
matters less than the relationship, whether UX reports to product, to a design
director or as a peer under a head of product. Hybrid roles get the sharpest
warning: the "frankenjob" of a PM who still designs or a UX person who owns
product decisions is a golden opportunity to align the disciplines from the
outset and is also, because of constant context-shifting, doubled workload and
the impossibility of an honest disagreement with a peer who is also you,
incredibly difficult, so bring in a peer as soon as the team scales. Kristen Ramirez, senior UX designer at Procore
Technologies, adds the skill that lets a designer work across the boundary:
systems thinking, understanding business processes, technology constraints and
how objects relate across the ecosystem, and deliberately picking up the
languages of the other disciplines.

Whether the tension turns into a turf war is treated as an organizational design
question rather than a property of the disciplines.
[[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_01-frequently-asked-questions]]
attributes healthy partnership to clear role definitions and to leadership
articulating responsibilities out loud, and the same book's own worked example
shows the failure mode:
[[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_03-introduction]] describes Yahoo,
where UED reported into the product org and product and engineering were "locked
in an endless battle for ascendancy," and poses without yet answering whether
product and UX can instead be "superfriends." Matt LeMay's foreword,
[[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_02-foreword]], keeps the two halves
in tension: treating the whole team with respect is a value UX people carry into
product, and it has to coexist with the deadlines, estimates and high-pressure
stakeholders that force hard trade-offs.
[[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_14-chapter-11-chief-information-architect]]
turns the same user-centred instinct inward, arguing a product leader should
treat their own team as a product: understand colleagues' needs, craft
deliverables and communications that solve their problems, overcommunicate, and
build trust. It also names customer support, customer success and community
managers as invaluable sources of signal about what customers want, wasted when
those teams are siloed away.

### Meeting rhythm, follow-up and decision speed

Collaboration also depends on what happens once everyone leaves the room. The
most productive conversation is often the hallway one after the formal meeting,
where people voice unpopular opinions or offer support they would not give in
the group; one-on-ones with key stakeholders afterwards gather extra insight and
build coalition support across departments
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_10-chapter-9-follow-up-afterward]].
A written follow-up to the whole team within the hour, or at worst within the
day, creates the record, signals urgency and respect, and keeps settled
decisions from reopening; it recaps decisions and assigns specific tasks with
names and dates, including to non-designers, which distributes the work and
makes ownership visible. Not every comment deserves to be documented or
actioned: filter by whether the feedback aligns with project objectives and
whether the speaker is influential or likely to raise it again. And when a
meeting ends without resolution, the designer should make the call explicitly
and communicate it, since a stated decision draws out real opinions where
silence leaves the process stagnant
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_10-chapter-9-follow-up-afterward]].

The reciprocal obligations, written for executives and non-designers, come as
four "izes": **Realize** the designer's expertise (they solve problems
strategically, and the iterative work behind a recommendation is invisible from
outside), **Prioritize** their needs (business requirements, timelines,
technical access, stakeholder contact, budget for usability research),
**Authorize** decisions, and **Recognize** their humanity
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_12-chapter-11-how-executives-can-help-designers]].
Decision speed is singled out: projects move at the speed of decision-making
rather than the speed of talent, daily design reviews beat weekly meetings,
changing decisions after the fact and committee decision-making kill momentum,
and empowering designers to decide — with accountability — is what restores it.
A project checklist agreed at the start (vision, goals, metrics, user
understanding, workflow cadence, technical requirements, named decision-makers,
offered as a flexible guide rather than a fixed list) is that source's alignment
device, and its structural argument is that design leadership has to sit at the
executive table for the rest to hold
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_12-chapter-11-how-executives-can-help-designers]].

Dashinsky puts the same discipline before the meeting rather than after it: as a
participant, prepare by defining a clear goal; as an organizer, do not hold the
meeting at all unless it serves one of what he reports as Dropbox's "three Ds" —
an important decision, a debate or a discussion — and otherwise establish clear
ownership, share the agenda in advance and end with assigned action items. He
frames collaboration itself as a skill that improves with deliberate practice
rather than a personality trait
[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_16-14-stakeholder-management-collaboration]].

### Improving the process the team works in

A collaboration process is itself something a designer can own. Dashinsky argues
that knowing how to identify the problems in documentation, design systems,
processes and tools is a more valuable skill than executing well inside them, and
that whoever initiates and defines a process has more leverage than whoever
follows it [[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_12-10-tools-and-processes-craft]]. His four-step method treats the
process as a product to be researched: check whether it is already documented and
volunteer to create or update it if not; map it by listing the steps, the
collaborators and the tools used; find the inefficiencies through your own
observation and through team feedback; then solve them with tooling,
documentation, a design system or a policy change
[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_12-10-tools-and-processes-craft]].

The feedback-collection step is where the practice touches collaboration
directly: ask colleagues specific questions about their frustrations, tool pain
points, repetitive tasks and bottlenecks, and what they would change with a magic
wand, then prioritise what comes back on an impact/effort basis. When pitching
the work, frame it as benefiting the company — onboarding efficiency,
standardisation, a reference point for future changes, improved collaboration —
rather than as a personal preference
[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_12-10-tools-and-processes-craft]].

### Remote and distributed collaboration

Nielsen Norman Group's own 20-year experience as a fully distributed team supplies
the base case: pair webcam video with screen sharing and digital ideation tools
(Zoom, GoToMeeting, Google Docs, Miro, Mural); research need not be remote just
because the team is, since distributed members can travel to field sites, giving
broader geographic coverage than a co-located team; new talent needs assigned
mentors beyond their manager, shadowing on real projects, and periodic in-person
gatherings; and performance is judged on output and impact, not visible presence
[[2019-07-07_remote-ux-work-nng-case-study]]. That source states an **equal
communication rule** for hybrid teams: if any member is remote, everyone joins
remotely, including co-located people, so remote participants are not talked over.

Interviews with 17 remote workers and a survey of 306 practitioners found all three
major remote design challenges to be communication problems
[[2021-02-07_remote-design-challenges]]: slow feedback (fixed by commenting tools
such as Figma, InVision or Google Drive plus recurring feedback slots, and by
insisting on a *single* feedback channel rather than scattering it across PDF,
Figma and Slack), the loss of hallway conversation (fixed by recurring hour-long
collaborative working meetings and 5–10 minutes of social time at the start of
meetings), and unclear direction (fixed by one single source of truth for
requirements and designs, and by handing off over video rather than email alone,
which prevents misinterpretation of interactions and expectations).

Tool guidance converges on familiarity over capability. Favour low-complexity
tools the team already uses over specialized unfamiliar ones, keep the number of
tools small, don't make participants switch between them, and don't let the
technology become the subject of the session — "chances are the best tool is one
you've already been using"
[[2020-03-08_tools-remote-ux-workshops]], [[2020-03-29_remote-ux]]. Match features
to workshop type: generative work needs drag-and-drop whiteboarding that handles
large volumes of flat items, evaluative work needs democratic voting displayed as
matrices or lists, mapping work needs alignment that survives new objects
[[2020-03-08_tools-remote-ux-workshops]]. Synchronous sessions need calendar time,
videoconferencing and a real-time collaborative canvas; asynchronous ones need
firm deadlines to counter procrastination and version history to track
contributions. Mixing both shortens meetings: pre-structured shared documents
collect asynchronous input, then the group meets to discuss
[[2020-03-29_remote-ux]]. Remote facilitation adds camera-on for rapport,
icebreakers, explicit ground rules, homework to practise the tool, and dry runs
before the real session [[2020-03-29_remote-ux]].

For journey mapping specifically, distributed teams face amplified versions of the
usual problems (weak buy-in, scepticism about outcomes, difficulty iterating), and
tools are graded in three levels: Level 1 visualization only (Canvanizer,
OmniGraffle, Gliffy, Lucidchart) for teams of one; Level 2 real-time collaboration
(Mural, Miro, Smaply, UXPressia) for distributed teams, mirroring the in-person
workshop; Level 3 data integration (Touchpoint Dashboard, IBM Journey Designer,
CFN Insight) for enterprise CX programmes [[2017-02-05_remote-customer-journey-mapping]].
Mood boards can likewise be built asynchronously in FigJam, Miro or Mural
[[2023-02-26_mood-boards]].

**A disagreement worth noting.** Several sources treat tool selection as
consequential to collaborative outcomes
[[2017-02-05_remote-customer-journey-mapping]], [[2020-03-08_tools-remote-ux-workshops]],
[[2021-02-07_remote-design-challenges]], while the journey-mapping survey found
success ratings essentially identical whether teams used digital tools (56%) or
sticky notes (44%) [[2021-12-19_journey-mapping-impact]] — a finding echoed by the
34% physical / 30% digital split reported elsewhere with no success gap
[[2023-01-08_journey-mapping-how]]. A further caution comes from outside the team
context: research with cloud-storage users found people do not understand
collaboration features (permissions, access rights, file location) and invent
their own policies — divide and conquer, centralized approval, colour coding, time
sharing — coordinating through email and meetings instead of trusting the system
[[2019-11-24_cloud-storage]].

### Communication and storytelling as collaboration skills

One source makes the strongest version of the claim: communication about the
work is not separate from design, it *is* the job, and what separates a good
designer from a great one is the capacity to articulate a solution convincingly
rather than raw problem-solving ability
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_02-chapter-1-great-designers-are-great-communicators]].
Because design is subjective and every design has legitimate alternatives, work
cannot speak for itself; being articulate imparts intelligence, demonstrates
intentionality, expresses confidence and shows respect, and it requires
deliberately documenting the thought process instead of designing on intuition
alone. Three questions anchor each decision for that conversation: what problem
does it solve, how does it affect the user, and why is it better than the
alternative
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_02-chapter-1-great-designers-are-great-communicators]].

The same book puts the groundwork one level below the meeting, in
relationships: better communication with stakeholders starts with earning trust
and rapport, and the quality of the relationship matters more than the words
spoken in the room
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_03-chapter-2-stakeholders-are-people-too]].
Stakeholders' reactions are often shaped by things outside the project — a
hospital visit, personal stress, a budget cut — so feedback deserves a generous
reading; shared experiences (lunch, a conference, a common interest) make people
more receptive to design expertise; and asking open-ended questions about their
work, priorities and lives, the way a UX practitioner would with a user, reveals
what actually drives their reactions. Small, unglamorous practices carry weight
here: being authentic, checking in regularly, doing things for people, sending a
handwritten note
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_03-chapter-2-stakeholders-are-people-too]].

Because collaboration runs on communication, several sources treat narrative craft
as a team skill. Stories give teams shared vocabulary and common goals and are
more engaging than abstract description [[2019-04-28_persuasive-storytelling]];
six rules are offered: adapt vocabulary to the audience's industry language, appeal
to what they actually care about (budget, timeline, satisfaction), back points with
real research data and quotes, cover the whole experience before, during and after
use, pair the story with an artifact people can point back to, and follow up with a
written summary that becomes the reference for later discussions. A complementary
pair of tools: the **story triangle** (storytelling is an exchange, not a
broadcast — the audience infers from its own experience, so invite feedback) and the
**story mountain** (exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, resolution),
with detail calibrated so the audience is neither under-engaged nor boxed in
[[2022-05-15_two-tips-better-ux-storytelling]].

Visual communication does similar work: using imagery rather than words gives
everyone a shared baseline and lowers the risk of misalignment on abstract
concepts [[2016-09-18_design-thinking-team-building]]. Building common ground
organizationally means evangelizing UX concepts, standardizing vocabulary,
creating visual artifacts, doing one-to-one clarification, and learning about
coworkers; synchronous communication grounds more easily than asynchronous, and
shared artifacts markedly improve efficiency [[2021-05-09_common-ground]]. Design
guidance contributes vocabulary of its own: usability heuristics give teams a
shared language for discussing problems, and design principles let teams resolve
debates by reference to agreed values [[2025-04-18_design-guidance]]. Two
career-facing sources extend the same skill outward — research portfolios must
describe context, the team, and one's own responsibilities in plain language
without jargon [[2019-03-10_ux-researcher-portfolio]], and candidates who answer
interview questions with structured stories (STAR, METEOR) receive better hiring
recommendations, though only 23% do so [[2022-12-04_answer-ux-job-interview-questions]].

Influence across functions is presented as a communication problem too: learn how
product leaders operate in your specific organization by observing who is invited
to strategy meetings and which teams get funded, then match the register — metrics
like retention and conversion in data-driven companies, efficiency and
implementation cost in engineering-led ones, competitive differentiation in
product-led ones. Build deliberate alliances with influential product and
engineering stakeholders, choose battles by distinguishing fundamental usability
problems from negotiable preferences, and acquire baseline fluency in adjacent
disciplines [[2025-04-04_fuego-product-leaders]]. The **information pipeline** is a
systematic version of the same idea: gather from cross-team analytics, support
tickets, past research, roadmaps and communication archives; build relationships
with domain experts and map upstream and downstream dependencies; create
cross-functional spaces for sharing; and synthesize into credible recommendations
with tradeoff tables. Autonomy there comes from being informed enough to shape
shared decisions, not from deciding alone [[2026-04-17_information-pipeline]].

### Grow the team: mentorship, onboarding and rituals

Dashinsky extends collaboration past the current project to the making of the
team itself. Mentorship — actively helping colleagues grow through knowledge and
experience sharing, which he distinguishes from coaching, the more advanced form
that teaches someone to find solutions themselves — strengthens the team by
spreading expertise, letting teammates solve problems independently, and building
trust through the exchange; it also benefits the mentor, since teaching a subject
forces you to understand it well yourself
[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_20-18-mentorship]]. Opportunities are found actively, by rating teammates
against your own strongest competencies to locate gaps, and passively, by
noticing a teammate asking the same question repeatedly, another department
asking for something they could learn to do themselves, or someone struggling
despite feedback. Goals are set as outcomes rather than tools ("enable David to
ship interactive prototypes independently within three weeks, reducing developer
handoff time"), hard skills are taught with the EDIP model (explain, demonstrate,
imitate, practice), and soft skills by reverse-engineering your own approach into
its inputs, outputs and reasoning so the mental model transfers rather than the
example. Documentation and curated education hubs are offered as formats for
people who prefer them to live sessions
[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_20-18-mentorship]].

Hiring is presented as the same competency applied earlier: a leadership activity
open to individual contributors, not only managers, whose collaboration payoff is
bringing in cultural fit and diverse skills
[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_21-19-hiring-leadership]]. Cultural fit is read from how a candidate speaks
about past employers and teams, and from the questions they ask, which reveal what
they prioritise. Onboarding is where he puts the concrete advice: interview people
hired in the last six months about what confused or frustrated them and what they
wish they had known, document the professional ground (team structure and goals,
design processes, design systems, handoff standards, product and competition
knowledge, customer insights), connect new hires with domain experts, pair them
with a buddy, and add practical details such as lunch recommendations to reduce
the cognitive load of the first weeks. The argument for the effort is that a new
hire draws a salary while unable to generate value yet, so shortening the time to
peak performance is the point
[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_21-19-hiring-leadership]].

Rituals are the third lever, and the one that needs no mandate: initiating or
owning design critiques, huddles and celebrations contributes to collaboration
and to culture at the same time, as do company interest groups, which he notes
build relationships and visibility alongside improving the experience of work —
with particular value at larger companies, where such groups are more established
[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_22-20-culture-community]].

### Collaboration in Agile and at organizational scale

Within Agile delivery, ten practices were identified from 125 practitioners:
invest in release planning and discovery upfront, run design one to two sprints
ahead of development, build a collaborative culture using design-thinking
techniques (ideation, whiteboard sessions, journey mapping) to break silos, treat
design as iterative with low-fidelity prototypes, use the 15-minute standup to
keep UX activity visible and synchronized, and make user research a team event so
decisions rest on data rather than opinion [[2016-08-28_ux-success-agile]].

[[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_07-chapter-4-wrangling-engineers]]
describes the product manager's relationship to engineers in the same Agile
cadence, and defines it as facilitation rather than command: engineers do not
report to you 99% of the time, "nor should they," so the job is to provide focus,
direction and meaning, make goals clear, keep requests well scoped, and treat
engineers as core participants in the conversation and ideation that produces
solutions. Because engineers are makers who need uninterrupted time, it argues
for concentrating communication into two channels rather than micro-interruptions:
asynchronous written form developers can review when it suits them, and the
regular cadences of the Agile scrum rituals.
The cadences themselves are the primary channel, a fractal of daily stand-ups,
sprint rituals, monthly roadmap reviews, quarterly planning and annual
goal-setting that all repeat "iterate, review, recalibrate, repeat." Two
practices carry the weight: a definition of done tied to the original
requirements and to product acceptance, with team buy-in, which is what stops
constant slippage and the paralysis of perfectionism, and end-of-sprint
retrospectives, which the chapter treats as non-optional and as a driver of team
cohesion when run openly and supportively. Respect is earned rather than
assumed, by learning the technical lingo (it notes engineers use "design" to mean
something else), studying their folkways, and asking authentic questions that
build your own mental model of the architecture and its constraints instead of
pretending to a competence you lack. Estimation, on the same logic, is a
negotiation and not a demand: some engineers pad, some underestimate, and the
PM's job is to facilitate a balanced discussion toward a realistic assessment of
effort.

Above the team, several sources describe collaboration as an organizational design
problem. Silos inherited from scientific management fragment customer journeys at
the boundaries between functionally owned touchpoints, and many pain points cannot
be solved by product design alone; the answer is not destroying silos but enabling
connectivity across them, merging CX and UX so top-level business insight meets
user-centred research and design, and forming cross-functional journey teams
drawn from CX, UX, product, marketing, support, business and technology
[[2024-11-29_ux-and-cx-merge]]. Four organizations that made this shift report
that mindset change must precede operational change (a top-down mandate is not
enough — training, resource libraries and change management come first), that
methodology should be codified in a playbook with a dedicated owner, that
journey-operations teams should absorb onboarding and process flexibility, that
prioritizing on shared business objectives rather than departmental budgets turns
competition into cooperation, and that firm plans should extend only one quarter
out [[2025-01-24_journey-centric-design-lessons]].

Collaboration also decays if unattended. UX maturity regresses quietly: rituals
such as design reviews, retrospectives and research debriefs become hollow
checkboxes, and ownership concentrates in too few champions so the practice cannot
survive personnel changes. The countermeasures are structural refreshes (rotating
facilitators, new prompts, theme-based focus), a 30–45 minute quarterly check-in
on strategy, culture, process and outcomes, and a visible "UX health log" that
makes drift observable [[2025-09-19_signs-of-ux-maturity-regression]].

### AI as a participant

One study of 776 Procter & Gamble employees compared individuals and pairs working
with and without AI. Teams using AI produced both the highest average idea quality
and a disproportionate share of the top 10% of solutions; individuals with AI beat
individuals and teams without AI on average and matched AI teams on quantity and
detail, but did not match them on breakthrough ideas. The conclusion drawn is that
AI does not make team collaboration obsolete — it functions best as a teammate, and
because LLMs are built for individual use, leveraging them in a group requires
facilitation approaches different from traditional workshops. The same source notes
AI suits ideation, where "truth" is not the main criterion, more than data analysis,
where hallucination is an unacceptable risk [[2025-05-23_ai-creative-teammate]].
