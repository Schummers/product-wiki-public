# Roadmapping

## Definition

A UX roadmap is a strategic, living artefact that aligns, prioritises and
communicates a team's future work and the problems it needs to solve, acting as
a single source of truth and as the team's North Star
[[2020-11-01_roadmap-types]], [[2020-11-29_roadmapping-steps]],
[[2022-05-08_ux-roadmaps-faq]]. In the wider family of UX mapping methods it is
the one that looks forward: journey maps and service blueprints describe an
experience, roadmaps commit to what the team will work on next
[[2021-09-26_ux-mapping-methods-study-guide]].

The defining distinction in the corpus is roadmap versus project plan: a roadmap
is strategic and vision-oriented and captures problems to solve, while a project
plan is tactical and execution-focused and captures task details
[[2022-05-08_ux-roadmaps-faq]]. Roadmaps succeed when they make realistic
promises, value functionality over pretty visuals, and stay strategic documents
rather than feature-specific release plans [[2020-11-29_roadmapping-steps]]. Christian Crumlish states the same boundary from the product-management
side: a roadmap is a forward-looking document for prioritising near- and
medium-term efforts, not a launch plan and not a commitment to ship specific
features on fixed dates, and treating it as one is what causes misalignment and
breaks trust with stakeholders [[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_13-chapter-10-roadmaps-and-how-to-say-no]].

## Practice

### Choosing the scope

Three types are described, differing in breadth, owner and purpose
[[2020-11-01_roadmap-types]], [[2022-05-08_ux-roadmaps-faq]]:

- **Product roadmap** — the broadest, covering problems to solve across all
  departments including UX, engineering and marketing. Owned by product
  management and created collaboratively with cross-functional leads, which is
  what forces the tradeoff discussions and creates co-ownership of the vision.
  Pick it when the goal is a shared vision.
- **Field roadmap** — UX-specific, spanning research, design and content
  problems across several products. Owned by UX directors. It bridges UX
  disciplines and teams and educates stakeholders on the design process; it is
  reported as the most common type, because it coordinates across UX
  disciplines without restricting scope to a single product.
- **Specialty roadmap** — one UX area only, such as research or design, owned by
  the individual practitioner. Easiest to create given its narrow scope, and
  useful to communicate bandwidth allocation and balance workload.

The rule is to match roadmap type to the primary objective: product for shared
vision, field for UX education, specialty for resource allocation
[[2020-11-01_roadmap-types]].

### The six-step process

[[2020-11-29_roadmapping-steps]] lays out the sequence:

1. **Establish goals** — define the roadmap's primary purpose, decide the scope
   level (product, field, specialty), and identify the core team and
   stakeholders to involve.
2. **Gather inputs** — pull from existing artefacts (previous roadmaps, journey
   maps, service blueprints) and from research (qualitative and quantitative
   studies, support logs, surveys) to identify candidate problems.
3. **Create themes** — cluster candidate problems by affinity diagramming, then
   work each cluster into a polished theme with a beneficiary, a need and a
   business objective.
4. **Prioritise** — set 4–8 prioritisation criteria, both user-oriented and
   business-oriented, define a scoring scale, score each theme, then force rank
   to assign priorities across time horizons.
5. **Visualise and share** — fidelity should match audience and goal; internal
   planning can stay low fidelity, while stakeholder-facing roadmaps need higher
   fidelity, context and version tracking.
6. **Revisit** — a routine cadence, monthly at minimum, to move completed themes,
   update progress and add new ones, saving versions as you go.

The same source explicitly invites adapting the process: run it as a workshop,
draft a hypothesis roadmap alone then vet it with peers, or take one step a week
over a month.

### Prioritisation methods

Prioritisation should rest on objective criteria rather than subjective opinion,
and five methods are offered, each suited to a different context
[[2021-11-14_prioritization-methods]]:

- **Impact-effort matrix** — plots items by relative user value against
  implementation complexity, sorting them into quick wins, big bets, money pits
  and fill-ins. Good for quick, democratic, team-wide prioritisation.
- **Feasibility-desirability-viability scorecard** — scores each item on the
  three criteria and sums them; highly customisable, criteria can be added or
  swapped to match organisational priorities.
- **RICE** — reach × impact × confidence, divided by effort. Quantitative and
  formula-driven, suited to technical teams comfortable with data and with many
  items to rank.
- **MoSCoW** — clusters items into Must, Should, Could and Will Not Have through
  weighted voting and discussion; works best with clearly defined time horizons.
- **Kano model** — plots functionality against customer satisfaction to classify
  items as Attractive, Performance, Indifferent or Must-be; useful when a team
  struggles to hold user needs above business pressure.

No method is inherently better than another: choose on project context, team
culture and success criteria, and remember that the best method is the one the
whole team and its stakeholders buy into. These frameworks are not limited to
features — they also rank research questions, user segments, ideas and tasks
[[2021-11-14_prioritization-methods]].

### Now, Next, Later, and outcomes rather than features

Crumlish organises the roadmap into three time horizons, deliberately getting
vaguer the farther out they go: **Now**, what is currently being worked on;
**Next**, what the team is prepared to take up after the current items; and
**Later**, what matters but is not yet active [[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_13-chapter-10-roadmaps-and-how-to-say-no]]. What sits in those
horizons is desired outcomes and results, such as "double retention" or
"support user collaboration", grouped into themes that connect to company
strategy, not lists of feature requests or solutions [[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_13-chapter-10-roadmaps-and-how-to-say-no]]. The roadmap
also ladders team goals up to organisational strategy, often through OKRs, with
different levels of the organisation owning different pieces of it [[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_13-chapter-10-roadmaps-and-how-to-say-no]].

### Saying no

The same chapter treats declining requests as part of roadmapping rather than a
separate social skill: decline without diminishing the value of the request by
understanding the problem it aims to solve, connecting it to current
priorities, asking what existing commitments should be dropped to make room for
it, and asking for the data that would justify its priority [[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_13-chapter-10-roadmaps-and-how-to-say-no]].
Matt LeMay pushes back on the framing inside the same chapter: "I see PMs fall
in love with saying 'no' and miss out on critical information," so his rule is
to ask why before saying yes or no, since there is a reason behind an
executive's idea and it is the PM's job to understand it.

### Timing, effort and cadence

- Create a roadmap at key moments: a new initiative, a crisis, a leadership
  change, or annual planning [[2022-05-08_ux-roadmaps-faq]].
- It takes less than a week for most practitioners, typically produced in a
  roadmapping workshop lasting a few hours to a few days
  [[2022-05-08_ux-roadmaps-faq]].
- Update monthly through a recurring meeting, which is also what keeps the
  organisation aligned [[2022-05-08_ux-roadmaps-faq]]; the six-step process sets
  the same monthly minimum for revisiting [[2020-11-29_roadmapping-steps]].
- Crumlish splits the cadence in two: monthly reviews of the workload to keep
  the roadmap reflecting reality, and quarterly reviews to move items between
  horizons, with regular stakeholder updates whose job is managing expectations
  about when things will actually ship [[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_13-chapter-10-roadmaps-and-how-to-say-no]].

### Communicating it

- Present with storytelling and progressive disclosure — Now, Next, Future
  columns — rather than dropping the whole document at once
  [[2022-05-08_ux-roadmaps-faq]].
- Involve the right people at each stage: participation and ownership increase
  the likelihood of buy-in and support later
  [[2022-05-08_ux-roadmaps-faq]], [[2020-11-29_roadmapping-steps]].
- As a mapping method, the roadmap works as a shared visual artefact that aligns
  diverse stakeholders on direction
  [[2021-09-26_ux-mapping-methods-study-guide]].
