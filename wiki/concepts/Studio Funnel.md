# Studio Funnel

## Definition

The studio funnel is the repeatable pipeline through which a studio turns a large number of raw ideas into a small number of launched, funded and eventually exited companies. Szigeti calls it the main asset of a venture builder: the overall ability to build startups "by the dozen" comes from a disciplined sequence of stages, each with its own inputs, outputs, success criteria and resource envelope, so that ideas can be processed systematically and cheaply at scale ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_16-the-studio-funnel]]). Kannan and Peterman describe the same object from the outside, as a conversion process whose efficiency varies enormously between studios: the ones they interviewed needed anywhere from 30 to 107 top-level ideas to launch a single company ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_06-process]]).

The funnel is also the unit of financial modelling. Once the stages and their pass-through rates are set, they determine how many companies a studio produces, how much it burns to do so, and how long it must survive before the first exit ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_19-a-preview-on-financial-modeling]]). The playbook and the research book agree on the shape (ideation, validation, build, launch, follow-on funding, exit) and on the discipline (strict budgets, fast kills, an abundance of alternatives at the top). They differ on throughput: the playbook's stories and prescriptions aim at a dozen or more ventures a year, while the research book found that most studios launch at most four.

## Practice

### Stage structures found in the sources

Each source draws the funnel with a different number of stages. They are listed rather than merged, because the differences reflect real choices about where a studio draws its gates.

- **Szigeti's eight stages** — create an abundance of ideas; validate and select; trash the failures; initial product build; go/no-go decision; seed funding; scaling and independence; exit ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_16-the-studio-funnel]]).
- **Innonic's five stages** — ideation through continuous collection; research-based pre-validation (2-4 weeks); validation with a Minimal Awesome Product and a dedicated CEO (3-6 months); formation with significant capital (18 months, EUR 200,000 allocated); growth toward independence or venture-capital fundraising ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_12-changing-ecommerce-one-startup-at-a-time]]).
- **Drukka's sequence** — minimum market research, rapid MVP building, aggressive traction-seeking, business plan and investor deck preparation, seed fundraising ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_13-stretching-our-limits]]).
- **TechnoSpark's conveyor** — a hardware-oriented progression where startups launch, mature and exit at various maturity points, from launch through market-leadership ranking, tracked over 7-9 year cycles ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_06-technospark-a-conveyor-of-hardware-startups]]).
- **Kannan and Peterman's three steps** — brainstorming and early idea development; customer validation through user research and marketing testing; building and testing early prototypes. Variation between studios shows up in the stage gates, the evaluation criteria, the funnel efficiency and the number of companies launched per year ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_06-process]]).

### Filling the top

- Szigeti prescribes daily 60-minute ideation workshops, which he says yield 300+ viable startup concepts in a year. The reason is psychological as much as numerical: sunk cost fallacy is one of the leading ways studios burn cash, and it is easier to kill an idea when plenty of alternatives exist ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_16-the-studio-funnel]]).
- Innonic collects ideas continuously from customer conversations, conferences and reading into a shared spreadsheet, then has a Validation Team evaluate them with several models ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_12-changing-ecommerce-one-startup-at-a-time]]).
- Kannan and Peterman note that ideas can enter through structured founder programs, a few full-time EIR positions, or assignment of already-developed concepts to a team; none of the paths is financially superior in their model, and the choice depends on studio philosophy ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_21-other-assumptions]]).

### Scoring, budgeting, killing

- Rate ideas on three dimensions: severity of the problem, size of the market, and confidence that the studio can build something innovative that grows fast. Then budget strictly per idea, so that killing one never threatens the portfolio; Szigeti's example is 10k-20k EUR and 3-6 months for a sharing-economy concept ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_16-the-studio-funnel]]).
- Drukka's numbers match: starting from EUR 100,000 of seed capital, it allocated 10,000-20,000 USD per initiative, which forced it to begin with low-risk copycat sharing-economy ideas before moving to proprietary concepts ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_13-stretching-our-limits]]).
- Innonic budgets by layer: a couple thousand dollars for pre-validation, EUR 20,000 for full validation, EUR 200,000 at formation ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_12-changing-ecommerce-one-startup-at-a-time]]).
- Kills must be fast and transparent, with clear success and failure criteria and fixed deadlines; the studio's structural advantage over a lone startup is that the core team moves from a failed concept to the next one intact ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_16-the-studio-funnel]]).
- The research book observes the same practice: many ideas are killed in the earliest research stages, and the ones that advance are heavily scrutinised against short-term evaluation periods, because studios must fund ideas for years before a single exit and cannot afford to sink money into a failing one ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_06-process]]).

### Conversion rates

- Kannan and Peterman's headline figure: 30 to 107 top-level ideas per launched company, with more efficient funnels lowering the time and cost spent per idea ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_06-process]]).
- Drukka's reported outcome: of an initial dozen initiatives, almost half reached a seed-ready stage (working product, growing user base and revenue, sustainable growth engine) within 18-24 months ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_13-stretching-our-limits]]). Its later CEO process filtered 3-5 bare-minimum concept validations per incoming CEO down to the viable 1-2.
- Szigeti's financial chapter treats the pass-through rates (fresh ideas to validated ideas, validated ideas to built products, built products to seed funding) as the critical variables of the whole model: small changes in these rates dramatically change studio cash flow ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_19-a-preview-on-financial-modeling]]).
- The research book's evidence on the far end of the funnel: companies founded out of studios convert from Seed to Series A at 72% versus 42% for other companies ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_06-process]]).

### Throughput and cadence

This is where the two books diverge most clearly.

**Startup Studio Playbook:**

- Szigeti's portfolio guidance is to decide deliberately how many ventures run in parallel, with 12-15 per year given as an example, based on available capital and core-team capacity ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_16-the-studio-funnel]]).
- TechnoSpark launches 10-15 new companies annually ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_06-technospark-a-conveyor-of-hardware-startups]]).
- Drukka went from 6-7 concurrent ventures to a dozen a year by batching startups by industry, running joint mentoring sessions for related CEOs, reusing code bases, and outsourcing CEO pre-validation ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_13-stretching-our-limits]]).
- Innonic, by contrast, self-funds 2-3 new ventures a year from the profits of its legacy businesses ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_12-changing-ecommerce-one-startup-at-a-time]]).

**Venture Studios Demystified:**

- 73% of the studios interviewed aim to launch at most 4 companies a year, 13% target 4-6, and 13% launch 6-12. The authors read this spread as either differences in process efficiency or different bars for launch-readiness ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_06-process]]).
- The binding constraint they identify is people: each ideating team member can manage 1-3 ideas at a time, so team size directly caps parallel throughput ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_21-other-assumptions]]).

So the playbook's "by the dozen" ambition sits in the top 13% of what the research book observed. The research book does not say a dozen is wrong; it reports that most studios do not reach it.

### Timelines

- Innonic: 2-4 weeks research, 3-6 months validation, 18 months formation ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_12-changing-ecommerce-one-startup-at-a-time]]).
- Drukka: seed-funded companies reached their seed rounds within 18-24 months ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_13-stretching-our-limits]]).
- Kannan and Peterman: 6-18 months to Seed, then roughly 12 more months to Series A, consistent with the GSSN averages of 11 months to Seed and 15 to Series A ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_06-process]]); their model assumes a 12-month process from concept to spin-out, with a 6-18 month observed range ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_21-other-assumptions]]).
- TechnoSpark, in hardware, tracks 7-9 year cycles and aims to compress the 15-20 years an average hardware startup takes by a factor of 2-3 ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_06-technospark-a-conveyor-of-hardware-startups]]).

### The lower funnel: build, gate, fund, exit

- Build phase inputs: a dedicated CEO and possibly a CTO per initiative, a few weeks of build time for a simple web app with one core feature and good design, then an attempt to generate traction and test user acquisition before seeking follow-on funding ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_16-the-studio-funnel]]).
- Go/no-go: a working MVP, active users and a scalable growth engine (or, at a higher bar, product-market fit) are the criteria for the studio to fund a seed round and spin the company off, or redirect the resources ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_16-the-studio-funnel]]).
- Seed and spin-off: the studio's portfolio track record is leverage with investors; the spin-off negotiates which services the studio keeps providing and on what terms, revisits founder equity, and keeps the studio in an active co-founder role ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_16-the-studio-funnel]]).
- Exit: either quick, low-hanging acquisitions that build a pool of experienced local CEOs and recycle capital, or long-horizon attempts at market-capturing companies ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_16-the-studio-funnel]]). TechnoSpark's conveyor lets companies exit at various maturity points and collects detailed decision and market-access data from each for use in future ventures ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_06-technospark-a-conveyor-of-hardware-startups]]).

### Modelling the funnel

Szigeti recommends two spreadsheets: one for a single startup's cash flow (idea generation, validation, initial build, core-team and infrastructure costs), then an aggregate studio-funnel model driven by the pass-through rates at each stage. The output is the monthly burn of the core team multiplied by the years until the first major exit, which is the capital the studio has to raise or self-fund ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_19-a-preview-on-financial-modeling]]). The research book's model uses the same levers from the observed side: idea sources, ideas per team member, and a median 12-month process duration ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_21-other-assumptions]]).
