# Product Discovery

## Definition

Discovery is the preliminary phase of a design or product project: researching the problem space, framing the problem to be solved, and gathering enough evidence and initial direction to decide what to do next. It covers both the Discover and the Define stages of the double-diamond model, and a well-run discovery ensures that any solution proposed later is desirable to users, viable for the organization, and feasible with the available technology [[2020-03-15_discovery-phase]]. Its four expected outcomes are an understanding of users and their needs, an understanding of problems and opportunities, an identification of existing constraints, and a shared vision across the team about goals and measures of success [[2020-03-15_discovery-phase]]. It is one of the hardest parts of the design process precisely because it is filled with unknowns: the problem may be undefined, and who the users are and what they need is often open [[2024-09-27_7-tips-discovery]].

*Continuous Discovery Habits* reframes the word from a phase to a cadence. Discovery there is the work of deciding what to build, distinct from delivery, the work of building and shipping it, and continuous discovery is defined as a minimum of weekly touchpoints with customers by the team that builds the product, through small research activities conducted in service of a desired outcome [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_04-chapter-one-the-what-and-why-of-continuous-discovery]]. The point is a systematic approach a team adopts continuously rather than episodically, built on the insight that companies fail when they stop obsessing about customer needs, pain points, and desires [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_03-introduction]]. The NN/g sources above describe discovery as something a project has; the book describes it as something a team does every week.

Its purpose is exploration, not confirmation. The French-language corpus insists that discovery interviews should surface the customers' real problems upstream of a project rather than validate preconceived hypotheses [[2025-04-01_371_L_interview_longue_en_phase_de_Discovery_-_Guide_UXR]], and survey evidence shows some teams do the opposite, using discoveries to validate a solution they had already chosen [[2020-05-17_discoveries-in-industry-revealed]]. The same exploratory posture is applied beyond product features: an observation phase to identify a design team's friction points before launching DesignOps initiatives [[2026-03-17_405_Comment_adopter_le_DesignOps_-_Les_réponses_d_Amine_Ambari]], and an immersion into an unfamiliar industry before a product manager can form a view of it [[2026-05-26_415_Le_produit_dans_l_industrie_IA_Adaptabilité_&_lien_humain_-_Celine_Saint-Jean]]. One source in this set uses "discovery" in a different sense entirely: the mechanisms by which a shopper finds a desired product on an ecommerce site, through search, category browsing, faceted filtering, and recommendations [[2018-08-12_ecommerce-homepages-listing-pages]].

*User Story Mapping* grounds the same not-confirmation posture from inside the Agile literature. Patton describes discovery's goal as learning whether you are building the right thing: talking to customers, validating that the problems you think they have actually exist, prototyping solutions without building software, and iterating with real development partners once something ships, rather than assuming the initial idea is correct [[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_08-3-plan-to-learn-faster]]. He is explicit that discovery is learning, not building — it does not aim at shippable software or exhaustive requirements — and that a good discovery ends with more ideas discarded than kept: "if you're not cutting away more ideas than you keep, you're probably not doing discovery work right" [[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_19-14-using-discovery-to-build-shared-understanding]].

## Practice

### When discovery is warranted

Discovery is needed when many unknowns prevent moving forward, when the team is not aligned on goals, or in the face of a new market opportunity, an acquisition, a policy change, a shift in organizational strategy, or a chronic problem [[2020-03-15_discovery-phase]]. Rushing into implementation with too little time for UX activities is read as a sign of an organization's low UX maturity [[2020-05-17_discoveries-in-industry-revealed]]. The book frames the same imbalance at company level: many companies put heavy emphasis on delivery, on whether the team shipped what it said it would on time and on budget, while under-investing in discovery and forgetting to assess whether they built the right thing [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_04-chapter-one-the-what-and-why-of-continuous-discovery]]. Cagan adds that most teams genuinely want to do this work and understand the theory, but lack the coaching and structure to know what to do and when [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_02-foreword-marty-cagan]].

The evidence for doing it at all is quantitative: in a survey of 436 UX practitioners, 83% of projects that included a discovery phase were reported successful, against 52% of those without, and outright failure dropped from 16% to 4% [[2020-05-17_discoveries-in-industry-revealed]]. Yet only 20% of organizations run one for every project; most run one per two to four projects [[2020-05-17_discoveries-in-industry-revealed]].

### Framing the problem first

Before research begins, the team should identify and frame the problem. A problem statement is a concise description of what needs solving; it gives direction, creates alignment, and prevents discovery from meandering [[2021-08-22_problem-statements]]. It answers the five Ws — who is affected, what the problem is, where and when it occurs, and why it matters — and should carry three elements: background and origin of the problem, the people affected (internal and external), and the organizational impact, whether reputational, financial, or operational [[2021-08-22_problem-statements]]. A discovery effort should have one problem statement, focused on one problem, and the statement doubles as the communication tool that wins stakeholder buy-in for the exploration [[2021-08-22_problem-statements]]. The same framing can be written as an opportunity rather than a deficit; the structure is identical [[2021-08-22_problem-statements]].

Two sources add procedure to this. A clear statement is the third of seven tips for successful discoveries, because it fixes the scope and stops the team researching the wrong things [[2024-09-27_7-tips-discovery]]. In an Agile setting, have team members individually draft problem or opportunity statements and then converge to discuss and resolve misalignments — the divergence itself surfaces perspectives and knowledge gaps [[2023-02-05_discovery-in-agile]].

### Starting from opportunities

Patton names the largest unbroken unit of work an "opportunity": an idea the team believes will solve a problem, evaluated before anything gets broken down further, because there is never enough time and people to build every idea, and customers would be overwhelmed if there were [[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_18-13-start-with-opportunities]]. The first story conversation about an opportunity should establish who it is for, what problem it solves, what the product might look like, why it benefits the organization, and how it compares in size to past work; his Opportunity Canvas, built on Marty Cagan's Opportunity Assessment and Alexander Osterwalder's Business Model Canvas, organizes nine such topics spatially so a group can see dependencies at once [[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_18-13-start-with-opportunities]]. Patton is careful to say opportunities are not requirements: a roadmap of opportunities is not a mandate, and if the decision-makers are absent from the go/no-go conversation, the team should articulate its assumptions and invite them to correct it rather than proceed alone [[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_18-13-start-with-opportunities]].

Patton then structures the discovery that follows an opportunity into four steps: framing the idea, understanding customers and users, envisioning the solution, and minimizing and planning [[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_19-14-using-discovery-to-build-shared-understanding]]. Its outputs are not just smaller stories but simple models — personas, journey maps, story maps, UI sketches, architectural sketches — built collaboratively rather than locked away in a document nobody reads, because words alone let developers imagine something different from what was intended, and words and pictures together prevent that miscommunication [[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_19-14-using-discovery-to-build-shared-understanding]]. He also inverts the usual prioritization order: start from specific business goals, let those drive focus onto specific users and their goals, and only then arrive at features — most teams make the mistake of prioritizing features first [[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_19-14-using-discovery-to-build-shared-understanding]].

### Scoping and timeboxing

Discovery starts broad and requires the team to investigate the context of the problem [[2020-03-15_discovery-phase]], but breadth is not the same as unlimited scope. Teams should cluster the unknowns about users and the problem space and vote on which are the most important and most risky to address [[2024-09-27_7-tips-discovery]]. Timeboxing activities creates urgency and focus, countering the planning fallacy by which teams underestimate how long research takes [[2024-09-27_7-tips-discovery]].

The sources are not fully aligned on duration. The survey reports that 58% of discoveries last two weeks or less and that practitioners find this insufficient unless users are easily accessible and the problem space is small; time pressure to deliver is the single biggest barrier, cited by 35%, ahead of lack of management buy-in at 28% [[2020-05-17_discoveries-in-industry-revealed]]. The Agile guidance accepts the constraint instead of fighting it: discovery timing should not be arbitrarily fixed to sprint length, and the healthy mindset is to scale discovery, not skip it — focusing on the specific, valuable activities and assumptions rather than every possible question [[2023-02-05_discovery-in-agile]].

### From phase to cadence: continuous discovery

The book traces an evolution the timeboxing debate above does not mention: from annual, business-driven planning, through Agile practices that shortened delivery cycles from yearly to weekly releases and brought in more customer feedback, to a point where teams could ship fast but still struggled to decide what to build, until discovery practices became integrated into daily decision-making [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_04-chapter-one-the-what-and-why-of-continuous-discovery]]. The resulting cadence is weekly contact with customers, carried by the building team itself, and a digital product is never done: it should keep evolving as the market, customer needs, and available technology change [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_04-chapter-one-the-what-and-why-of-continuous-discovery]].

Adopting that cadence rests on six prerequisite mindsets: outcome-oriented, customer-centric, collaborative, visual, experimental, and continuous, with a focus on the value created rather than the features shipped [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_04-chapter-one-the-what-and-why-of-continuous-discovery]]. Success is then defined as the value the shipped code creates for customers and for the business, not as the code itself [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_04-chapter-one-the-what-and-why-of-continuous-discovery]]. Mercuri's foreword describes what the shift looked like on one team: from heavy, infrequent research to deeper understanding through regular contact and lightweight methods; from long lists of metrics and outputs to fewer goals held with greater clarity; from falling in love with a single idea to generating many and testing them faster through smaller simulations; and from big reports to showing leaders the team's thinking earlier and having better conversations about where to go next [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_01-foreword-chris-mercuri]]. The reward the team reports beyond better outcomes is confidence [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_01-foreword-chris-mercuri]].

Two framings of duration therefore coexist in this corpus. The NN/g survey and Agile guidance treat discovery as a bounded effort to be scoped and timeboxed [[2020-05-17_discoveries-in-industry-revealed]], [[2023-02-05_discovery-in-agile]]; the book treats it as a habit with no end date, kept alive by weekly interviews [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_04-chapter-one-the-what-and-why-of-continuous-discovery]]. The practical payoff of the second framing is agility inside the practice: one team killed an opportunity on Tuesday, chose a new one on Wednesday, and used its already-scheduled Thursday interviews to learn about it [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_15-chapter-twelve-managing-the-cycles]].

### Discovery inside Agile: the SCALE framework

The SCALE mnemonic structures discovery within sprint constraints [[2023-02-05_discovery-in-agile]]:

- **Speak and spike** — communicate discovery needs, and use spikes to buy dedicated sprint time for research. Constrain a spike to one or two key questions and complete it early in the sprint so it can inform the delivery work that follows.
- **Capture** — identify where the team is aligned and where it conflicts.
- **Assign** — distribute the activities.
- **Learn and share** — communicate findings continuously rather than in one final handover.
- **Evaluate and decide** — assess whether the team is ready to move forward.

Supporting conditions matter as much as the mnemonic: UX representation in leadership, tracking discovery velocity, and avoiding premature assignment of delivery tasks [[2023-02-05_discovery-in-agile]].

### The team

The ideal discovery team is three to seven dedicated members: someone who can research, a facilitator or team leader, a sponsor or owner with domain expertise and organizational influence, and someone technical [[2020-03-15_discovery-phase]]. Reality is smaller and thinner: the average discovery team is four people, typically designers (74%), researchers (60%), and product managers (52%), with only 36% including a product owner [[2020-05-17_discoveries-in-industry-revealed]].

Cross-functional participation is repeatedly urged. UX, product, and engineers should all take part, because the shared understanding built collaboratively speeds delivery and reduces rework [[2023-02-05_discovery-in-agile]]. Involving the whole team in the research itself gives members firsthand knowledge of user needs and pain points, creating empathy and consensus [[2024-09-27_7-tips-discovery]]. Roles and norms — who owns which task, meeting frequency, who attends research, how things are documented — should be agreed collaboratively and as early as possible, for instance in a kick-off workshop; this prevents confusion and boosts morale [[2024-09-27_7-tips-discovery]].

The book names the unit: the product trio, a cross-functional team of product manager, designer, and software engineer who make product decisions together, leveraging each role's expertise while balancing speed of decision-making against inclusiveness; it can be expanded with additional members when the speed of decision-making allows [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_04-chapter-one-the-what-and-why-of-continuous-discovery]]. In Mercuri's account discovery became a whole-team responsibility, with most team members involved in customer interviews, journey mapping, ideation, and discussing results [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_01-foreword-chris-mercuri]]. The trio's diversity is put to work deliberately: each member first works individually, then perspectives are merged, so that decisions are not made by the loudest voice or the most senior person [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_07-chapter-four-visualizing-what-you-know]]. This converges with the NN/g counsel that the whole team should take part in the research itself [[2024-09-27_7-tips-discovery]].

Stakeholder buy-in is the recurring failure point. Without it, discoveries get cut short or go unsupported [[2024-09-27_7-tips-discovery]], and practitioners report struggling to demonstrate discovery's ROI, particularly when leadership believes it already knows the problem [[2020-05-17_discoveries-in-industry-revealed]].

### Activities

Common discovery activities are exploratory research (user interviews, diary studies, field studies), stakeholder interviews, workshops (kickoff, assumption mapping, research-question generation, affinity diagramming, mapping, ideation), and follow-up exploration [[2020-03-15_discovery-phase]]. In practice the two dominant ones are user interviews (62%) and stakeholder interviews (60%) [[2020-05-17_discoveries-in-industry-revealed]]. Discovery includes more than user research: business documents, stakeholder interviews, analytics, past research, and heuristic evaluation all count, and prioritizing among them is what makes discovery fit the time available [[2023-02-05_discovery-in-agile]].

Patton treats the story conversation itself as a discovery activity, not just a documentation format. His checklist for a good one — really talking about who is affected, digging into why with the "why stick," discussing context and failure modes, naming assumptions, and asking whether a better solution exists — is how a team learns what is really needed [[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_12-7-telling-better-stories]]. Discovery is where the team learns what problem to solve and what solution is small enough and valuable enough to build; these conversations constrain the solution and eliminate waste before anything is built, which is the same "cutting away more than you keep" discipline the four-step process above insists on [[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_15-10-bake-stories-like-cake]].

### Interviewing in discovery

The long discovery interview has its own discipline [[2025-04-01_371_L_interview_longue_en_phase_de_Discovery_-_Guide_UXR]]:

- Define the interview's objective before starting — adjusting a roadmap, finding opportunities. An unprepared long interview is set up to fail, and poor preparation produces superficial data or slides into confirmation bias.
- Choose among three approaches: purely exploratory interviewing; probe testing, where a long-term vision (mockups, futuristic concepts) is shown to provoke immediate reactions and capture emotion; and co-construction with expert users. Each serves different objectives and different targets.
- Ban closed questions that end in yes or no. Open questions let the user develop their thinking and reveal unexpected information.
- Do not rush to fill silences; leaving the blank lets the user go deeper. Reformulating what the user said invites them to correct imprecisions and point at the real irritants.
- Ask what users actually do to solve their problems, not what they would want. Present behaviour is a more reliable expression of real needs than projection.

### Mapping to manage the volume

Discovery generates many new learnings fast, and mapping is how teams keep that volume manageable and actionable: it creates shared understanding, breaks a large problem into smaller chunks, and highlights key actors, causal links, and root causes [[2022-01-16_mapping-in-discovery]]. Maps also exploit the picture-superiority effect — people retain visual information better than text — and force questions ("what happens after this?", "how does this event affect users or the business?") that expose unknowns the team must resolve before deciding [[2022-01-16_mapping-in-discovery]].

Three methods, each for a different moment [[2022-01-16_mapping-in-discovery]]:

- **Ecosystem maps** — hub-and-spoke or concentric circles showing the people, organizations, products, and services around a central user, with distance standing for metaphorical closeness. Most useful at the very beginning, when domain complexity is still unknown.
- **Chronological maps** — experience maps, service blueprints, user-journey maps. Each answers a different question: new products and services, business-process problems, or improving the customer experience of an existing product.
- **Process maps** — flowcharts, sequence diagrams, HTA diagrams. More granular, created later once a specific critical area has been isolated.

Mapping is not a one-sitting exercise. Start low-fidelity, on flipchart paper with sticky notes, precisely to signal that the map will change as learning progresses; schedule sessions to update it, and digitize or finalize only at the end of discovery [[2022-01-16_mapping-in-discovery]]. Picking the one map type that aligns with the discovery's goal keeps the effort focused rather than overwhelming [[2022-01-16_mapping-in-discovery]].

The book puts one map at the very start, before any interview: an experience map of what the team currently knows about how customers experience a process, so discovery begins by taking inventory of the known and its gaps [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_07-chapter-four-visualizing-what-you-know]]. Drawing on cognitive-science findings that an external representation creates common ground, it asks each trio member to draw their own map individually, with boxes, arrows, and stick figures rather than words, because language is vague and drawing is more specific and reveals gaps a discussion would obscure; the individual maps are then merged into a shared one [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_07-chapter-four-visualizing-what-you-know]]. The map's scope follows the desired outcome, narrow for an optimization outcome and broader for an open-ended one, and it captures what customers think, feel, and do at each step, including where they get stuck, go wrong, course-correct, or abandon, not just the happy path [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_07-chapter-four-visualizing-what-you-know]]. Both sources agree that the map is a first draft: the NN/g guidance keeps it low-fidelity so it can change [[2022-01-16_mapping-in-discovery]], and the book treats it as hunches, not truth, to be revised week to week as interviews teach the team more [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_07-chapter-four-visualizing-what-you-know]].

### Structuring the opportunity space

Patton's own opportunities stay flat, evaluated roughly one at a time as they arrive and mapped as colored sticky notes over the existing product to reveal hot spots of need [[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_18-13-start-with-opportunities]]. Continuous interviewing generates countless opportunities, the needs, pain points, and desires customers reveal, and the book's answer to that volume is the opportunity solution tree, a structure that organizes them so the team can reason about, assess, and prioritize them systematically instead of jumping from one need to another [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_09-chapter-six-mapping-the-opportunity-space]]. Where a flat backlog hides dependencies and overlaps, a tree exposes them through parent-child (subset) and sibling (distinct but related) relationships, letting the team break large, seemingly unsolvable problems into smaller sub-opportunities and deliver value over time [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_09-chapter-six-mapping-the-opportunity-space]].

Building it follows rules. Top-level branches map to distinct moments in the customer's journey, so that they are independent and non-overlapping; both the experience map and the patterns across interviews can be used to identify those moments [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_09-chapter-six-mapping-the-opportunity-space]]. An opportunity enters the tree only if it is framed as a customer need rather than a solution, appears in more than one interview, and aligns with the desired outcome; anything else is a side quest [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_09-chapter-six-mapping-the-opportunity-space]]. Structure is then added by grouping similar opportunities as siblings, finding the parent that encompasses them, merging redundant phrasings, and iterating until every branch descends from its top-level parent. The anti-patterns named are solutions disguised as opportunities (an opportunity admits multiple solutions, a solution is one path), framing so broad that an item belongs under several parents, and missing parents that leave a vertical stack [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_09-chapter-six-mapping-the-opportunity-space]]. The decision consequence is that the team compares siblings at each level rather than every opportunity at once, which the book presents as a guard against analysis paralysis [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_09-chapter-six-mapping-the-opportunity-space]].

### Generating solutions: ideation

Once a target opportunity is chosen, ideation is the bridge to testing: it produces the solution candidates the team will explore, distinguish, and refine [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_11-chapter-eight-supercharged-ideation]]. The book's guidance runs against the usual brainstorm. Creativity research shows individuals ideating alone outperform brainstorming groups in quantity, diversity, and originality, because groups suffer social loafing, conformity, production blocking, and downward norm setting while feeling more productive than they are; so the team alternates individual ideation with group sharing, and never ideates as a group in real time [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_11-chapter-eight-supercharged-ideation]]. Quantity is treated as the best predictor of quality, with the most original ideas arriving late in a session, so the cycle repeats until 15-20 ideas exist, spread across time and places, with breaks for incubation and deliberate looks at analogous products in unrelated industries to get categorically different ideas rather than variations on one [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_11-chapter-eight-supercharged-ideation]]. Evaluation is a group act: filter out ideas that do not address the target opportunity, then dot-vote with three votes per person to select three finalists, so that every team member is excited about at least one [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_11-chapter-eight-supercharged-ideation]]. This sits alongside the NN/g counsel to analyse and ideate as a team [[2024-09-27_7-tips-discovery]]: the book agrees on group evaluation but insists the generation itself be individual.

### Analysis and outputs

Analyze, synthesize, and ideate as a team: cluster observations, map learnings, and generate How-Might-We questions together, which yields better insights and more creative solution ideas than solo analysis [[2024-09-27_7-tips-discovery]]. Typical deliverables are problem statements, finalized maps, user-need statements, personas, and high-level concepts or wireframes to be explored in the develop phase [[2020-03-15_discovery-phase]].

The book adds an output the NN/g list does not carry: a decision not to build. One team found that customers complained about late payments but did not want third-party help solving the problem, and killing that opportunity quickly after testing saved weeks of development; the book's phrase is that the fruit of discovery work is often the time saved by deciding not to build something [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_15-chapter-twelve-managing-the-cycles]].

### Discovery is cyclical

The book is explicit that discovery is messy and non-linear: teams loop back through earlier habits, interviewing, opportunity assessment, brainstorming, several times as they learn, and effective teams expect forks in the road rather than a straight run from discovery into delivery [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_15-chapter-twelve-managing-the-cycles]]. Four cases illustrate the moves. Not every validated opportunity needs a solution now (the late-payments example above). Opportunities can be sorted temporally into now, next, and future, as a team did by deferring cosmetic-condition solutions requiring heavier investment until it had validated the foundational need with vehicle-specific data, which later made the case for that investment [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_15-chapter-twelve-managing-the-cycles]]. Conflicting customer and business needs can be reconciled by finding the moment where customers are already willing to engage in the desired channel, as a lender did with "What can I afford?" as a digital opportunity while customers still valued human contact elsewhere in the loan process [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_15-chapter-twelve-managing-the-cycles]]. And a big problem such as "I can't get in touch with candidates" can be decomposed into sub-opportunities (calling is ineffective, a need for text communication, scheduling friction, no-show management) and solved incrementally, with small changes compounding into meaningful business impact rather than one big, hairy problem solved at once [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_15-chapter-twelve-managing-the-cycles]]. The discipline the trio needs is to recognize when it is going down a troublesome path and course-correct together [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_15-chapter-twelve-managing-the-cycles]].

Patton's book carries a lighter-weight version of the same loop, tied to a single release rather than to managed cycles of an opportunity tree. After a release ships and the team tests it with users, the team writes new story cards capturing what it learned, feeding them back into the discovery cycle so the product keeps evolving rather than being finalized at ship — "handing off all the details about the story to someone else to build doesn't work" is the failure mode this loop is meant to prevent [[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_14-9-the-card-is-just-the-beginning]]. His own worked example, a product owner named Eric at Liquidnet, runs the loop at the scale of one product: validate that customers have the problem, sketch and prototype without building software, ship a "minimum viable product experiment" that is deliberately less than viable to a small group of development partners, measure what they actually do rather than what they say they like, and keep iterating until those partners start recommending the product to others [[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_08-3-plan-to-learn-faster]].

### Keeping the practice alive

The habits come from a coaching practice: Torres applied product thinking to her own curriculum, measuring outcomes across cohorts, identifying where teams got stuck, and iterating on her methods, and teams leave her 12-week program with the habits to discover, iterate, and refine products [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_03-introduction]]. Cagan's foreword presents experienced product coaches as the most scalable way to improve organizations, since world-class internal leaders are hard to hire, and the book as a substitute for a coach one does not have [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_02-foreword-marty-cagan]]. The closing chapter treats the book as a starting point rather than an ending: translating book learning into workplace practice is hard, and the practice continues through a newsletter, a community of practice, skill-specific deep-dives such as story-based interviewing and opportunity mapping, master classes, and one-on-one coaching for a team's own organizational constraints [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_18-chapter-fifteen-whats-next]].

### Discovery in an unfamiliar domain

Entering an emerging industry demands a deeper immersion: combining colleagues' internal expertise with self-training through videos, podcasts, and courses, while staying critical of how fast those resources go obsolete [[2026-05-26_415_Le_produit_dans_l_industrie_IA_Adaptabilité_&_lien_humain_-_Celine_Saint-Jean]]. The same source warns against the opposite failure — becoming so technical that the product manager is biased and forgets that users, often novices, need pedagogy and a business-oriented framing rather than toxic complexity. Adaptability, and the willingness to accept not being an expert, is presented as the key competence in that setting [[2026-05-26_415_Le_produit_dans_l_industrie_IA_Adaptabilité_&_lien_humain_-_Celine_Saint-Jean]].

The DesignOps case applies discovery internally: start with an observation phase to identify the team's pain points, then distribute the corrective actions among designers, for instance through guilds, before considering a dedicated role — do not charge in head-down, but proceed in a reasoned and evidenced way [[2026-03-17_405_Comment_adopter_le_DesignOps_-_Les_réponses_d_Amine_Ambari]].

### Product discovery by the user

In the ecommerce sense of the term, discovery is a property of the interface. Navigational options and category labels must make sense both on their own and in relation to each other, and polyhierarchy — letting an item sit under several parent categories — improves findability [[2018-08-12_ecommerce-homepages-listing-pages]]. Category and listing pages can be merged by surfacing subcategories separately above the product listings, which encourages browsing and reduces choice overload while keeping items discoverable. Listings should carry a concise name, a large identifying photo, available colors and styles, price, and optionally ratings, availability, and a quick-view tool. On mobile, every click matters, so listing pages must carry enough differentiating information for a decision without a trip to the detail page [[2018-08-12_ecommerce-homepages-listing-pages]].
