# Young Users

## Definition

"Young users" covers a wide developmental span, from small children through
teenagers to young adults, and the sources treat it as several distinct
audiences rather than one. Children aged 3–12 differ from adults in cognitive
development, physical ability, reading skill, and motivation, and "children and
adults are different, and children need a design style that follows different
usability guidelines" [[2019-01-13_childrens-websites-usability-issues]].
Teenagers aged 13–17 form a separate group with their own developmental profile
in reading, patience, and risk assessment
[[2019-03-17_usability-of-websites-for-teenagers]]. Young adults aged 18–25 are
studied as a third [[2016-04-10_young-adults-ux]]. Across these bands the
sources insist on fine-grained distinctions: the minimum useful split for
children is young (3–5), mid-range (6–8), and older (9–12), and children
themselves are acutely aware of age mismatches
[[2019-01-13_childrens-websites-usability-issues]]; the gap between a 7- and a
17-year-old is far more pronounced than between a 37- and a 47-year-old
[[2019-04-28_usability-testing-minors]].

The other constant is the demolition of the "digital native" myth. Being raised
in a media-saturated digital world does not confer superior social skills,
better multitasking, or innate technical knowledge
[[2016-01-03_millennials-digital-natives]]; children are not magic tech-wizards
[[2019-01-13_childrens-websites-usability-issues]]; and teens are not tech
wizards craving multimedia and graphics
[[2019-03-17_usability-of-websites-for-teenagers]]. Eighteen years of usability
testing with 125 children showed that most guidelines held steady across
decades, confirming that usability is driven by human cognitive capacity rather
than by technology [[2019-01-13_childrens-websites-usability-issues]].

## Practice

### Physical development and interaction design

Gross motor skills develop earlier than fine motor skills, which sets what
interactions are possible at each age
[[2018-07-08_children-ux-physical-development]]. Children under 5 have limited
motor abilities and need simple touchscreen interactions; 6–8 year-olds can
manage simple clicking and keyboard use; around 9 more advanced interactions
become possible; by 11 children can use roughly the same interaction range as
adults [[2018-07-08_children-ux-physical-development]]. Concrete
recommendations: for under-9s emphasise swiping, tapping, and dragging, and make
buttons at least 2cm × 2cm; on desktop avoid complex gestures like dragging or
scrolling for under-9s, sticking to simple clicks and arrow keys; avoid
two-handed use or quick manual reactions for under-8s; allow alternative
interaction methods (dragging or tapping) for the same task; and reduce
navigation demands by recommending related content rather than sending children
back to a homepage, which also spares working memory
[[2018-07-08_children-ux-physical-development]]. The stakes are motivational:
"UIs that exceed the kids' motor ability will discourage them and even make them
lose confidence in the device"
[[2018-07-08_children-ux-physical-development]].

### Cognitive development

Children's reasoning, inference, and connection-making abilities are still
developing through the preoperational (2–7) and concrete operational (7–11)
stages [[2018-12-16_kids-cognition]]. Five design implications follow: state
goals and operations explicitly, since children cannot infer unstated goals;
tailor instructions to comprehension level, because under-6s cannot reliably
interpret subtle feedback or infer others' emotions and need exaggerated cues
and explicit language; leverage existing mental models through skeuomorphic
design and real-world metaphors, which cut cognitive load; reduce cognitive load
generally with self-explanatory interfaces, error prevention, and autocorrect
(search autocorrection compensates for spelling difficulty); and balance clarity
with flexibility, avoiding overly prescriptive instructions such as specifying
mouse versus trackpad [[2018-12-16_kids-cognition]].

Reading skill is called a primary design differentiator: pre-readers, beginning
readers, and moderately skilled readers need different font sizes, text lengths,
and complexity [[2019-01-13_childrens-websites-usability-issues]].

### Technology experience and expectations

Many young children now use tablets and smartphones for over a year before ever
touching a computer, so touch interaction, swiping, scrolling, and app
navigation are primary while mouse and keyboard are secondary or unfamiliar
[[2019-01-13_childrens-websites-usability-issues]]. Exposure to interactive
experiences raises expectations across the board: some children tried tapping
non-touchscreen displays [[2019-01-13_childrens-websites-usability-issues]].
Children with device experience do become comfortable trying several approaches
before giving up (refreshing, restarting), while still lacking advanced
troubleshooting skills [[2019-01-13_childrens-websites-usability-issues]].

### Designing for teenagers

The fifteen-year study of 100 teenagers across 210 websites and 30 apps found
teens are goal-oriented rather than aimless browsers, so usability matters as
much for them as for anyone [[2019-03-17_usability-of-websites-for-teenagers]].
Guidance: avoid heavy text and use visual formatting with white space and bullet
points, since teens get enough reading at school and dense screens bore them;
present professional, clear content instead of pointless multimedia and fancy
interactive features; treat speed as critical, since slow sites are
deal-breakers and heavy widgets fail on older devices and slower connections;
avoid condescending tone and childish design, including the word "kids", garish
colours, and gratuitous animation; and design mobile-first, avoiding complex
mouse gestures, small buttons, and rollover effects that do not translate to
touch [[2019-03-17_usability-of-websites-for-teenagers]]. Content should be
written at a sixth-grade reading level, in short sentences with bullet points
and chunked information [[2019-03-17_usability-of-websites-for-teenagers]].

### Designing for young adults

Young adults are extremely confident navigating interfaces, even radically new
design patterns, and that confidence makes them error-prone: they click before
thinking and rarely blame themselves for usability issues
[[2016-04-10_young-adults-ux]]. The Millennials research describes the same
pattern, adding that they are quick to criticise the interface, its
organisation, or its designers [[2016-01-03_millennials-digital-natives]]. On
multitasking, both sources converge: young adults perform parallel activities
sequentially, losing context and efficiency to rapid context-switching
[[2016-04-10_young-adults-ux]], and Millennials may choose to multitask more but
are not more efficient at it, with heavy multitaskers performing worse at
task-switching than light ones [[2016-01-03_millennials-digital-natives]].

Behavioural specifics: intensive browser-tab use, both page parking (multiple
tabs supporting a single task) and parallel browsing (one tab per task), more
than older adults [[2016-04-10_young-adults-ux]]
[[2016-01-03_millennials-digital-natives]]; above-average ability to determine
clickability in flat designs, and even less reading than the average user
[[2016-01-03_millennials-digital-natives]]; and expectations of Google-level
simplicity carried into complex enterprise applications
[[2016-01-03_millennials-digital-natives]]. Young adults are also more skeptical
of online information than teenagers, demand more evidence for claims, and are
sensitive to tone, disliking condescension or sites trying too hard to be cool
[[2016-04-10_young-adults-ux]].

One clear age contrast: unlike teenagers, young adults appreciate interactive
features such as games and quizzes only when they serve the current task —
gratuitous interactivity feels insulting [[2016-04-10_young-adults-ux]]. The
research across seven countries and 91 young adults found no major behavioural
differences by country, suggesting the guidelines hold globally
[[2016-04-10_young-adults-ux]].

### Researching with minors: study design

Testing with minors requires adaptation at every phase — recruitment, task
writing, environment, facilitation [[2019-04-28_usability-testing-minors]].
Recruit extra participants, since both parent and child affect attendance and
roughly one in nine recruited users fails to show; segment by maturity and
interest rather than age alone; use age-appropriate incentives such as gift
cards, small toys, or stickers rather than cash, remembering parents may control
money; keep sessions short with breaks (3–12 year-olds tolerate 60 minutes,
teenagers 90); prepare more and more varied tasks than you expect to need, since
children and teens disengage easily; and facilitate with warmth and praise,
dressing casually, because young children go quiet if the facilitator keeps a
poker face, which makes think-aloud harder to elicit
[[2019-04-28_usability-testing-minors]]. The environment should be
child-friendly but distraction-free, and youths are likely to feel uncomfortable
talking with an unfamiliar adult in a lab setting
[[2019-04-28_usability-testing-minors]].

### Researching with minors: consent and assent

Minors, typically under 18, cannot legally give informed consent; ethical
research requires both written parental or guardian consent and verbal assent
from the child or teen [[2026-02-13_research-minors-consent]]. Assent must be
developmentally appropriate: the child must understand what is being asked and
feel free to decline or stop, in plain language matched to their age, and a
child should never be expected to read or comprehend an adult-style consent form
[[2026-02-13_research-minors-consent]]. Consent forms must cover purpose, what
the child will do, data storage and protection, compensation, the voluntary
nature of participation, and researcher contact
[[2026-02-13_research-minors-consent]]. A verbal assent script explains what you
are doing, what they will do, and that they can stop anytime, looking for clear
verbal agreement rather than a nod [[2026-02-13_research-minors-consent]].

Parental presence varies by age: 3–6 year-olds may benefit from a parent nearby,
positioned behind and silent; 7–12 year-olds are comfortable with a parent
nearby or outside the room; teens typically participate alone
[[2026-02-13_research-minors-consent]]. Compensation should be fair and
age-appropriate: monetary compensation to parents for their time and effort,
small gift cards for children 9 and up, a choice from pre-approved toys for
3–8 year-olds [[2026-02-13_research-minors-consent]].

The stated rationale for doing this work at all: including minors surfaces real
usage patterns, helps teams design with empathy, ensures products are safe and
age-appropriate, and boosts inclusivity, including surfacing usability issues
for nonreaders [[2026-02-13_research-minors-consent]].
