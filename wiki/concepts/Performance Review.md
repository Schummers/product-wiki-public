# Performance Review

## Definition

A performance review is the formal evaluation a designer goes through with
their manager and, in many companies, with peers. The sources treat it less as
a verdict than as a checkpoint: [[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_02-why-you-should-read-this-book]]
describes it as an event where designers learn which skills they need to
improve, and frames the book's material as a way of turning that feedback into
a development plan. Where a company runs a competencies matrix, the review is
what that matrix calibrates: [[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_07-5-design-competencies]]
says companies use these documents to calibrate internal evaluation and set
growth goals, and is explicit that they do so because career growth correlates
with the value a designer brings to the business, not out of altruism.

The recurring argument across the sources is that a review is only as good as
what feeds it. On the input side that means external, non-self-generated
assessment ([[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_09-7-assess-and-prioritize-competencies]])
and feedback tracked over time
([[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_14-12-feedback-communication]]);
on the output side, a plan and a record that live between reviews rather than
being assembled the week before
([[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_10-8-design-career-growth-canvas]],
[[2024-08-20_342_Votre_archive_design_grâce_au_brag_document_-_Tuto_étape_par_étape]]).

## Practice

### Assess yourself against something other than yourself

[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_09-7-assess-and-prioritize-competencies]]
argues that self-assessment alone is insufficient, invoking the
Dunning–Kruger effect: people with low ability overestimate their competence,
and the gap between self-evaluation and actual skill shrinks as competence
rises. Its recommendations:

- Combine manager and peer input, ideally as 360-degree feedback. If the
  employer does not provide it, ask colleagues or ex-managers who know your
  work well.
- Judge readiness by the majority rule the chapter reports: companies typically
  expect most competencies at a level, not every one, and expect them
  demonstrated consistently over time rather than once.
- Prioritise what to develop by weighting industry requirements, your own
  career goal (the closer you are to quitting, the heavier that weight), and
  the opinions of people who know your work.

### Keep a plan between reviews

[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_10-8-design-career-growth-canvas]]
positions its canvas explicitly as structure and documentation *between* formal
reviews, so progress can be measured and discussed without waiting for the
annual or semi-annual cycle.

- For each competency, record current level, target level, action items, the
  next step for the coming days, a timeline, and a metric.
- Because many competency improvements are hard to measure objectively, the
  chapter treats regular one-on-ones with the manager as the practical way to
  get feedback on progress.
- Share the plan with the manager as a question ("Here's what I'd like to
  improve and here's my plan. What do you think?"), which educates them about
  growth planning and puts the responsibility to support it on them.
- Revisit it twice a year, or whenever context changes: new career goal, new
  manager feedback, change in employment status, shift in personal
  circumstances.

### Turn scattered feedback into growth signals

[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_14-12-feedback-communication]]
proposes treating feedback as data rather than incidents: document feedback on
things within your control, categorise it (oral and written communication, UX
skills, strategy, and so on), and calculate frequency and severity scores. The
chapter argues patterns reveal areas for improvement more reliably than
individual incidents, and adds a second tracking method: document what you
thought was the right thing to ship against what was eventually shipped, and
read the delta as an indicator of what to improve.

### Keep an archive of what you actually did

[[2024-08-20_342_Votre_archive_design_grâce_au_brag_document_-_Tuto_étape_par_étape]]
presents the brag document as an archive of projects, successes and failures,
and names performance reviews with managers as one of the situations where
keeping it pays off. Its advice is about lowering friction so the habit
survives:

- Pick a format (graphic, textual, audio) that matches your existing habits and
  tools, since the higher the bar you set yourself, the less likely you are to
  hold it over time.
- Use a deliberately minimal template (context, actions, links to mockups). The
  author is blunt that attempting an exhaustive case study for every project is
  the direct route to failure in the large majority of cases.
- Update on a regular rhythm, end of day or end of week, via a reminder or a
  to-do list item, so details are captured while memory is fresh.
- Keep the document private: that removes the pressure to make it good and
  leaves you free to make it merely useful. Clean formatting comes later, only
  when there is a real need to present it.

### Make the review a summary, not a reveal

[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_24-22-getting-promoted]] argues
that accomplishments should be documented systematically and shared in
one-on-ones *before* the formal review, so the review summarises achievements
the manager already knows about rather than surprising them. The same chapter
frames business impact as the lens for that documentation, and notes that at
most companies promotion is decided by a committee rather than by the manager
alone. [[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_02-why-you-should-read-this-book]]
adds the case of the solo designer managed by non-designers, with no design
peers or design leadership to champion their needs, who has to take ownership
of this process alone.
