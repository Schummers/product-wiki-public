# Design Critique

## Definition

A design critique is the structured practice of putting design work in front of
stakeholders and teammates while it can still change, so that feedback reaches
the design before it is final [[2021-05-30_derailed-design-critiques]]. The
corpus describes it in two settings that share the same mechanics: as one
segment inside a collaborative workshop, where a "Present and Critique" phase
sits between individual sketching and convergence
[[2017-07-02_facilitating-design-studio-workshop]], and as the recurring
stakeholder meeting where a designer shows work, absorbs reactions and
negotiates what happens to it, which is the whole subject of Tom Greever's
*Articulating Design Decisions*
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_04-chapter-3-design-the-meeting]],
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_07-chapter-6-form-a-response]],
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_09-chapter-8-lock-in-agreement]].

Across the sources the hard part is never getting people to talk; it is making
what they say usable. Unstructured discussion produces hypothetical scenarios
unsupported by data, subjective and unactionable comments, and premature
implementation debate [[2021-05-30_derailed-design-critiques]], and even good
feedback loses its value when nobody tells the contributors what happened to it
[[2026-05-22_after-design-critique]]. So the corpus treats critique as
something to be designed on three fronts: the structure of the session, the
designer's own listening and response inside it, and the follow-through after
it ends. Artiom Dashinsky's *The Path to Senior Product Designer* treats the
critique as one instance of a wider skill, feedback, and insists it has two
sides a designer has to be competent at, giving it and receiving it
[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_14-12-feedback-communication]].
[[2026-06-12_ai-era-critique]] pushes the idea past design reviews
altogether: with generative AI systems, where the same input can produce
different outputs, critique stops being a gate-stage review of static work and
becomes the ongoing method by which quality itself is defined.

## Practice

### Structure the session before it starts

- **Give the feedback a frame.** [[2017-07-02_facilitating-design-studio-workshop]]
  separates the critique segment from the sketching that precedes it and runs
  it through a framework such as Six Thinking Hats, guiding discussion through
  focused lenses (logic, emotions, strengths, weaknesses, creativity, process);
  structured critique is more objective and more productive than free-form
  feedback.
- **Say what feedback you need.** [[2021-05-30_derailed-design-critiques]]
  recommends explicit feedback guidelines — user-goal alignment, brand
  consistency, content questions, visual design — set as expectations before
  the session, so stakeholders know what to comment on instead of improvising.
- **Design the meeting itself.**
  [[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_04-chapter-3-design-the-meeting]]
  applies cognitive-load reduction to the meeting the way it would be applied
  to an interface. Open by setting context: state the goal, summarise previous
  decisions, show where you are on the process timeline, specify what feedback
  is needed at this stage, restate the goal. Structure the content with memory
  principles (primacy and recency, repetition, surprise, mixed media) and break
  it into chunks with visual transitions rather than one long presentation.
- **Remove distractions.** The same chapter treats placeholder content — stock
  images, lorem ipsum, misaligned layouts — as capable of derailing an entire
  conversation, and sets a rule for it: "Distractions should only happen once.
  If you go to two meetings and the same distraction comes up both times,
  you're doing it wrong."
- **Prepare the room, not just the deck.** Write down the objections you expect
  (most people react predictably from their role and values), prepare
  alternative designs and supporting data for them, align one-on-one beforehand
  with people who can advocate for the work in the meeting, and practise out
  loud with a printed agenda and a short team huddle
  [[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_04-chapter-3-design-the-meeting]].
- **Involve the team early.** [[2021-05-30_derailed-design-critiques]] traces a
  large share of derailment to exclusion: team members who felt outside the
  design process are the ones who bring pseudoevidence and off-topic ideas into
  the critique. Early involvement builds shared vocabulary and alignment.

### Listen before responding

[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_05-chapter-4-listen-to-understand]]
argues that an articulate response starts with listening, and splits the skill
in two. Implicit listening: let stakeholders talk without interruption (they
clarify and rephrase themselves as they go, and gain confidence they were
heard); hear what is not being said, since "That's interesting" may mean
disagreement; and master the pause, waiting two or three seconds of silence
before answering. Explicit listening: take detailed notes, ask clarifying
questions, and repeat feedback back in design vocabulary — converting "I like"
into "Does this work?" — to build shared language.

Its central move is to separate the suggestion from the problem: stakeholders
propose specific visual changes when the underlying issue is something else, so
the question to ask is "What problem are you trying to solve?" The notes that
come out should be accessible, organised by agenda item, specific about who
said what, definitive about decisions versus open items, actionable with named
owners, linked and forward-looking — and they must record not only what was
decided but why, because without that record there is no paper trail when
opinions shift.

### Get the receiving mindset right

[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_06-chapter-5-get-in-the-right-mindset]]
frames the designer in a critique as a facilitator rather than a defender or a
salesperson: you do not have the final say, and accepting that lack of control
is what frees you to focus on influence. Practical consequences the chapter
draws:

- Prepare stakeholders to represent your work elsewhere — give them the
  vocabulary and confidence to explain the thinking when you are not in the
  room.
- Lead with "yes" to keep the conversation open and reinforce a team mentality;
  it buys time and commits you to nothing, and "yes, but" negates it.
- Build a positive persona: confidence without arrogance, authenticity, humour
  to disarm tension, and orientation toward the stakeholder's needs.
- Change the vocabulary: avoid "you're wrong", "from a design perspective",
  "like / don't like" and jargon, speaking about effectiveness and function
  instead of preference, and adopt the words your stakeholders already use.

The chapter states the ego condition directly: receiving feedback productively
means letting go of ego, recognising the value in other people's ideas, and
looking for the problem behind a suggestion rather than rejecting the
suggestion.

### Form a response

[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_07-chapter-6-form-a-response]]
separates strategy (what to say) from tactics (how to deliver it). The strategy
rests on three questions every design decision should be able to answer: what
problem does it solve, how does it affect the user, why is it better. On top of
that it adds three moves — appeal to a nobler motive by connecting the decision
to an agreed business goal or metric; represent the user by telling the story
of a specific person observed rather than invoking a generic "the user"; and
demonstrate effectiveness by showing visuals, prototypes or side-by-side
comparisons instead of describing them. Its five tactics: show a comparison,
propose an alternative, give a choice while naming what will be lost, ask
others to weigh in, or postpone the decision. Feedback outside the project's
scope is redirected to a backlog item rather than argued with, and fluency in
all of this is presented as a product of practice.

[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_09-chapter-8-lock-in-agreement]]
compresses the whole book into the **IDEAL Response**: Identify the problem,
Define the solution, Empathize with the user, Appeal to the business, Lock
in agreement. The last letter is the one the chapter insists on — ask directly
("Do you agree?") rather than leaving the discussion open, because without a
stated commitment the work remains "just an IDEA" and the decision gets
relitigated in the next meeting. It also draws a distinction the other sources
do not: **support is not consensus**. You will never get everyone to agree on
every detail, but you can get everyone's agreement to move forward, which lets
people voice concerns while still committing to a path. The framework is
offered as a mental checklist to internalise, not a script to read, and the
chapter grounds it in a series of worked case studies.

### Keep the session on track

[[2021-05-30_derailed-design-critiques]] names three recurring derailments and
a tactic for each:

- **Pseudoevidence.** Team members playing devil's advocate raise hypothetical
  scenarios with no user testing or research behind them. Pause, capture every
  scenario on sticky notes, rate them by likelihood, targeted user or persona,
  and business objective, then focus the design on the top one to three.
- **Subjective, vague feedback.** Handled upstream by the feedback guidelines
  above, and downstream by sorting.
- **Off-topic and premature discussion.** Use a parking lot to defer
  out-of-scope items, and a persistent **knowledge board** — unlike a
  temporary parking lot — to log questions, assumptions and hypothetical
  scenarios, tracking what has been tested, what is still unverified and what
  is established fact.

### Decide what to act on

[[2021-05-30_derailed-design-critiques]] sorts feedback after the session into
three buckets: **To Do** (actionable and agreed), **To Persuade** (suggestions
you disagree with), **To Clarify** (needs more context).

[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_11-chapter-10-dealing-with-changes]]
works the same problem from the motives behind a request. Stakeholders ask for
changes because they misunderstood, because their alternative really is better,
because a need of theirs is unmet, because they are being unreasonable, or —
frequently — simply because they want to be heard. Its tools:

- **Parkinson's Law of Triviality**: expect disproportionate energy on trivial,
  visible details, because accessible issues are easier to grasp and comment on
  than complex ones.
- **"Painting a duck", named and rejected**: the trick of including an
  obviously flawed element so criticism lands there, and its Disney ancestor
  "hairy arms", are recounted and then ruled out — designers should not deceive
  or manipulate stakeholders this way. What survives is the observation that the
  fixation is predictable, and better prevented by setting context and fidelity
  up front than by deflecting it.
- **Plan how the change will be absorbed**: the legitimate anticipation is to
  decide in advance how expected changes will be handled, with pre-planned
  "real estate", an extra user study, or a reevaluation of the approach.
- **Make the change a design challenge**: when a change is unavoidable, treat
  it as a new constraint and work with the stakeholder's underlying need toward
  a better solution.
- **The emotional bank account**, a term the chapter takes from Stephen Covey's
  *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People*: every kept promise, met metric and
  follow-up is a deposit, every missed deadline, dropped piece of feedback and
  refused change a withdrawal — refusing a change on the user's behalf costs
  trust even when the refusal was right. Keeping a positive balance is what
  buys the ability to disagree later.
- **Know when you are actually wrong**: three red flags are that the problem
  still exists, that users do not understand the design, or that the majority
  of stakeholders disagree. Admitting the mistake and moving to solutions
  rebuilds trust faster than defensiveness.

The two sources point the same way but weigh it differently, and the difference
is worth noticing: [[2021-05-30_derailed-design-critiques]] answers difficult
input with **process** (guidelines, sticky-note prioritisation, knowledge
boards, parking lots), while
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_11-chapter-10-dealing-with-changes]]
answers it with **relationship** (trust balance, anticipation, making the
person feel heard). Neither rejects the other's remedy.

[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_14-12-feedback-communication]] adds
a third answer, a processing order applied to each piece of feedback before any
of it is acted on. Understand the root cause first, by asking clarifying
questions; then evaluate relevancy against the project's context, its readiness
level, its scope and the original request; then prioritise; then respond, in one
of four registers the chapter names: yes, yes but later, requires research, or
no. Its stated reason for insisting on prioritisation is that without a severity
system designers become overwhelmed, since design "may be the role that has to
deal with the most feedback inside the organization". The methods it offers are
to ask outright how strongly the person feels about it, to listen for severity
phrasing that runs from "it's not critical" to "I think it's critical we…", or
to adopt a tagging system, for which it cites HubSpot's FlashTags (#fyi,
#suggestion, #recommendation, #plea).

### Give feedback, and learn from the feedback you get

The same chapter covers the side of the exchange the rest of the corpus mostly
leaves implicit: being the person who gives the critique. Its structure for
design feedback is to check the comment is valuable and relevant, understand
what led to the current solution before reacting, raise the problem with
evidence, attach a severity status, and offer a solution where that is
appropriate; the clarifying question it suggests is "Could you explain the
reasoning behind…?", precisely so a solution is not proposed before the
designer's intent is understood. For feedback that is not about design, it
recommends describing the behaviour with concrete examples, articulating the
impact, and then either encouraging continuation or, for negative feedback,
positioning a solution as a question, with the conversation opened rather than
sprung: "Can we talk about X?", or "I'd be happy to give you some feedback; do
you have time now?". It reports a study featured in Harvard Business Review
finding that no one likes to provide negative feedback but everyone wants to
hear it.

It also proposes treating the feedback you receive as data about yourself rather
than only about the work: document the feedback on things within your control,
categorise it (oral and written communication, UX skills, strategy, and so on),
and score it by frequency and severity, on the argument that patterns reveal
areas for improvement more reliably than individual incidents. Its other
instrument is the delta, documenting what you thought was the right thing to ship
against what was eventually shipped, which it reads as an indicator of where to
improve (see [[Performance Review]]).

### Close the loop after it ends

[[2026-05-22_after-design-critique]] is the corpus's argument that the session
is only half the practice: feedback "is valuable only if someone closes the
loop on it", and "the followup is where trust actually gets built". Two
follow-up tactics:

1. **An immediate recap** — a brief message listing what is moving forward,
   what is still under investigation, and what will not be pursued. Low effort,
   and it makes contributions feel valued while clarifying next steps.
2. **A deeper follow-up with attribution** — showing before and after, and
   connecting a specific change to the specific feedback that caused it.
   Without that attribution, stakeholders assume they were ignored.

Feedback you did not act on is the part that builds the most credibility, more
than a highlight reel of wins: name the feedback, state the decision, give the
reason, say what happens next. Trigger a follow-up after significant design
changes, before handoff to development, and whenever feedback influenced major
direction — stakeholders should learn the final decision from you, not from a
demo or the live product. The cost of skipping is cumulative rather than
immediate: each missed follow-up makes the next critique slightly less engaged,
because nothing has shown participants that their input mattered.

### When the work being critiqued is an AI system

[[2026-06-12_ai-era-critique]] adapts the practice to probabilistic systems,
where the same input can produce different outputs and exact behaviour cannot
be specified. The designer's task shifts from specifying behaviours to defining
what "good" looks and does not look like, through a **judge-evaluate-iterate
loop**: define judge criteria for acceptable output, evaluate actual output
against them, identify failures, iterate on prompts or training data. Criteria
must be objective enough for consistent judgement across evaluators but
grounded in user research and context rather than arbitrary, since vague
criteria push evaluators back onto subjective judgement. An LLM can serve as
the judge for scale, but only calibrated against human annotations — an F1
score of 0.8 or higher indicates reliable automated evaluation, and human
review remains necessary. The chapter's underlying point is that opacity
persists even after hundreds of iterations, which is precisely why critique,
not specification, becomes the method for shaping behaviour.
