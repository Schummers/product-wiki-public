# Business Impact

## Definition

Business impact is the link between design work and the outcomes an
organisation actually tracks: revenue, cost, risk, speed to market, retention.
The sources treat that link as real but chronically unstated. UX has always
influenced those numbers — when users cannot complete key tasks, support tickets
are created, conversions are missed and customers leave — and the work is to
surface and claim that connection rather than to invent it
[[2026-07-03_reporting-ux-business-outcomes]]. Historically, the field grew
fastest when structural business pressure made experience quality pay: the PC
revolution made the user the buyer, and the web made experience the gatekeeper
to revenue [[2017-12-24_100-years-ux]].

The recurring failure mode is measuring and reporting in a register leadership
does not use: effort instead of impact, UX-internal scores instead of business
metrics, vanity dashboards instead of goals
[[2026-07-03_reporting-ux-business-outcomes]], [[2025-10-03_ux-metrics-goals]].
The recurring remedy is to start from the problem and the business goal rather
than the feature [[2016-08-14_outcomes-vs-features]], to measure the downstream
consequences of experience quality over time
[[2018-05-06_annoying-ads-cost-business]], and to frame improvement work at the
level of journeys and investment strategies the business can decide on
[[2020-10-11_refine-remodel]].

A later strand treats that link as a designer competence rather than a
reporting exercise. Design schools teach visual craft but not how design
affects business, how to optimise a design against a company's KPIs or how to
measure its performance, and the designers who gain influence are the ones who
understand design's value inside the organisation
[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_01-introduction]]. Read that way, the
scope is wider than the product itself: pricing, reviews, branding, marketing,
operations and support decide whether most prospects ever become customers at
all, and they are all design problems
[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_25-bobby-ghoshal-how-designers-should-change-their-mindset]].

Artiom Dashinsky pushes that strand to its conclusion by making impact the unit
in which seniority itself is measured: he states that the main predictor of a
designer's seniority is the impact they have which benefits the organization,
and that a designer's impact inside a company is correlated with how likely
they are to increase revenue or reduce costs
[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_11-9-growing-your-impact]],
[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_19-17-strategy]]. He reads the
field's history the way [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_01-introduction]]
does: years of emphasis on aesthetics, which he calls the "dribbblization of
design", left designers without the business communication, stakeholder
management, ownership, strategic thinking and hiring skills that would change
the perception of design as visual work, and the growth problem he sets out to
solve is moving designers from being seen as UI people to demonstrating value
in fixing business problems
[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_01-why-i-wrote-this-book]].

## Practice

### Start from outcomes, not outputs

- An output is the product created; an outcome is the problem solved. Defining
  the outcome first is what prevents building the wrong thing, however good the
  interface [[2016-08-14_outcomes-vs-features]].
- The five steps proposed: state the problem clearly and who it is for, gather
  user data proving the problem exists, define the core competencies that create
  competitive advantage, set concrete goals with clear success criteria, and
  test multiple ideas with users [[2016-08-14_outcomes-vs-features]].
- Part of stating the problem is stating how solving it helps the business: both
  user and business goals are in scope, and evidence replaces gut feel
  [[2016-08-14_outcomes-vs-features]].
- The same discipline applies to design exercises in hiring: even an open brief
  should serve the company's strategic objectives (growth, revenue, retention)
  rather than showcase craft on a subject with no business value
  [[2025-09-02_393_Case_Study_Design_Mes_3_pires_erreurs_à_ne_pas_reproduire...]].

### Weighing a solution before choosing it

- Assess each candidate solution on four dimensions: reach (how many customers
  it can touch), value for the customer, potential revenue, and implementation
  effort [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_13-step-5-prioritise-and-choose-an-idea]].
- Plot the candidates on an effort/impact matrix, impact being reach plus value
  plus revenue, and aim for the quick-wins area; present the reasoning behind
  each score, especially for the idea you keep, rather than the verdict alone
  [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_13-step-5-prioritise-and-choose-an-idea]].
- Reach is the dimension most easily missed: a solution can genuinely solve a
  user need and still carry low impact because too few people can use it, a
  smartwatch app reaching only smartwatch owners being the example given
  [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_13-step-5-prioritise-and-choose-an-idea]].

### Choose metrics that connect to organisational goals

- Three traps to name and remove: vanity metrics that look good but yield no
  insight, siloed metrics defined by UX alone without stakeholder buy-in, and
  noise metrics nobody uses [[2025-10-03_ux-metrics-goals]].
- Begin any metrics exercise by agreeing where UX actually touches business
  outcomes — revenue, retention, adoption, support costs — before picking a
  single metric [[2025-10-03_ux-metrics-goals]].
- Good metrics reflect usability improvements the team can influence by design,
  are trackable, and are actually used in decisions rather than merely
  documented [[2025-10-03_ux-metrics-goals]].
- [[2025-10-03_ux-metrics-goals]] offers a 3–4 hour cross-functional workshop
  for this: a pre-survey to uncover disconnects, clustering goals and KPIs,
  identifying UX contributions, defining and prioritising metrics, then embedding
  them in existing workflows (sprint reviews, roadmap planning, OKRs). Inherited
  defaults such as CSAT, NPS or SUS adopted without that conversation are part
  of the problem.
- Expect stakeholders to push for easy-to-measure but misaligned metrics; ask
  probing questions, offer alternatives capturing the same intent, and if needed
  test-drive a metric to prove it is better [[2025-10-03_ux-metrics-goals]].
- Metric choice follows the problem statement rather than a standard list: a
  doctor-facing dashboard meant to increase the number of patients seen per day
  is measured by task completion time and patient satisfaction (NPS)
  [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_15-step-7-measure-success-how]]. The
  eight example indicators offered are task success rate, completion time,
  engagement, retention, revenue, conversion, user acquisition and NPS
  [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_15-step-7-measure-success-how]]. The two
  sources agree on the test (does the metric follow from the goal) but not on
  whether a ready-made list helps: [[2025-10-03_ux-metrics-goals]] counts
  defaults such as NPS or CSAT adopted without a cross-functional conversation
  as part of the problem.
- Worked examples run the measurement through to the business outcome instead of
  stopping at the interface. For a self-publishing platform, completion rate,
  step-wise completion and feature retention are proposed because they track
  whether entry barriers fell, and therefore whether Amazon's marketplace supply
  and revenue rose
  [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_19-3-2-a-self-publishing-platform-for-amazon]].
  For a freelancer dashboard, completion and engagement are paired with
  downstream questions: do freelancers earn more, do they stay, do usage and NPS
  rise [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_20-3-3-a-dashboard-for-freelancers]].
- Naming the metric and setting its target are separate jobs: in a design
  exercise the candidate proposes which KPIs matter without threshold numbers,
  and in real product work the final call belongs to a product manager or data
  analyst [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_15-step-7-measure-success-how]].

### Report in the language leadership uses

- Two reporting mistakes dominate: presenting effort ("we conducted 24
  interviews and 3 usability studies"), which reads as resources spent with
  nothing to show, and presenting UX-internal metrics ("SUS improved from 62 to
  74"), which leadership does not track
  [[2026-07-03_reporting-ux-business-outcomes]].
- Leaders evaluate an investment through five questions: does it impact revenue,
  reduce cost, mitigate risk, improve speed to market, improve retention or
  satisfaction? Frame UX results against one of those
  [[2026-07-03_reporting-ux-business-outcomes]].
- Distinguish upstream from downstream metrics: task success rates, error rates
  and SUS say how the design performed; support contact volume, conversion rates
  and churn say what the work was worth. The difference is where in the chain you
  measure [[2026-07-03_reporting-ux-business-outcomes]].
- The translating data usually already exists inside the organisation — partner
  with finance, product analytics, support or marketing to convert before-and-
  after data from redesigned flows into business terms; even directional data
  persuades when it is honest [[2026-07-03_reporting-ux-business-outcomes]].
- Dashinsky gives the same translation as a promotion argument: design drives
  business impact through acquisition, conversion, retention, churn reduction,
  support effort reduction, cost reduction and labour reduction, and he
  recommends documenting each achievement through that lens, as effort, output
  and impact, rather than as work done
  [[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_24-22-getting-promoted]].
- The stakes of not doing this: teams that cannot connect their work to business
  outcomes get categorised as cost centres and cut accordingly
  [[2026-07-03_reporting-ux-business-outcomes]]. Metrics disconnected from
  organisational goals leave UX undervalued and misdirect teams toward local
  optimisation [[2025-10-03_ux-metrics-goals]].

### Measuring the cost of a degraded experience

- A longitudinal study of 35 million Pandora users over 18 months quantified the
  cost of annoyance: users exposed to 38% more ads listened 2.8% fewer hours per
  week, while cutting ad load by 25% raised weekly listening by 1.7%
  [[2018-05-06_annoying-ads-cost-business]].
- The effect compounds. The one-month change was 0.4% and grew to 2.8% after a
  year, so short-window analytics understate the damage and can lead to the
  wrong decision [[2018-05-06_annoying-ads-cost-business]].
- The loss splits across two business levers: 41% of the lost usage came from
  users who left (loyalty) and 59% from reduced use by those who stayed; the
  article expresses this through B = V × C × L, where degraded UX hits both
  conversion and loyalty [[2018-05-06_annoying-ads-cost-business]].

### Framing the investment decision

- Experience performance across whole customer journeys moves satisfaction and
  business outcomes more than optimising individual touchpoints in isolation, so
  journeys are where UX investment should be argued
  [[2020-10-11_refine-remodel]].
- Three strategies differ by investment level, organisational change and
  potential impact: refine (low investment, iterative smoothing of existing
  journeys), remodel (medium, reimagining journeys with operational and
  foundational change), rebuild (high, entirely new experiences on new
  technologies and business models) [[2020-10-11_refine-remodel]].
- Pick between them on organisational readiness: current state, investment
  capacity, commitment to transformation, risk tolerance, unmet needs and
  competitive landscape [[2020-10-11_refine-remodel]].

### Impact as the measure of seniority

- Dashinsky reports Dropbox's definition of what drives seniority: consistency
  (how often you impact the organization), velocity (how large that impact is)
  and accountability (owning the outcome). Because at mature companies impact is
  looked at individually rather than in relation to the team, an argument he
  illustrates with Intercom, he holds that the designer keeps significant
  control over their own impact
  [[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_11-9-growing-your-impact]].
- Growing that impact is not a matter of working more hours but of shifting
  toward higher-return activities. The three-step method proposed: identify what
  an activity impacts (product, work, or people), determine its current level of
  impact (from a task up to a product line for product, from learning up to
  managing for work, from self up to industry for people), then upgrade it to
  the next level
  [[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_11-9-growing-your-impact]].
- The worked contrast: a designer who simply executes the original design brief
  provides less value than one who suggests how to simplify a task so that
  development effort falls while business impact is maintained
  [[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_19-17-strategy]].
- The same reasoning applies to when to stop. A design is often good enough to
  ship before its designer is satisfied with it, and shipping late hurts the
  business more than shipping with imperfect polish, so knowing the business
  case is what lets a designer stop refining with confidence
  [[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_17-15-shipping-ownership]].

### Business literacy as a design skill

- The gap the book opens with: design schools teach visual skills, not the
  cross-functional ones tech companies need, which include how design affects
  business, how to work with engineers, how to present to non-designers, how to
  research customers, how to optimise a design for the business' KPIs and how to
  measure its performance
  [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_01-introduction]].
- Bobby Ghoshal's argument for widening the frame: 90-95% of prospective
  customers never experience the product at all, stopped earlier by pricing,
  reviews, branding, marketing, operations or customer service, so the shift he
  proposes is from "user experience designer" to "customer experience designer"
  [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_25-bobby-ghoshal-how-designers-should-change-their-mindset]].
- What that implies day to day: spend the bulk of your time away from the desk
  building diplomatic bridges with marketing, finance, operations and support,
  and learn the company's business strategy, margins and conversion metrics
  across channels. That, rather than deeper specialisation in visual or motion
  tools, is what earns design relevance and investment
  [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_25-bobby-ghoshal-how-designers-should-change-their-mindset]].
- For designers moving toward founding, Justin Maxwell frames the same literacy
  as big-picture thinking: align the organisation to explicit OKRs, constraints
  and objectives, choose words carefully, spend dollars wisely and triage
  product decisions against stated goals, with user need and principled business
  strategy coming before cosmetic appeal
  [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_26-justin-maxwell-advice-for-designers-who-want-to-become]].
- The closing resource list widens "impact" past business KPIs altogether,
  pointing to sustainable design, product ethics, design's effect on vulnerable
  populations and the role of endings
  [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_30-additional-resources]].
- Dashinsky makes the same literacy concrete and asks for the documents:
  request from your manager the company's internally defined KPIs (revenue per
  customer, conversion rate, host churn are his examples) and its OKRs, learn
  how the company makes money and how it spends money to acquire customers
  (most probably via ads from Google, Facebook, an app store, or sales
  outreach), and read the vision statement, the roadmap and the organizational
  chart. His rationale is that the CEO's primary question about any design is
  how it increases revenue or reduces costs
  [[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_19-17-strategy]].
- The competitive half of the same competency: identify direct competitors and
  track their product updates, launches and business moves using tools such as
  G2, Product Hunt or Google auto-complete, and read both their negative
  reviews (to fix your own problems) and their positive ones (to understand
  what users value). The first business analysed is effortful; it leaves a
  mental framework that makes the next one faster
  [[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_19-17-strategy]].
- Where Ghoshal argues for bridges to other functions, Dashinsky gives the
  translation table those bridges need: product managers value roadmap
  alignment and shipping on time, engineers value clean code and performance,
  salespeople value competitive advantage, and the technique is to pitch an
  idea in terms of the other person's stated interests rather than design
  principles
  [[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_16-14-stakeholder-management-collaboration]].
- Business literacy also changes which career path a designer can aim at:
  business ownership is presented as the path with the highest impact
  potential, and product positions as having a higher promotion ceiling than
  design positions inside organizations
  [[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_03-1-design-career-paths]].
- External advocacy is treated as a business asset too: the chapter notes that
  companies such as Figma and Intercom position themselves as thought leaders
  by publicly sharing their design work and processes, and relays Figma's VP of
  design saying that publishing articles about their internal design process
  brings design candidates to their door
  [[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_23-21-advocacy-community]].
- One cost figure the book puts on the other side of the ledger: citing Gallup,
  it puts the cost of replacing an employee at between 50% and 200% of that
  employee's annual salary depending on seniority, which applied to the
  Glassdoor salary range for product designers it estimates at $37,000 to
  $234,000 per departing designer
  [[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_01-why-i-wrote-this-book]].

### The long-run argument

- UX growth has tracked business incentive, not only cultural recognition:
  100x growth in practitioners from 1950 to 1983, 1,000x from 1983 to 2017 under
  PC and web pressure, and a projected 100x from 2017 to 2050
  [[2017-12-24_100-years-ux]].
- The projected next phase shifts the addressable market from consumer software
  toward augmenting human cognition and knowledge-economy productivity, with
  overall experience quality estimated at under 10% of what it should be
  [[2017-12-24_100-years-ux]].
