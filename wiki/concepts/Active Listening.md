# Active Listening

## Definition

Across this corpus, active listening names a deliberate, learnable skill of
understanding what another person means, not merely registering their words,
whether that person is a design stakeholder or a research participant. In
stakeholder settings, Tom Greever splits it into two components: implicit
listening, which happens without external demonstration and includes letting
the other person talk fully, hearing what is left unsaid, and uncovering the
real problem behind a stated request; and explicit listening, the visible
behaviours (note-taking, clarifying questions, repeating feedback back) that
show the other person they have been heard
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_05-chapter-4-listen-to-understand]].
Its stated purpose in a stakeholder conversation is to ensure understanding
before responding, rather than waiting for a turn to speak
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_05-chapter-4-listen-to-understand]].

*UX Research* treats active listening as a research-facilitation competency
rather than a communication technique: attending to what a participant says
and what they leave unsaid, reading their comfort level in real time, and
adjusting the session accordingly rather than following a fixed script
[[2016-11-04_ux-research_11-chapter-10-improv-in-research]],
[[2016-11-04_ux-research_10-chapter-9-managing-people-during-research]]. Both
books converge on the same underlying claim, applied to different rooms:
listening is not passive reception but an act that determines how well the
listener can act on what they hear, coupled in the research context with
reading nonverbal cues, and coupled in the stakeholder context with
note-taking and rephrasing
[[2016-11-04_ux-research_12-chapter-11-facilitating-research]],
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_05-chapter-4-listen-to-understand]].

## Practice

### Listening to stakeholders (Articulating Design Decisions)

- Listening's purpose is understanding before responding, not merely waiting
  for a turn to speak
  [[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_05-chapter-4-listen-to-understand]].
- **Implicit listening** — let stakeholders talk without interruption: they
  often clarify and rephrase as they go, and build confidence that they were
  heard. Hear what is not being said: subtext and indirect language ("That's
  interesting" can mean disagreement; a sudden drop in enthusiasm signals
  something else going on). Uncover the real problem behind a proposed
  solution by asking "What problem are you trying to solve?", since
  stakeholders often propose a specific visual fix when the underlying issue
  differs. Master the pause: wait two to three seconds after a stakeholder
  finishes, which signals reflection and gives the brain time to form a
  considered response
  [[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_05-chapter-4-listen-to-understand]].
- **Explicit listening** — take detailed, actionable notes (organized by
  agenda item, specific about who said what, clear about decisions versus
  open items); ask clarifying questions; repeat feedback back in design
  vocabulary, converting "I like" into "Does this work?", so both sides end up
  sharing a language
  [[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_05-chapter-4-listen-to-understand]].
- Anticipating what stakeholders will say and letting them talk fully are
  treated as prerequisites for guiding a productive meeting, part of the
  broader practice of designing the meeting itself
  [[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_04-chapter-3-design-the-meeting]].
- Asking good, open-ended personal and work-related questions is presented as
  the primary tool for understanding a stakeholder's perspective and what
  drives their reactions — the same open-ended questioning UX practitioners
  use with users, turned on stakeholders
  [[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_03-chapter-2-stakeholders-are-people-too]].
  The chapter on communicating design work gestures toward this in passing
  too: understanding stakeholder concerns and alternatives requires careful
  observation of feedback, even though its main focus is the designer's own
  speaking
  [[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_02-chapter-1-great-designers-are-great-communicators]].
- After a meeting, filtering feedback for the written follow-up is itself a
  listening judgment: deciding which comments reflect genuine stakeholder
  needs versus casual brainstorming or tangential opinions requires having
  understood stakeholder intent and influence during the conversation, not
  just recorded their words
  [[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_10-chapter-9-follow-up-afterward]].

### Listening to research participants (UX Research)

- Reading a participant's comfort level and detecting shifts in tone and
  thoroughness — for example when responses shrink from thorough explanations
  to one-word answers, or repeat what was already said — are the cues that
  tell a researcher when to keep probing and when to stop, since losing the
  cadence and ease of a conversation makes it very difficult to regain trust
  [[2016-11-04_ux-research_10-chapter-9-managing-people-during-research]].
- Active listening is framed as a core improv principle: focus on what is
  being said rather than on what you plan to say next. In practice this means
  using the discussion guide flexibly to hit its milestones rather than
  following it as a script, so real-time adjustments and richer feedback stay
  possible [[2016-11-04_ux-research_11-chapter-10-improv-in-research]].
- Coupling active listening with observation of body language and
  microexpressions — what the participant says and what they leave unsaid —
  lets a researcher adjust questions in real time and recognize discomfort or
  uncertainty, which helps probe more effectively
  [[2016-11-04_ux-research_12-chapter-11-facilitating-research]].
- In debrief sessions, Bibiana Nunes's sidebar "Voice from the Streets" frames
  giving and receiving feedback as a listening exercise: teams do not want
  justification, they want to be heard and understood, and listening
  carefully to others' stories about a session is what reveals where
  miscommunications or errors occurred
  [[2016-11-04_ux-research_13-chapter-12-debrief-sessions]].
