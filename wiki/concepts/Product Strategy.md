# Product Strategy

## Definition

Product strategy is the high-level approach and set of decisions that guide how a product is developed, positioned, and evolved to achieve both business and user goals [[2024-10-25_product-ux-glossary]]. Across these sources it is consistently defined by what it is *not*: it is not a feature list. The recurring move is to put the outcome — the problem being solved — before the output, the thing being built [[2016-08-14_outcomes-vs-features]]; to state what problems should be solved rather than how they will be built [[2020-07-05_ux-roadmaps]]; and to establish a research-backed understanding of user problems and desired outcomes before deciding whether a given technology is the right answer [[2025-03-28_ai-superpowers]]. Strategy in the French-language corpus is described the same way: choosing your battles and justifying your decisions in order to steer the product roadmap, intrinsically tied to company strategy [[2026-05-05_412_De_Product_Designer_à_CPO_(Chief_Product_Officer),_carrière_produit]]. Christian Crumlish draws the line by role rather than by artifact: defining and driving strategy is what separates the product manager from the project manager, whose core expertise is keeping projects on track, on time and on budget, executing a plan rather than deciding which plan is worth executing [[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_04-chapter-1-what-exactly-does-a-product-manager-do]]. He also traces where the strategic repertoire came from, the role having been assembled out of five archetypes: market sizing and the obsession with product-market fit from marketing, technical depth from engineering, the "Build, Measure, Learn" loop from lean, and problem reframing from design [[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_04-chapter-1-what-exactly-does-a-product-manager-do]].

The second thread is that strategy is where user goals and business viability must both be held. Focusing exclusively on the optimal user experience while ignoring business goals leads to failure; recommendations have to balance both to keep the organization viable [[2017-10-08_ux-lessons]]. That balance is also a matter of vocabulary and credibility: learning the business terms product managers use — revenue models, lifecycle stages, CAC and CLV, OKRs, product-market fit, blue-ocean strategy, SWOT, the business-model canvas — is what lets UX enter strategic conversations on equal footing and position its work as a business driver rather than a compliance requirement [[2024-10-25_product-ux-glossary]].

## Practice

### Outcomes before outputs

An output is the product or service created; an outcome is the problem solved. Defining the outcome first is the way to avoid building the wrong thing, and many products fail because they solve problems nobody cares about [[2016-08-14_outcomes-vs-features]]. The parallel drawn is the classic marketing distinction: a feature is what the product offers, a benefit is what customers actually want. A wonderful interface for the wrong features fails regardless of craft [[2016-08-14_outcomes-vs-features]].

The five steps proposed for making outputs valuable [[2016-08-14_outcomes-vs-features]]:

1. State the problem clearly — for whom it is solved and how it helps the business, as a shared team understanding.
2. Gather user data proving the problem exists, rather than deciding on gut feel and cognitive bias.
3. Define the organization's core competencies, what creates sustainable competitive advantage, instead of chasing trendy or peripheral features.
4. Set concrete goals with clear success criteria.
5. Test multiple ideas with users to identify the winning solution.

### Vision as a marker of strong teams

Marty Cagan, in his foreword to Patton's *User Story Mapping*, frames a
compelling product vision, pursued with genuine passion, as the defining
trait separating strong product teams from weak ones: good teams have a
vision they pursue with "missionary-like passion," while bad teams are
"mercenaries" executing someone else's orders without that commitment
[[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_03-foreword-by-marty-cagan]]. Cagan extends
the same contrast across the whole practice of strategy: good teams draw
ideas from KPIs, direct customer observation and data, while bad teams
passively collect requirements from sales and stakeholders; good teams
rapidly test ideas to find out which are worth building, while bad teams
hold meetings to produce a prioritised roadmap instead of validating
anything; and good teams expect many of their favourite ideas to fail and
expect several iterations before an idea produces the desired outcome, while
bad teams simply build what is on the roadmap and consider the job done once
it ships on schedule [[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_03-foreword-by-marty-cagan]].

### The market side of strategy

Crumlish's chapter on the business of product treats the market itself as a strategic object, and argues that business value and user value are not contradictory: the organization building the product has to reap some of the value generated in order to sustain itself, so the balance is to rake neither too much off the top, which compromises quality, nor too little, which sinks the business and the product with it [[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_08-chapter-5-the-business-of-product-is-business]].

- **Define the target market specifically.** A segment has to be tight enough to be actionable, his example being "adults in the US aged 18–35 thinking about buying their first new car", because that specificity is what lets you craft experiences that reach it [[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_08-chapter-5-the-business-of-product-is-business]].
- **Distrust the "mere 1%" argument.** Reaching a million customers out of a hundred million is "not significantly easier (or even necessarily easier at all)" than reaching a million out of four million. The ease is an illusion produced by the size of the denominator [[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_08-chapter-5-the-business-of-product-is-business]].
- **Go-to-market is orchestration, not a launch event.** It promotes awareness, enables discovery, communicates the customer as the hero of their journey, coordinates with partners, produces marketing materials, sets pricing, and aligns personnel and budgets [[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_08-chapter-5-the-business-of-product-is-business]].
- **Know whether you launch or optimize.** Some product managers thrive launching into the unknown, others at molding and improving what already exists, and the two demand different energies [[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_08-chapter-5-the-business-of-product-is-business]]. Hà Phan, product leader and general manager at Pluralsight, reframes the choice rather than accepting it: what looks like always starting new things is really building a platform or system, an inherently long game in which you are always starting new things and always optimizing old ones at the same time [[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_08-chapter-5-the-business-of-product-is-business]].

### Strategy as differentiation, not feature parity

Disruption in these sources comes from unmet needs and end-to-end experience, not from feature competition. The three services analysed (Rent the Runway, Airbnb, Uber) share usefulness — answering an unmet need — usability, and an overall experience that extends beyond digital touchpoints [[2017-02-19_3-user-experiences-reshaping-industries]]. Elements of that strategic advantage include two-way rating systems that create accountability and reduce perceived risk in peer-to-peer transactions; backend operations that improve the frontend experience, as with demand forecasting that lowers the odds a shopper ever sees an out-of-stock indicator; omnichannel consistency that widens access; and contextual information delivered at the moment of decision, such as upfront pricing and wait times before commitment [[2017-02-19_3-user-experiences-reshaping-industries]].

### Product-led growth

Product-led growth is a strategy in which customers discover the product's value by using it, rather than through sales representatives or contracts — a freemium or free-trial model that lowers sales cost and delivers value fast, but requires acquisition and retention at scale [[2023-05-21_product-led-growth-ux]]. What that implies operationally:

- The first experience is decisive: users judge quickly whether to proceed, and without a fast demonstration of value they leave for competitors.
- Connect UX work to product-led metrics — time to value, conversion rate, retention, churn, customer-acquisition cost.
- Reduce friction at acquisition: minimize onboarding tutorials and login barriers so the benefit lands before payment.
- Separate perception from reality: users may not recognize value that is actually delivered, so research should test both delivery and communication.
- Apply the Pareto Principle: roughly 20% of customers generate 80% of revenue, so invest heavily in the converted segment.
- Monitor churn by analysing usage patterns, identifying at-risk segments, and promoting the features that engaged users adopt early [[2023-05-21_product-led-growth-ux]].

### Roadmaps as the strategic artifact

A UX roadmap is a strategic, living artifact that aligns, prioritizes, and communicates a team's future work and the problems it intends to solve. It answers "what problems should we solve?", not "how will we build it?", and is not a release plan, a Kanban board, a product backlog, or a journey map [[2020-07-05_ux-roadmaps]]. Structure it by time horizon — Now (well-defined, in progress), Next (near future), Future (six months or more, more ambiguous) — and by theme. Each theme names the beneficiary and their need, the business outcome, and the ownership and type of work: research, design, strategy [[2020-07-05_ux-roadmaps]]. Optional secondary components add context: product areas for complex products, subthemes, confidence estimates based on research validation, and disclaimers for risks or requirements [[2020-07-05_ux-roadmaps]].

Crumlish adds the upward connection: the roadmap is where team goals ladder up to larger organizational strategy, often expressed through OKRs, with different levels of the organization owning different pieces of it. What it carries is therefore desired outcomes and results, "double retention" or "support user collaboration", organized into themes that connect back to company strategy, rather than features or solutions [[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_13-chapter-10-roadmaps-and-how-to-say-no]].

The diagnostic test is blunt: if you are transcribing a feature list into a timeline, you are building a release plan, not a roadmap — roadmaps should reflect user problems discovered through research, not accumulated feature requests [[2020-07-05_ux-roadmaps]].

### Prioritization methods

Prioritization is how strategy becomes sequence, and it should rest on objective criteria rather than subjective opinion. Five methods, each suited to a different context [[2021-11-14_prioritization-methods]]:

- **Impact-effort matrix** — plots relative user value against implementation complexity, sorting items into quick wins, big bets, money pits, and fill-ins. Good for quick, democratic, team-wide prioritization.
- **Feasibility-desirability-viability scorecard** — scores each item on three criteria and sums them; highly customizable, criteria can be added or swapped for organizational priorities.
- **RICE** — reach × impact × confidence, divided by effort. Quantitative and formula-based, suited to technical, data-comfortable teams with many items to rank.
- **MoSCoW** — clusters items into Must Have, Should Have, Could Have, Will Not Have through weighted voting and discussion. Works best with clearly defined time horizons.
- **Kano model** — plots functionality against customer satisfaction to classify items as Attractive, Performance, Indifferent, or Must-be. Useful when a team struggles to hold user needs against business pressure.

No method is inherently better; choose on project context, team culture, and success criteria, and remember that the best method is the one everyone including stakeholders buys into [[2021-11-14_prioritization-methods]]. These methods apply not only to features but to research questions, user segments, ideas, and tasks [[2021-11-14_prioritization-methods]].

### Deciding whether to build at all

Two sources make the same argument in different technological settings: capability is not justification.

For smartwatches, companion apps are a dangerous opportunity for feature creep because a companion app is easier to justify than a new app from scratch — yet in a study of over 200 submitted interactions across five days, more than 80% were with native applications like messages and timers, and third-party apps struggle for traction [[2023-12-01_smartwatch-app]]. Value comes from context and from the specific advantages of a wearable (proximity to the body, hands-free use, quick access), not from replicating phone functionality. The strategic recommendation is often to prioritize effective notifications over a standalone app, and to do adequate discovery work before committing resources [[2023-12-01_smartwatch-app]].

For AI, the rule is that the problem comes before the technology: establish a research-backed understanding of user problems and desired outcomes, then ask whether generative AI is the right solution for that specific problem [[2025-03-28_ai-superpowers]]. The four areas where it currently delivers genuine value are content creation, text summarization, basic data analysis, and perspective taking; adopting AI without validating its relevance to user outcomes produces useless features regardless of technical sophistication [[2025-03-28_ai-superpowers]]. The framing question offered is whether the technology makes the user more likely to engage in the specific behaviour that leads to the desired outcome and creates positive business impact [[2025-03-28_ai-superpowers]].

### The strategic posture of the designer

UX should move beyond reacting to design requests: shape the product vision, take part in strategic discussions, and use data to evangelize across the organization, since it is very difficult to argue with data [[2017-10-08_ux-lessons]]. Systemic change takes years depending on the organization's UX maturity, so trust is earned incrementally through small visible wins and by letting teams experience new approaches firsthand [[2017-10-08_ux-lessons]].

The career account from the French corpus describes the same shift taken further: design as a means and a prism rather than an end, in service of deploying overall product strategy, with problem-solving and value creation as the real objective [[2026-05-05_412_De_Product_Designer_à_CPO_(Chief_Product_Officer),_carrière_produit]]. Moving toward Head of Product or CPO means leaving the world of intuition for the factual — being data-oriented, measuring one's actions, adopting a return-on-investment logic — and taking an interest in brand positioning, company decisions, and overall business stakes rather than staying inside the design domain [[2026-05-05_412_De_Product_Designer_à_CPO_(Chief_Product_Officer),_carrière_produit]]. Its advice on ambition runs the other way from title-chasing: rather than aiming at a title ten years out, find what motivates you day to day; in hiring, the value is on pragmatic designers who can concretely produce and collaborate with engineering, particularly in the AI era [[2026-05-05_412_De_Product_Designer_à_CPO_(Chief_Product_Officer),_carrière_produit]].

### The shared vocabulary

To operate at this level, the glossary source lists what UX professionals need to be fluent in [[2024-10-25_product-ux-glossary]]: revenue models (subscription, freemium, advertisement-based) and how they shape strategy; the product lifecycle stages (introduction, growth, maturity, decline), each with different strategic priorities and design challenges; the metrics that measure value creation, notably customer acquisition cost, customer lifetime value, and their ratio, which reveals whether a segment is profitable; product-market fit, reached when the product delivers ongoing value to both users and business, and achieved through iterative refinement on user feedback; strategic frameworks (blue-ocean strategy, SWOT, business-model canvas); and the organizational practices that make execution possible — OKRs to align teams on measurable outcomes, RACI to clarify roles, feature flags for safe experimentation, hypothesis-driven development to reduce risk.
