# Equity Ownership

## Definition

Equity ownership, as the two books in this corpus use the term, is the share of a company that a venture studio keeps in exchange for having built it. A studio is not a financier that arrives after the company exists: it generates the idea, validates it, builds the first product, recruits the founding team and provides the first capital, and for that work it receives co-founding shares priced at nearly zero at issuance ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_02-part-1-venture-studio-basics]]). Both books treat that cheap founding equity as the core of the studio's economic model, and both acknowledge the same tension it creates: a stake large enough to justify the studio's front-loaded costs can leave founders with too little, alarm later investors, and dilute heavily as rounds are raised ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_03-startup-studios-in-a-nutshell]], [[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_14-equity-ownership]]).

The books diverge on the size of that stake. Szigeti's practitioner playbook (2019) describes studios holding 50% or more, justified by the studio acting as an active co-founder rather than a mentor ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_03-startup-studios-in-a-nutshell]]). Kannan and Peterman's research project (2022), built on interviews with 19 studios, documents a market-wide move away from majority ownership toward a 25 to 40% range, with only 4 of 19 studios still taking a majority ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_14-equity-ownership]]). The corpus also uses the concept in a second, narrower sense: equity as a compensation instrument for the studio's own team, through option plans or continuous co-ownership schemes, when a studio cannot match VC-backed salaries ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_08-building-a-100-co-owned-venture-builder]], [[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_11-ahead-of-time]]).

## Practice

### Why a studio takes founding equity

The rationale is the same in both books: the studio does co-founder work, so it takes co-founder shares. Szigeti frames it as the source of the studio's higher return on investment, since larger stakes produce substantially larger exit returns than the minority positions traditional VCs hold ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_03-startup-studios-in-a-nutshell]]). Kannan and Peterman make the same point from the investor's side: compared with venture capital, which gets access to a company after its creation and some development, studio equity is priced at nearly $0 at issuance, which is what gives the model its upside ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_02-part-1-venture-studio-basics]]). In their strategy chapter they put it more sharply: the studio model is premised on generating returns by receiving cheap initial ownership, not by writing large checks ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_05-strategy]]).

The comparison with other support models makes the scale explicit. Accelerators take 5 to 10% for a 3 to 6 month programme, incubators 5 to 15%, and both work with companies that already exist. Studios build the company from the ground floor and take substantially higher founding stakes, which positions them closer to ownership and control than either ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_03-comparison-to-other-models]]).

From the entrepreneur's side, the deal is a trade: founding equity and early-stage autonomy in exchange for capital, ideation, operational support and de-risking. Kannan and Peterman note that founders may think of the studio as a non-technical co-founder and grant it co-founder level shares, but that the services being paid for are delivered in pre-Seed and often stop being available later in the company's life. Whether the price is acceptable depends on the founder's alternatives; if the alternative was not starting a company at all, it may be more than acceptable ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_23-implications-for-stakeholders]]).

### How much: the two books give different numbers

- **Startup Studio Playbook (2019)**: studios typically hold 50%+ of their portfolio companies. Szigeti lists "capitalization table skepticism" among the model's key risks, but treats the majority stake as the norm, justified by the studio's ongoing contribution ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_03-startup-studios-in-a-nutshell]]).
- **Venture Studios Demystified (2022)**: historically most studios pursued majority ownership (>50%), and those studios struggled: the high stake prevented them from securing top founding talent, and the non-traditional cap table confused potential VC investors. The typical stake is now 25 to 40%, and only 4 of 19 interviewed studios take a majority ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_14-equity-ownership]]). The same book quotes founding stakes of roughly 30 to 45% in its strategy chapter ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_05-strategy]]), 35%+ for studios running structured founder programmes or limited EIR positions ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_08-working-with-founders]]), and a 30% pre-seed assumption in its financial model ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_18-equity-ownership]]).

Read together, the two books describe a shift over time rather than a contradiction: the 2019 playbook records the majority-stake era, and the 2022 study records the retreat from it and the reasons. The corpus does not offer a reconciled single number.

### Variables that move the split, venture by venture

Kannan and Peterman found that few studios keep a standard split across their portfolio. Stakes vary with whether the studio generated the idea, the founder's experience, any capital the founder contributes, salary arrangements, and the founder's role. Within a single studio's portfolio, real splits can span 5 to 50%, which makes standard founder compensation rare and negotiations complex ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_14-equity-ownership]]).

Two specific patterns from the founder chapter:

- **Earlier founder entry means more founder equity, and better outcomes.** Studios saw stronger results when a founding team or CEO joined earlier, even when that meant granting them a higher founding stake. Bringing a leader in late, onto a developed concept, reduces their personal investment and their ability to "put a stamp on the business." The book reads this as studios accepting higher dilution in exchange for better venture outcomes ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_08-working-with-founders]]).
- **Lighter-touch models at lower equity.** Alongside the 35%+ stakes of full co-founding, some studios are exploring roughly 10% equity models to support more ventures without full co-founder involvement ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_08-working-with-founders]]).

### The founder-motivation and cap-table objection

Both books report the same objection from investors: if the studio holds a majority early on, the CEO and team lack skin in the game. Szigeti's advice is to show investors the post-seed cap table adjustments that raise team ownership, and to let the venture's CEO speak to investors directly rather than have the studio front every conversation ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_20-fundraising-and-investor-relations]]). He also lists founder motivation and concerns about "founder authenticity" among the model's structural challenges ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_03-startup-studios-in-a-nutshell]]).

Kannan and Peterman treat the same objection as the force that lowered stakes industry-wide: excessive studio equity both deterred talent and confused VCs, and studios had to move to levels founders and investors would accept ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_14-equity-ownership]]). They add a forward-looking version: as studios multiply and compete for top-tier EIRs and founders, splits will need to be in line with industry averages, and founding ownership will likely decline toward a floor, with established studios keeping a premium above the average ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_18-equity-ownership]]).

### Dilution through funding rounds

The second half of the equity story is what happens after founding. A studio that starts at 30 to 45% is diluted as VCs and new employees take their pieces, and at exit its stake can be as low as 5%, which caps absolute returns despite the cheap entry ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_05-strategy]]). The book's model tracks a cap table from pre-seed through Series D: 30% at pre-seed, 19% at Seed, 15% at Series A, 12% at Series B, 9% at Series C, 8% at Series D. Round size is a sensitive input: a 10% increase in round sizes across all stages cuts the studio's exit return from $22M to $19M per company and leaves the cash balance 17% lower by year 10; a 20% increase drops exits to $16M and the balance 31% lower ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_18-equity-ownership]]).

The response most studios have adopted is to raise their own investment fund and use it to buy their pro-rata allocation in later rounds. This keeps a target stake (often 20%) through Series A and B, at the price of paying higher valuations, and produces significantly higher absolute returns at exit, potentially $22M per exit ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_05-strategy]]). Studios with large funds can target 20 to 35% maintained through Series A and B; most lack the capital for full participation and see ownership dilute ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_14-equity-ownership]]). A fund that invests in preferred equity at cheap valuations across several rounds lets the studio hold and even increase its position ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_17-raising-a-fund]]). The fundraising mechanics are on [[Studio Fundraising]].

### Ownership floors: how low a stake can go

Kannan and Peterman model the point at which the equity stake stops supporting the studio. Their conclusions, all for a studio without a separate fund:

- At **10% founding equity**, the studio has enough to operate but not enough to return money to investors, which makes the model unsustainable.
- At **8%**, the break-even point, dilution takes the studio to 1% by Series D and each exit brings in only $3.6M, just covering ongoing costs.
- A studio with no fund therefore needs at least 10% founding equity to justify the model, and the authors expect competitive pressure to push stakes toward that 10% floor over time ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_18-equity-ownership]]).

In the book's overall model, equity ownership is one of four levers on studio economics and is rated high-impact alongside raising a fund and exit timing ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_15-model-overview]]). The wider model lives on [[Studio Economics]].

### Where the equity sits: common versus preferred

In the dual-entity structure that most interviewed studios have migrated to, ownership is split by type. The operating company does the pre-Seed development work and receives common founder shares for it; the separate investment fund invests cash in Seed and later rounds and holds preferred equity. Studio partners can therefore participate in both streams, founder equity through the operating company and carry plus management fees through the fund, which the authors describe as "double dipping" and as potentially more lucrative than traditional VC fund management when the studio succeeds. Most studios only bring in an external VC to lead at Series A, preserving cheap founder equity for the studio until the market has validated the company; a few bring external capital in earlier to signal validation ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_11-studio-structure]]). The structure itself is covered on [[Studio Structure]].

### Equity as compensation inside the studio

Two founder stories in the Startup Studio Playbook use equity to solve a different problem: retaining talent when the studio cannot match VC-backed salaries.

**Lab.Coop's continuous co-ownership.** After Brickflow ran out of funding, its team founded Lab.Coop as a 100% co-owned venture builder. Every team member is a co-owner, and equity is not a one-time grant: it evolves through a formula tracking tenure, complexity of work and peer-recognised skills (the Badge-Based Compensation Scheme). Any partner can propose new badges or badge valuations based on internal or market wage tensions, and badge sets map to compensation tiers, so wage evolution is bottom-up. Salary, common stock and preferred stock are all regulated by transparent formulas rather than individual negotiation. The founders' stated aim was to split not only profits but participation in high-level decisions, which is why co-ownership is paired with holacracy-based governance ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_08-building-a-100-co-owned-venture-builder]]).

**Laicos's stock option plan.** Ryan Negri set up an employee stock option plan so his Tampa studio could compete with VC-backed startups for developers and designers. The process required an attorney to set a strike price through a company valuation, define vesting schedules and exercise terms, and handle the tax implications. Individual grants were set on objective criteria, time at the company, skills and perceived future value, which removed ambiguity and any perception of favouritism. Options were handed out in a symbolic "piece of the pie" ceremony, which the chapter credits with shifting the team's psychology from salaried employees to co-owners. Without equity, competing for top developers was impossible ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_11-ahead-of-time]]).

Both stories converge on transparency: formula-based or criteria-based allocation, disclosed openly, is what makes equity work as a motivator rather than a source of disputes. The broader compensation picture is on [[Team Compensation]].

### What the corpus does not settle

- The books do not agree on a recommended stake, and the 2022 study reports a 5 to 50% spread inside individual portfolios, so no single figure can be read as "standard".
- Kannan and Peterman state that their financial model is simplified and that the thresholds (10%, 8%) apply to a studio without a fund; the corpus gives no equivalent floor for a fund-backed studio.
- The competitive prediction, that stakes drift toward a floor as VCs and studios compete for founders, is stated as an expectation, not an observation ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_18-equity-ownership]]).
