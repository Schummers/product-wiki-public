# Business Metrics

## Definition

_Partially written: what follows covers only *Product Management for UX People*; the other sources listed below have not yet been folded into this prose._

Business metrics, in Christian Crumlish's account, are the quantitative signals a product manager reads daily to judge product health and set priorities: North Star metrics, daily active users, and the rest of the dashboard. He extends the UX analogy about materials to make the point: as a potter develops a feel for clay and a UX practitioner for digital materials, the product manager's material is data, and it requires "deep immersion for sustained periods". What that immersion buys is "very sensitive antennae" that react fast when the morning North Star report shows an unexpected dip in daily active users or a spike in sales ([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_05-chapter-2-do-you-want-to-be-a-product-manager]]). The same metrics reappear in the analytics chapter as the signals tracked daily to monitor health and guide prioritisation ([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_09-chapter-6-product-analytics-growth-engagement-retention]]).

## Practice

### Does a product manager have to love numbers? The book disagrees with itself

[[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_05-chapter-2-do-you-want-to-be-a-product-manager]] poses three screening questions to a UX practitioner considering the move to product: do you love spreadsheets, are you a good writer, are you deeply curious about interpersonal dynamics. Crumlish then states that you "really do have to like numbers, math, analysis, spreadsheets, and databases to be a great product manager" — and his own sidebar immediately contradicts him, twice, with both objections left standing:

- **Clement Kao** (principal PM at Blend, cofounder of Product Manager HQ) says truly amazing B2B product managers can get away without numbers, because they are so deeply locked in with each customer's priorities. He adds that there is no escaping numbers in B2C.
- **Matt LeMay** (Sudden Compass) says the claim is "sometimes but not always true": "I don't like any of those things ('numbers, math, analysis, spreadsheet, or databases'), but I have the three qualities you described earlier."

The page keeps all three positions rather than picking one. What survives across them is the object, not the affection: the metrics still have to be read, whether or not the reader enjoys them.
