# Journey Mapping

## Definition

A journey map is a visualization of the process a person goes through in order
to accomplish a goal [[2018-12-09_journey-mapping-101]]
[[2021-09-26_ux-mapping-methods-study-guide]]. Mechanically, the method starts
by compiling a series of user goals and actions into a timeline skeleton, then
fleshes that skeleton out with the user's thoughts and emotions to create a
narrative, and finally condenses the narrative into a visualization used to
communicate insights that inform design
[[2016-07-31_customer-journey-mapping]]. The map is organised around five
components: an actor (a persona), a scenario with expectations, high-level
journey phases, the actions, mindsets and emotions inside each phase, and a set
of opportunities [[2018-12-09_journey-mapping-101]]. Read as zones, the same
structure is a lens (persona and scenario), a mapped experience (phases,
actions, thoughts, emotions), and an insights zone (discoveries, opportunities,
ownership, metrics) [[2016-07-31_customer-journey-mapping]]
[[2016-10-16_journey-mapping-ux-practitioners]].

Its purpose is as much organisational as analytical. Journey mapping produces a
holistic view of the customer experience, uncovering moments of frustration and
delight across a series of interactions, and in doing so counters the
fragmented understanding that is chronic in organisations where KPIs are
assigned per department [[2016-07-31_customer-journey-mapping]]
[[2018-12-09_journey-mapping-101]]. Surveyed practitioners rate education about
user pain points and internal alignment as the outcomes journey mapping
achieves best [[2021-12-19_journey-mapping-impact]], and several sources
converge on the point that the practice — the shared act of building the map —
matters more than the artifact it leaves behind
[[2019-04-07_journey-mapping-faq]] [[2020-05-31_journey-map-digital-template]]
[[2016-07-31_customer-journey-mapping]]. Journey mapping sits inside a family of
UX mapping methods, distinct from but adjacent to experience maps, service
blueprints, ecosystem maps, process maps and asset maps
[[2021-09-26_ux-mapping-methods-study-guide]] [[2022-01-16_mapping-in-discovery]].

## Practice

### Scope: one actor, one scenario

Every source that addresses scope says to narrow it. A map should focus on a
single persona in a single scenario; different user types or scenarios need
separate maps [[2018-12-09_journey-mapping-101]]
[[2019-04-07_journey-mapping-faq]]. Unclear scope is the single largest source
of practitioner frustration: 52% of frustrations centre on unclear scope and
process, and 36% of failures are attributed to a lack of clear focus
[[2016-10-16_journey-mapping-ux-practitioners]]. Scoping means answering "whose
experience?" (a critical persona) and "what experience?" (a prioritised
journey), with the team sharing a mutual understanding of the boundaries
[[2017-05-28_customer-journey-mapping-process]]. Practice is looser than the
advice: 71% of surveyed practitioners map end-to-end customer journeys or
active product usage, while support experiences get the least attention (26%)
[[2023-01-08_journey-mapping-how]]. Personas are usually pre-existing (49%),
though 27% develop them during research and 14% during workshops, and 62%
identify the journey phases during research or workshops rather than at project
start [[2023-01-08_journey-mapping-how]]. Phases themselves should be
determined by research and organisational data rather than guessed
[[2018-12-09_journey-mapping-101]].

### Always attach the map to a business goal

Maps created without alignment to a business goal will not produce applicable
insight, so the goal must be established before the process begins
[[2016-07-31_customer-journey-mapping]]. Typical strategic goals include
shifting the organisation's perspective from inside-out to outside-in, breaking
down departmental silos, assigning ownership of touchpoints, targeting specific
customers, and explaining why quantitative data shows a particular pattern
[[2016-07-31_customer-journey-mapping]]. In practice, maps are used to evaluate
existing products or services (89%), to envision future states (73%), and to
explore new potential products or services (61%)
[[2023-01-08_journey-mapping-how]]; mapping both current-state and future-state
journeys is what reveals the gaps between them
[[2019-04-07_journey-mapping-faq]].

### A five-phase process

One source lays out a standardised sequence: (1) Aspiration and Allies — build
a cross-disciplinary team with decision-making authority and define scope;
(2) Internal Investigation — mine existing organisational data and interview
stakeholders across silos; (3) Assumption Formulation — synthesise internal
research into a draft hypothesis map in workshop sessions; (4) External
Research — use the hypothesis map to identify gaps and shape qualitative
customer research; (5) Narrative Visualization — evolve the draft with the core
team and polish only if stakeholder sharing requires it
[[2017-05-28_customer-journey-mapping-process]]. The same source notes that 6–8
research participants are enough to start, and that stakeholders should stay
involved throughout so the findings do not surprise them
[[2017-05-28_customer-journey-mapping-process]]. Mapping does not have to
happen in one sitting: during discovery it should be an ongoing process, kept
low-fidelity (flipchart paper and stickies) precisely to signal that the map
will change as the team learns, and digitised only at the end
[[2022-01-16_mapping-in-discovery]].

### Research: grounded in truth, not assumptions

Journey maps must result from actual research; quantitative data can support
but cannot replace qualitative research and user observation
[[2016-07-31_customer-journey-mapping]]. Assumption-based maps lack credibility
and risk driving poor design decisions, because most stakeholders have neither
a broad enough view of the whole journey nor a deep enough view of user needs
at each stage [[2019-02-10_research-journey-mapping]]. A quarter of reported
failures come from maps based on assumptions rather than data
[[2016-10-16_journey-mapping-ux-practitioners]]. The recommended approach is
multipronged: audit existing internal data first (past focus groups, support
call logs, analytics, satisfaction scores), then combine interviews, field
studies, diary studies and competitive analysis, and finally supplement with
quantitative data (surveys, analytics, satisfaction metrics, social sentiment)
to demonstrate the frequency and magnitude of the issues found
[[2019-02-10_research-journey-mapping]]. Interviews capture stories and
context, but because what people say they do is not always what they do, field
studies are needed to verify actual behaviour
[[2019-02-10_research-journey-mapping]]. Surveyed practice matches this only
partly: 86% use interviews for external research and 76% for stakeholder
research, while diary studies remain underused (12% external, 6% internal)
despite being well suited to journeys that unfold over time
[[2023-01-08_journey-mapping-how]].

**Where the sources disagree.** The 2016–2019 guidance is emphatic that maps
must be research-first [[2016-07-31_customer-journey-mapping]]
[[2019-02-10_research-journey-mapping]], and the five-phase process places the
hypothesis map *after* internal investigation and *before* external validation
[[2017-05-28_customer-journey-mapping-process]]. Later survey work reports that
62% of practitioners begin with a workshop-based hypothesis-first approach
using existing team knowledge while only 35% conduct upfront research, and
treats both as valid depending on context and goals
[[2023-01-08_journey-mapping-how]]. A separate finding cuts against the usual
justification for skipping research: hypothesis-first teams reported spending as
much or more total time than research-first teams, suggesting upfront research
investment produces efficiency gains rather than costs
[[2022-12-18_journey-map-how-much-time]].

### Do not skip the insights zone

Most practitioners include the lens and the experience zones but omit the
insights zone — opportunities, ownership, and metrics — which is the section
that turns a narrative into an action plan; without ownership there is no
accountability, and without metrics improvement cannot be measured
[[2016-10-16_journey-mapping-ux-practitioners]]
[[2018-12-09_journey-mapping-101]]. Maps that do not lead to action fail: 27%
of reported success depends on actually implementing the recommendations, and
maps that sit unchanged on a shelf are described as "just a pretty map that no
one uses" [[2016-10-16_journey-mapping-ux-practitioners]].

### Analysing a finished map

Seven analytical lenses turn a descriptive map into prioritised improvements:
expectation mismatches (where prior experience sets an expectation the
interaction does not meet), unnecessary touchpoints that can be removed to
lower interaction cost, low-point friction (the dips in the emotional journey,
which matter disproportionately because of the peak-end rule), high-friction
channel or device transitions, time evaluation (annotating stages with duration
to see whether the timing is appropriate), moments of truth where the whole
experience hinges on a single interaction, and high points where expectations
are met or exceeded and which can be amplified or recreated elsewhere
[[2020-03-22_analyze-customer-journey-map]]. Mapping also exposes roadblocks,
triggers and missing next-step instructions where users hit friction moving
across channels, making the improvements targetable
[[2017-03-19_seamless-cross-channel]]. Highs and lows analysis is built into at
least one published template [[2020-05-31_journey-map-digital-template]].

### Collaboration and stakeholder buy-in

The activity of creating the map across departments is often more valuable than
the artifact, and its greatest reported benefit is growing cross-department
consensus and a shared vision [[2016-07-31_customer-journey-mapping]]
[[2016-10-16_journey-mapping-ux-practitioners]]. The process compels teams to
establish a shared mental model of the user experience
[[2018-12-09_journey-mapping-101]] [[2019-04-07_journey-mapping-faq]].
Collaborative creation is the norm: 64% of respondents created their last map
with a team, and those who did reported higher success for creating internal
alignment, plausibly because a team that co-creates a map is more likely to
trust its insights [[2021-12-19_journey-mapping-impact]]
[[2023-01-08_journey-mapping-how]]. Cross-functional participation typically
involves product (70%), marketing (46%) and customer service (42%)
[[2023-01-08_journey-mapping-how]]. Of eight critical success factors ranked by
practitioners, involving customers and involving cross-functional teams ranked
highest, ahead of selecting the right journey and the right persona or segment
[[2021-12-19_journey-mapping-impact]]. Buy-in should be secured early —
stakeholders' knowledge also helps focus the research — and maintained
throughout [[2019-04-07_journey-mapping-faq]]
[[2017-05-28_customer-journey-mapping-process]]; a lack of organisational trust
or buy-in accounts for 11% of reported failures
[[2016-10-16_journey-mapping-ux-practitioners]]. Journey mapping will
frequently reveal gaps and opportunities that lie beyond the authority of the
UX professional running the project, which is why allies matter
[[2017-05-28_customer-journey-mapping-process]].

### Tools, fidelity and visual design

Digital tools for distributed teams come at three levels: Level 1 for
visualization and sharing only (Canvanizer, OmniGraffle, Gliffy, Lucidchart),
Level 2 for real-time simultaneous collaboration (Mural, Miro, Smaply,
UXPressia), and Level 3 for integrating personas, metrics and voice-of-customer
data (Touchpoint Dashboard, IBM Journey Designer, CFN Insight); team size and
data-integration needs drive the choice
[[2017-02-05_remote-customer-journey-mapping]]. A spreadsheet template is
offered as a mid-fidelity alternative that needs no special software or
accounts, requires no technical onboarding, passes most organisations'
data-protection policies, and raises team ownership by using a familiar
interface [[2020-05-31_journey-map-digital-template]]. Whatever the format, the
map should be treated as a living document — version numbers and dates signal
that it will change — since a physical, static artifact leads people to
misread the result of a mapping effort as "final"
[[2020-05-31_journey-map-digital-template]]
[[2017-02-05_remote-customer-journey-mapping]].

On tools and polish the sources are not aligned. One argues that the right
tools reduce journey-mapping failure modes and increase the chances of a
favourable outcome [[2017-02-05_remote-customer-journey-mapping]]; the survey
found 56% used digital tools and 44% tangible ones like sticky notes, with
similar success ratings regardless of tool choice, and ranked visual design
*lowest* of eight critical success factors
[[2021-12-19_journey-mapping-impact]]. The visual-design guidance is compatible
but conditional: invest in polish when the map has a large or public-facing
audience or high stakes requiring stakeholder buy-in, and then do it
systematically — establish a visual system (header, primary and secondary text,
arrows, labels) before adding content, pick a palette of three to six colours
used consistently across related maps, lay a foundation of title, owner and
date plus structural lines, align and distribute elements evenly, use a
coherent icon set with a legend, and test and iterate on the map as you would
an interface [[2022-12-04_ux-mapping-methods-visual-design-guide]]. The blunt
version: do not make a map pretty for the sake of it
[[2022-12-04_ux-mapping-methods-visual-design-guide]]. The earlier warning is
the same one from the other direction — resist creating beautiful graphics
before data synthesis is complete [[2016-07-31_customer-journey-mapping]].

### Time and effort

A survey of 343 practitioners puts the mean total investment at 73.8 hours,
just under two full working weeks, spread across building buy-in, collecting
internal stakeholder data, external research, synthesis and artifact creation.
External research (20.6 hours) and synthesis (16.4 hours) consume the most;
securing resources the least (8.9 hours). In-house practitioners reported 79.8
hours against 71.7 for agency practitioners (agencies spending more of it on
the artifact itself), and companies with 500+ employees 87 hours against 64.9
for smaller ones, reflecting more formalised approval and stakeholder processes
[[2022-12-18_journey-map-how-much-time]].

### Distributed and remote teams

Geographic dispersion amplifies the three standard failure modes — lack of
buy-in for the process, lack of belief in the outcomes, and difficulty
iterating on a static document
[[2017-02-05_remote-customer-journey-mapping]]. Real-time collaborative tools
mirror the benefits of an in-person workshop for remote participants
[[2017-02-05_remote-customer-journey-mapping]], and a shared spreadsheet gives
distributed teams a collaborative artifact they can maintain together over the
long term [[2020-05-31_journey-map-digital-template]].

### Relation to neighbouring methods

- **Service blueprints** share the customer-action swimlane but take the
  organisational rather than the user perspective: they add employee frontstage
  and backstage actions, support processes and physical evidence, revealing the
  invisible work that enables each touchpoint
  [[2019-12-01_service-blueprinting-faq]] [[2018-02-04_5-steps-service-blueprinting]]
  [[2021-08-29_service-blueprints-choose-what-experience]]
  [[2019-10-20_service-blueprinting-practice]]. Blueprinting starts *from*
  customer-journey mapping: the customer actions established by the journey map
  become the blueprint's foundation layer
  [[2018-02-04_5-steps-service-blueprinting]]
  [[2019-12-01_service-blueprinting-faq]]. Blueprints are recommended for
  omnichannel or cross-functional experiences
  [[2021-09-26_ux-mapping-methods-study-guide]].
- **Asset maps** catalogue the screens, emails, notifications and interface
  elements users encounter across channels in a workflow. They focus on what
  users see and interact with, where journey maps focus on thoughts and
  feelings; the two combine into a fuller picture, and asset maps work best on
  bounded workflows rather than sprawling journeys [[2021-03-14_asset-mapping]].
- **Ecosystem maps and process maps** are the other two mapping methods
  recommended for discovery: ecosystem maps (hub-and-spoke or concentric
  circles) suit the start of discovery when domain complexity is unknown, and
  process maps zoom into a single workflow later, once a critical area has been
  isolated. Journey maps are the chronological method in between
  [[2022-01-16_mapping-in-discovery]].
- **UX roadmaps, empathy maps, affinity diagrams, scenario maps and cognitive
  maps** round out the mapping family, each serving a different purpose from
  ideation to research synthesis to future planning
  [[2021-09-26_ux-mapping-methods-study-guide]].

### From mapping to journey-centric operations

Journeys are also a level of design scope in their own right, sitting between
the single interaction and the lifetime relationship: interactions that test
flawlessly in isolation break down once woven into a realistic journey
[[2023-07-06_ux-vs-cx]]. Seamlessness across channel handoffs — letting users
resume where they left off without reestablishing context, via authentication,
email links, passcodes, OS handoff, wishlists or save-for-later — is what a
cross-channel journey needs, and it usually requires backend integration to
undo organisational silos [[2017-03-19_seamless-cross-channel]]. Taken to its
operational conclusion, this becomes journey-centric design: organising
business and design operations around customer journeys rather than individual
products, not by dismantling silos (near-impossible) but by creating
connectivity across them, with end-to-end journey analytics measuring business
impact better than product-level metrics and a data foundation that makes
AI-driven personalisation possible; the shift is meant to be evolutionary, a
layer of journey strategy coordinating product-team priorities rather than a
replacement for product design [[2024-10-18_journey-centric-design]].
