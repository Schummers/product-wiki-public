# Idea Validation

## Definition

Idea validation is the stage of a studio's process that sits between generating ideas and building a product: a deliberately cheap, time-boxed test of whether a market wants what the studio is considering, run before any serious money or people are committed. Both studio books treat it as the reason the studio model exists rather than as one step among many. Szigeti's playbook presents it as the studio's core service to founders: proving viability with the studio's resources so that a failed idea costs a few weeks and a small budget instead of a founder's savings and years ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_04-are-startup-studios-for-me]]). Kannan and Peterman, from their interviews with 19 studios, describe the same idea as the second step of a standardised three-step process built on design thinking, where customer validation through user research and marketing testing must be passed before a prototype is built ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_06-process]]). A third work, written from inside one studio, describes validation at the level of a single idea rather than a portfolio: Hexa's three-step process, becoming an expert through user interviews, then scoping the idea in writing, then drawing low-fidelity wireframes, is presented as what has let it validate over 50 startup ideas and rule out many more before writing any code ([[2026-03-05_10x-method_02-finding-and-validating-the-idea]]).

What distinguishes studio validation from what a lone founder does is scale and detachment. Several candidate ideas are tested in parallel, each with a strict budget and a fixed deadline, and most are expected to be trashed. The methods themselves are ordinary (customer interviews, landing pages, paid traffic, direct outreach, letters of intent); the discipline is in running them across many ideas at once, killing quickly, and treating a kill as a normal outcome rather than a loss. The two books differ mainly in what they measure: the practitioner playbook gives per-idea budgets and durations from specific studios, while the research project reports process-level durations and cost ranges that vary widely across the studios interviewed. Hexa sits apart from both on method rather than on scale: its three steps rest on interviews, a written scope and wireframes, and it names none of the landing pages or paid traffic the other two works rely on ([[2026-03-05_10x-method_02-finding-and-validating-the-idea]]).

## Practice

### Why studios validate before building

- Szigeti frames validation as the answer to the "9 out of 10 startups fail" problem: a studio lets founders prove viability before full commitment, so that when an idea fails the team is reassigned rather than dissolved and its knowledge is kept. Design Thinking and Lean Startup methods are the named toolkits ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_04-are-startup-studios-for-me]]).
- Kannan and Peterman describe studios as deliberately designing their process to reduce the probability of failure, by evaluating product-market fit upfront through user research and by using stage gates so that a weak idea cannot advance and consume more resources ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_02-part-1-venture-studio-basics]]).
- The same book positions validation as what separates a studio from an accelerator: a studio validates ideas it generated itself as part of creating the venture, whereas an accelerator takes in companies whose concept already exists and works on the stretch from validation to customer acquisition ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_03-comparison-to-other-models]]).
- Szigeti's strategic advice narrows what should enter validation at all: follow client money and build for demonstrated market problems, knowing who the first paying customer will be, rather than testing idealistic "Uber for X" concepts ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_15-vision-and-strategy]]).

### Methods

Both books list the same family of techniques; the playbook's guest chapters are the most specific.

- **Innonic's toolkit** — customer interviews, Business Model Canvas analysis, and landing pages whose conversion is measured against traffic bought on Facebook and Google Ads or generated by cold emails. The studio considers the process refined enough to have turned it into an online course ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_12-changing-ecommerce-one-startup-at-a-time]]).
- **Szigeti's generic validation stage** — landing pages, online ads and direct customer contact, used to measure two things: market interest and customer acquisition cost ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_16-the-studio-funnel]]).
- **"Offering only, no product"** — in the framework of [[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_15-vision-and-strategy]], a newly recruited CEO runs bare-minimum concept tests that sell an offer without building anything, across 3-5 candidate ideas at once, while the studio tests the same ideas with investors in parallel.
- **Kannan and Peterman's evidence** — user research plus marketing testing, with click-through rates and customer letters of intent cited as the signals that stage gates check ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_06-process]]).
- **Hexa's three steps** — user interviews, a written scope, then low-fidelity wireframes, run in that order on one idea before any code is written ([[2026-03-05_10x-method_02-finding-and-validating-the-idea]]). Detailed below.

### Hexa's three-step process

[[2026-03-05_10x-method_02-finding-and-validating-the-idea]] is the only source here that spells validation out as an ordered, repeatable sequence applied to a single idea, entirely before any code is written. Hexa reports having run it on over 50 startup ideas, ruling out many more.

1. **Become an expert through interviews.** 25-30 potential users, 30 minutes each. Observe their workflows and ask open-ended questions ("Why?", "How?", "Can you show me?"), avoiding leading ones. The chapter sets a stopping rule as well as a target: enough conversations get you to 80% of the learning, and the remaining 20% is not worth chasing.
2. **Write the idea down.** Eight dimensions: summary, problem, solution (MVP plus long-term vision), implementation, go-to-market, market size, opportunity (why now), competition, and hypotheses. The argument for writing is that it forces clarity and surfaces gaps and unexamined assumptions the one-liner hid.
3. **Draw wireframes.** 3-4 low-fidelity wireframes showing what the product does, not how it looks. Where the product is not visual (APIs, AI agents), the equivalent artefact is API documentation or dialogue flows rather than screens.

The chapter states the goal of the sequence as moving beyond having an idea to having a well-thought-out plan for building something exceptional, and frames the whole exercise as validating iteratively rather than building blindly.

### Running several ideas in parallel

- The playbook's pattern is a small batch per CEO: 3-5 candidate ideas tested in parallel, most trashed, 1-2 graduating to the build phase ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_15-vision-and-strategy]]).
- The research book's constraint is per person rather than per batch: each team member who ideates can manage 1-3 ideas simultaneously through customer validation and prototyping, which makes team size the direct limit on how many ideas a studio can validate at once ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_21-other-assumptions]]).
- Innonic collects ideas continuously into a shared spreadsheet and has a Validation Team evaluate them with several models, stating that no single "golden standard" method dominates ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_12-changing-ecommerce-one-startup-at-a-time]]).

### Time and budget

The two books give figures at different levels, so they are listed separately rather than reconciled.

**Startup Studio Playbook (per idea, from named studios):**

- Szigeti's generic guidance: the validation stage typically lasts a few days to a few weeks on a small budget ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_16-the-studio-funnel]]).
- Innonic distinguishes two layers: research-based pre-validation costs 2-4 weeks and only a couple thousand dollars per idea; full validation with a dedicated CEO and a Minimal Awesome Product costs EUR 20,000 over 3-6 months. The gap between the two enforces what the chapter calls ruthless prioritisation of only the most promising concepts ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_12-changing-ecommerce-one-startup-at-a-time]]).

**Venture Studios Demystified (per process, across 19 studios):**

- The financial model assumes a 12-month end-to-end process from concept to spin-out, with actual timelines ranging from 6 to 18 months depending on the studio and market. Customer validation and MVP design costs vary across studios and are treated as model inputs rather than fixed numbers ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_21-other-assumptions]]).
- Where validation is run by entrepreneurs in residence, programs typically last 3-6 months; EIRs draw a salary but share costs during the validation and MVP phases, and reported outcomes range from 3 of 50 EIRs launching to 1-4 selected from 200 applicants who all launch ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_21-other-assumptions]]).

Note the difference in what is being counted: the playbook's "few days to weeks" is the validation test alone, while the research book's 6-18 months covers the whole path from concept to spin-out, of which validation is the second of three steps.

### Deciding, and killing

- Validation ends with a decision on which ideas advance to the build phase; the playbook insists on clear success and failure criteria, fixed deadlines, and fast, hard decisions, because postponing a kill frustrates the team and burns resources ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_16-the-studio-funnel]]).
- Kannan and Peterman report the same behaviour from the studios interviewed: many ideas are killed in the earliest research stages, and the ideas that survive are scrutinised against short-term evaluation windows, with stage gates requiring evidence such as LOIs, click-through targets or an investment committee review before more funding is released ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_06-process]]).
- The playbook adds that emotional distance is a prerequisite: keep an emotional distance from ideas until traction appears, and rely on an abundance of alternatives so that letting go is psychologically easy ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_16-the-studio-funnel]]).

### What follows a validated idea

- In Szigeti's funnel, ideas that pass validation enter an initial build phase with a dedicated CEO and possibly a CTO, a few weeks of build time for a simple web app with one core feature and good design, and the goal of generating traction and testing user acquisition before follow-on funding is sought. The next gate checks for a working MVP, active users and a scalable growth engine, or at a higher bar product-market fit ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_16-the-studio-funnel]]).
- Innonic deliberately does not build a minimum viable product at that point but a "Minimal Awesome Product", one that either solves a painful problem with a unique solution or is markedly more user-friendly than any competitor, because in its view modern users expect well-functioning, well-designed software and have no tolerance for poor initial quality ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_12-changing-ecommerce-one-startup-at-a-time]]).
- Kannan and Peterman's third step, building and testing early prototypes, exists to validate the product concept further before a full company launch, and a studio's own stage gates decide whether an idea receives additional funding ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_06-process]], [[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_02-part-1-venture-studio-basics]]).

### Where the sources differ

- **Vantage point.** Szigeti prescribes: parallel bare-minimum tests, strict per-idea budgets, kill fast. Kannan and Peterman observe: studios broadly follow a three-step process, but durations, costs and success rates vary widely between the 19 studios they interviewed, and they present those as assumptions to be chosen in a financial model rather than as recommendations ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_21-other-assumptions]]).
- **Depth of validation before building.** Innonic's second validation layer already includes a Minimal Awesome Product and a dedicated CEO over 3-6 months ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_12-changing-ecommerce-one-startup-at-a-time]]), whereas the research book keeps customer validation and prototype building as two separate steps, with validation of customer demand completed before prototype development begins ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_06-process]]). Both agree on the order; they draw the line between "validating" and "building" in different places.
- **What counts as a validation test.** Szigeti and Innonic measure demand with landing pages, online ads and cold email, signals from strangers who were never asked to be interviewed; Kannan and Peterman have stage gates check click-through rates and letters of intent. Hexa's sequence stops at interviews, a written scope and wireframes, and names none of those market tests ([[2026-03-05_10x-method_02-finding-and-validating-the-idea]]). All three place the exercise before the build, but they disagree on whether validating means examining the idea or selling it.
- **Outcome evidence.** Only the research book offers aggregate results: companies founded out of studios convert from Seed to Series A at 72% versus 42% for non-studio companies, which the authors attribute to systematic ideation and de-risking, validation included ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_06-process]]). The playbook gives individual studio stories instead.
