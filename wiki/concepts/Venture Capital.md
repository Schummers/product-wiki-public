# Venture Capital

## Definition

In this corpus, venture capital is never described on its own terms. It appears through the lens of the venture studio, in four roles at once: the model studios are contrasted against, the investor base a studio must persuade, the cap-table convention a studio's ventures must eventually fit, and the fund economics that studios increasingly adopt for themselves. Both books agree on the core contrast. *Venture Studios Demystified* places VC funds "furthest away" from the studio model: financiers who provide capital and ad-hoc advice through a network, enter after a company has been created and partly developed, and never take on the role or responsibilities of a co-founder ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_03-comparison-to-other-models]]). The *Startup Studio Playbook* frames the same distance from the fundraising side: studios are a new and unproven asset class next to traditional VC, so the studio founder's credibility and early traction are the highest barriers to raising institutional money ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_20-fundraising-and-investor-relations]]).

The relationship is also converging. *Venture Studios Demystified* documents studios raising their own investment funds with standard management fees and carry to fight dilution and to fund operations, and anticipates VCs moving downstream into studio-building while studios move upstream toward VC economics ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_05-strategy]], [[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_25-industrys-path-forward]]). The practical content of this page is therefore about how a studio positions itself against VC, raises from investors who think in VC terms, keeps its ventures fundable by VCs, and borrows the VC fund structure without losing what makes the studio model different. Closely related pages: [[Studio Fundraising]], [[Equity Ownership]], [[Studio Economics]], [[Venture Studio]].

## Practice

### How the studio model differs from VC

*Venture Studios Demystified* draws the line on three axes: stage of involvement, duration, and operational role. VCs enter after company creation with some development already done; studios cover the whole journey from ideation through customer acquisition. VC support is ad hoc and network-based; studio support is continuous and hands-on. VCs do not co-found or engage in day-to-day operations; studios do ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_03-comparison-to-other-models]]). The same book's closing chapters position studios as a parallel funding model to VC, offering earlier support and structured ideation to entrepreneurs who lack a clear idea or want to de-risk company creation. The entrepreneur's side of that trade is explicit: founding equity and early autonomy given up in exchange for expertise, capital and structured ideation, with the caveat that the "co-founder" services a studio charges co-founder-level shares for are paid for in pre-seed and often stop being available later in the company's life ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_23-implications-for-stakeholders]]).

The *Startup Studio Playbook* describes the pitch a studio makes to investors against single-company investing: the studio reduces per-venture risk through operational expertise, diversification and shared resources, and offers a diversified portfolio of new startups with lower failure risk at portfolio level and better equity upside than a single angel ticket ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_20-fundraising-and-investor-relations]]). *Venture Studios Demystified* records the same claim from the studios it interviewed, in stronger language: a higher "batting average" and fewer zero outcomes make studios less reliant on one monster winner, which they present as better risk-adjusted returns than traditional VC. The authors add that whether this advantage persists at scale remains unproven ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_23-implications-for-stakeholders]]).

### VC as an investor in the studio itself

Both books treat raising money for the studio's own operations as the hard problem, and both trace the difficulty to the fact that investors are used to VC structures.

The *Startup Studio Playbook* lists five funding sources for a studio: founder capital, VC funding to the studio, corporate funding, family offices and angels, and studio-specific pre-seed funds, plus a hybrid where agency revenue bootstraps the ventures. VC funding to the studio is described as possible but difficult, requiring founder credibility and demonstrated traction. Most early successful studios were founded by established entrepreneurs or institutional founders with the capital to self-fund the first 5-10 ventures, which removes external fundraising friction until there is traction to show ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_20-fundraising-and-investor-relations]]).

*Venture Studios Demystified* reports what the operating-company investor base actually looks like across the 19 studios it interviewed: because studios are a new asset class unlike VC structures, studio leaders struggle to convince investors to fund the operating entity, a problem amplified in less mature ecosystems. Operating companies therefore keep a small LP base, mainly high net worth individuals and family offices who want direct exposure to early-stage ventures, accept the lack of exit guarantees, and can wait 5-7 years for returns. Typical raises are $10M-20M for the operating or holding company, with a few outliers at $50M. For dual-entity studios, the operating company and the investment fund are financed from separate LP pools, and the fund usually commands the larger and more diverse one. The authors expect traditional LP participation at the operating-company level to grow as studios demonstrate consistent returns and keep high initial ownership ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_13-studio-financing]]).

On the investor-relations side, the Playbook gives the operational guidance:

- **Approach investors centrally, as a studio, not per venture.** Educate them on the model, explain portfolio diversification, show early traction, and build trust by delivering quality companies. Once one deal closes with an investor, the second and third close faster ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_20-fundraising-and-investor-relations]]).
- **A minimal fundraising deck covers vision, financial model, team, portfolio and roadmap** ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_20-fundraising-and-investor-relations]]).
- **Corporate money is an increasing trend**, often structured so the corporation takes the majority of returns while the studio keeps operational autonomy ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_20-fundraising-and-investor-relations]]).

### Keeping ventures fundable: the cap-table problem

The single most concrete point of agreement between the two books is that a studio taking too much early equity breaks the venture's relationship with later VC investors.

The Playbook names the objection: investors worry that if the studio holds majority equity at the early stages, the CEO and team lack skin in the game. Its answer is to show post-seed cap table adjustments that raise team ownership, and to let the venture CEOs speak directly to investors ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_20-fundraising-and-investor-relations]]).

*Venture Studios Demystified* documents the market-wide consequence. Historically most studios took majority ownership; those studios struggled, because the high stake prevented them from securing top founding talent and the non-traditional cap table confused potential VC investors. The typical stake is now 25-40%, and only 4 of the 19 interviewed studios still take majority equity. Studios with large funds can target 20-35% ownership maintained through Series A and B, but most lack the capital for full participation in later rounds and get diluted ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_14-equity-ownership]]). For the full range of factors that move the stake venture by venture, see [[Equity Ownership]].

### VC economics versus studio economics

*Venture Studios Demystified* explains why the two models are drifting toward each other. VC assets under management have grown faster than deal counts, producing larger checks and mega-deals that capture 44% of VC capital, which pushes VCs toward later-stage companies. The studio model was premised on the opposite: returns from cheap founding equity at near-zero valuation, not from writing large checks, so that even a 50-100x exit on a modest company pays well. The weakness is dilution. A studio starting at roughly 30-45% at founding can be as low as 5% at exit once VCs and employees take their pieces, which caps absolute returns despite the cheap entry ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_05-strategy]]).

There is also a timing gap. Company-building costs and salaries are paid upfront while exits arrive years later, so studios need intermediate revenue to survive the VC-length horizon. The book documents three bridges: billing back launched companies (preferred, because it does not distract leadership), corporate co-builds (30-50% margins above cost, studio taking 30-35% equity in the co-built company, at the price of coordination complexity), and corporate consulting (40% margins, adopted by only 1 of 19 studios because it conflicts with the venture-building mission). The alternative to revenue is cost reduction: outsourcing MVP development, early go/no-go gates capping spend at $50K-100K per idea, or replicating proven models across geographies ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_10-revenue-generation]]). See [[Revenue Model]] and [[Studio Economics]].

### Borrowing the VC fund structure

The response documented across *Venture Studios Demystified* is for studios to raise investment funds of their own. At the time of the interviews, 11 of 19 studios had already raised a fund and 4 more planned to. A fund lets the studio invest its pro-rata allocation in later rounds and hold a target ownership (often 20%); it pays higher valuations for those shares but produces significantly higher absolute returns at exit, modelled at potentially $22M per exit. Management fees offset operating expenses and extend runway before exits are needed. About 50% of studios with funds also invest outside their own portfolio, which the authors read as convergence toward traditional VC economics ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_05-strategy]]).

The fund is modelled with standard VC terms. The book's base case assumes four closed-ended funds over a decade, growing from $45M to $120M, with 3-year investment horizons, a 2% management fee and 20% carry. Under those assumptions the operating company's incremental capital need falls from $10M to $4M and the studio stays near break-even through year 5; carry, if reinvested in the holding company, accelerates growth without new outside money. Fund and operating company together yield 37% IRR and 24x MoM versus 24% IRR and 9x for the operating company alone; with one early exit the operating company reaches 50% IRR and 57x on only $24M cumulative capital. The authors conclude that raising a fund is now essential to studio economics and that most studios are moving toward one ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_17-raising-a-fund]]).

Choosing the fund type is where VC convention weighs most heavily:

- **Most studios pick closed-ended funds**, mainly because the VC community accepts them and they tell investors the expected portfolio size and time horizon. Closed-ended studios typically raise a small first fund ($10M-20M), then $50M-100M, then $100M-200M to support follow-on investment ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_12-fund-type]]).
- **Evergreen funds are more flexible but harder to raise.** They let LPs join and leave as the studio matures, allow operating-budget changes and strategy pivots, and avoid a 10-year structure that, in the words of one studio quoted, forces unsustainable growth rates or premature exits when starting from the idea stage. But LPs understand 2% and 20% far more readily than a reinvestment model, so patient capital is a prerequisite. Some evergreen studios pay guaranteed 5-20% dividends on preferred shares over 5-10 years instead of carry. Studios that chose evergreen (AlleyCorp, Polymath, Founders, Rule 1 are named) usually had exceptionally patient LPs or prior venture success ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_12-fund-type]]).
- **Fund size limits follow-on participation.** Studios that cannot follow their companies through rounds often syndicate their pro-rata rights to LPs, which again aligns them with the traditional VC model ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_12-fund-type]]).
- **Studio fund cycles are faster than VC's**, every 3-4 years rather than a 10-year horizon, and successive raises are justified with unrealized valuations and early exits, which creates pressure to show portfolio success quickly ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_12-fund-type]]).

The Playbook describes an earlier, lighter version of the same idea: a dedicated pre-seed or venture-creation fund that finances studio venture creation, makes cost structures transparent, and separates studio runway from portfolio funding ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_20-fundraising-and-investor-relations]]). The two books do not disagree; the 2022 research simply finds the fund far more widespread and models it with full VC economics. The mechanics are developed in [[Studio Fundraising]].

### VC people inside studios

*Venture Studios Demystified* identifies former venture capitalists as one of three founder backgrounds for studios, alongside serial entrepreneurs and product-development agencies. VC-background founders bring market knowledge, pattern recognition from having seen many companies succeed or fail, asset-management experience and fundraising ability, and view the studio as a way to de-risk investments; Pioneer Square Labs and Sutter Hill Ventures are given as examples. Their weakness is less hands-on experience with early-stage ideation and building idea funnels. Conversely, serial-entrepreneur founders often lack the asset-management and fundraising expertise needed to raise an associated fund. The authors recommend founding teams that draw from all three backgrounds ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_04-studio-formation]]). See [[Studio Leadership]] and [[Studio Team]].

### Where the relationship is heading

The final chapter of *Venture Studios Demystified* expects three paths of expansion for the asset class: traditional VCs building studios of their own to work with high-potential founders, serial entrepreneurs starting studios, and established studios seeding new ones. It places studios at the start of a hype cycle, moving from innovation trigger toward the peak of inflated expectations, a phase in which LP demand for exposure outpaces studio supply and even non-premier founders can raise; as the cycle matures, LPs will concentrate capital with proven studio leaders. Two consequences follow for the VC relationship. First, as VCs and studios compete for founders moving upstream, equity splits will shift toward founders, possibly reaching an "equity floor" where the economics stop being attractive to studios. Second, follow-on investment can scale indefinitely while studio operations cannot, so studios with larger funds may deploy capital by seeding new studio leaders rather than expanding their own team ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_25-industrys-path-forward]]).

### Points where the two books differ

- **Scope of evidence.** The Playbook (2019) speaks from a practitioner's experience and describes VC funding to the studio as one option among five, difficult but possible ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_20-fundraising-and-investor-relations]]). *Venture Studios Demystified* (2022), interviewing 19 studios, finds the operating-company LP base is in practice mostly individuals and family offices, with the fund as the vehicle that attracts the broader investor base ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_13-studio-financing]]).
- **How much the fund matters.** The Playbook presents the studio-specific pre-seed fund as an emerging model ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_20-fundraising-and-investor-relations]]); *Venture Studios Demystified* calls raising a fund essential and models it in detail ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_17-raising-a-fund]]). Three years separate the two, and neither contradicts the other.
- **Majority equity.** The Playbook treats the studio holding majority equity early as a concern to be managed with later cap-table adjustments ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_20-fundraising-and-investor-relations]]); *Venture Studios Demystified* reports that the market has already moved away from majority stakes because of exactly that concern ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_14-equity-ownership]]).
