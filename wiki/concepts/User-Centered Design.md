# User-Centered Design

## Definition

User-centered design is an approach to product development in which user needs and research inform all decisions; it requires specialized professionals and organizational commitment, and is increasingly recognized as business-critical [[2017-12-24_100-years-ux]]. It names a family of methodologies that put user needs, research, and feedback at the core, of which design thinking is one instantiation [[2021-08-22_design-thinking-study-guide]], and in practice it means practitioners using human-centered research and design to create consistent, positive, and accessible experiences across all touchpoints [[2024-11-15_what-is-user-experience]]. Its defining move is the ordering of concerns: establish what users need to achieve first, and only then select the technology or feature that enables it [[2025-07-04_powered-by-ai-is-not-a-value-proposition]].

Two qualifications recur. It is not a matter of always being right about design decisions — understanding people is the core competency [[2017-10-08_ux-lessons]] — and it is not confined to end users: UX professionals are called out for failing to extend the same user-centeredness to the colleagues and stakeholders who are the actual audience for their deliverables [[2017-01-22_sketch-test]]. At its organizational limit, user research and user-centered design drive everything from the highest level of strategy down to the smallest design element [[2022-01-02_ux-maturity-stage-6]].

## Practice

### Understanding users: personas and jobs-to-be-done

Personas and jobs-to-be-done are both tools for keeping users central by understanding their needs, goals, and contexts, and the source argues against treating them as an either/or [[2017-08-06_personas-jobs-be-done]]. A job-to-be-done focuses on outcomes — the specific goals and success criteria users want, without prescribing how they accomplish the task — and is typically summarized in one sentence describing what the user needs to accomplish plus the context that affects it. Good personas go well past demographics to capture behavioural and attitudinal data: mental models, pain points, goals, emotional considerations. Personas help teams prioritize when the same job-to-be-done carries different requirements for different user groups, and they build empathy through narrative and personal detail in a way JTBD does not, even though JTBD does capture emotional criteria. The two are compatible: fold JTBD information into personas, use them side by side, or pick one according to organizational context and resistance to change [[2017-08-06_personas-jobs-be-done]]. The source also warns that designing from a clean slate purely for the jobs of brand-new users can severely disrupt the workflows of dedicated existing customers, who then have to relearn the product [[2017-08-06_personas-jobs-be-done]].

### Communicating the user through stories

A UX story is an account of events from the user's perspective, showing how an experience evolves [[2017-01-15_ux-stories]]. It keeps the focus on goals, motivations, and experience rather than on technical requirements, and it differs from use cases, scenarios, and storyboards by emphasizing character, motivation, and plot rather than action sequences for requirements — which is what makes it more memorable and persuasive. Its components are character, goal or motivation, context of time and place, plot, insight (significance and pain points), and spectacle (the visual presentation) [[2017-01-15_ux-stories]].

Practical points [[2017-01-15_ux-stories]]: keep stories simple yet engaging, understandable across levels of expertise and age, since complexity obscures; expect better recall, because facts laced into a coherent narrative are remembered better than isolated ones; use them across phases — summarizing research, generating ideas through what-if exploration, conveying abstract concepts, persuading stakeholders, evaluating designs against a baseline narrative; and keep them grounded in real user research, since fabricated stories risk satisfying imaginary needs. Their organizational function is buy-in and empathy: they let stakeholders empathize with users, align on common goals, and commit to solutions.

### Extending user-centeredness to your own audience

The sketch test applies user-research protocol to UX deliverables — wireframes, personas, journey maps, reports. A colleague reads the deliverable and produces a short sketch or summary; the discrepancies reveal what was misunderstood or unclear [[2017-01-22_sketch-test]]. It surfaces both perceptual problems (content visually obscured) and comprehension problems (meaning misinterpreted). How to run it [[2017-01-22_sketch-test]]:

- Recruit participants matching the deliverable's real audience — developers for wireframes, project managers for reports; colleagues are appropriate here.
- Watch behaviour rather than collecting suggestions: what they circle, underline, redraw, or struggle with reveals pain points better than verbal feedback.
- Provide scratch paper and permission to annotate the deliverable directly, which exposes mental models and assumptions.
- Do not correct misunderstandings during the session — correcting biases the remaining data, exactly as in user research.
- Use their sketches to guide revision, paying attention to the different visual metaphors, intermediate inferences, and unfamiliar terminology they reach for, which point at content gaps.
- Keep the focus on format clarity while accepting derivative content suggestions as valuable.

### Design thinking as one instantiation

Design thinking is a problem-solving framework built on the philosophy that a hands-on, user-centric approach promotes innovation, which in turn leads to differentiation and competitive advantage [[2021-08-22_design-thinking-study-guide]]. Its six phases [[2021-08-22_design-thinking-study-guide]]:

1. **Empathize** — research user behaviour and needs.
2. **Define** — synthesize findings into problem statements, with visualizations such as empathy maps and journey maps.
3. **Ideate** — diverge to generate ideas, then converge collaboratively on an agreed outcome, using how-might-we questions, brainstorming, sketching, and scenario mapping with personas.
4. **Prototype** — build representations, from low-fidelity sketches to high-fidelity interactive versions.
5. **Test** — evaluate with users; feedback loops drive refinement.
6. **Implement** — deploy.

The phases are iterative rather than linear: testing a prototype may reveal it does not work as expected and send the team back to an earlier stage [[2021-08-22_design-thinking-study-guide]]. Workshops are singled out as enabling hands-on problem-solving and inclusive decision-making in ways standard meetings cannot, with structured activities and facilitation letting diverse team members contribute [[2021-08-22_design-thinking-study-guide]].

### Holding the balance with business

Focusing solely on the optimal user experience while ignoring business goals leads to failure; recommendations have to balance both to keep the organization viable [[2017-10-08_ux-lessons]]. That article's other lessons: there is no perfect user interface, so prefer incremental improvement over solving everything at once; test on a regular cadence rather than too late, when there is no time left to adjust, and involve team members in research to build buy-in; move from reacting to design requests to shaping product vision and using data to evangelize, since it is very difficult to argue with data; earn trust incrementally through small visible wins, because systemic change takes years depending on UX maturity; and extend empathy to colleagues and stakeholders, resolving conflicts through compromise [[2017-10-08_ux-lessons]].

The same alignment requirement shows up in the French corpus, in the context of recruitment case studies: a design proposal must serve the company's strategic objectives — growth, revenue, retention — to have real impact, and even an open brief should serve company strategy rather than being spent on craft and interaction polish [[2025-09-02_393_Case_Study_Design_Mes_3_pires_erreurs_à_ne_pas_reproduire...]]. That source's first lesson is a user-centered one: always start from the overall context and the end users, and make that explicit at every step of a presentation rather than only holding it internally, because every step of the reasoning is what gets evaluated [[2025-09-02_393_Case_Study_Design_Mes_3_pires_erreurs_à_ne_pas_reproduire...]].

### User needs before technology

A value proposition is a promise to users of the value they can expect; it guides teams to prioritize features aligned with one specific user goal and to say no to nice-to-haves outside that journey [[2025-07-04_powered-by-ai-is-not-a-value-proposition]]. "Powered by AI" cannot serve as one, because it names a technology rather than a user benefit and makes users wholly responsible for working out how the tool fits their workflow. The consequences described: when "it has AI" is the value proposition, the only guiding principle left is making the experience as AI as possible, which tends to produce broad-scope chatbots with high adoption barriers; and because users and designers are aware of LLM limitations, advertising AI presence can reduce conversion unless the specific anxieties are addressed. Strong propositions state what the user achieves — "accelerate your research", "never miss a bill" — rather than how it is built, and successful products start by doing one thing well; how to deliver value should be considered only after establishing what that value is [[2025-07-04_powered-by-ai-is-not-a-value-proposition]].

### Scope of the discipline

User experience is the holistic relationship — perceptions, emotions, interactions — between a person and a product, service, or company; the UX field is the professional practice of designing and enhancing that experience for all users [[2024-11-15_what-is-user-experience]]. Distinctions the source insists on: usability is a critical component focused on task efficiency, while UX also covers brand perception, emotional response, and long-term relationship; the user interface is the set of components enabling interaction, while UX spans every touchpoint; and UX is not limited to websites or applications but applies to entire ecosystems and journeys. Good UX is a business imperative rather than a nice-to-have — organizations with adequate UX roles and knowledge build products that reduce support costs and increase conversion — yet many organizations sit in the bottom half of the UX-maturity scale and treat UX as expendable when budgets tighten, which the source calls short-sighted. Whatever the work is called, UX as a mindset, a user-first mentality prioritizing meaningful positive experiences, should occur in every department [[2024-11-15_what-is-user-experience]].

### The organizational end state

At stage 6 of the six-stage UX-maturity model, everyone is fully enlightened about the value of user-centered design, and user research drives everything from organizational strategy down to the tiniest element of a design system [[2022-01-02_ux-maturity-stage-6]]. What that looks like across the four maturity factors — strategy, culture, process, outcomes: top-level leadership committed to UX with dedicated senior roles (UX VPs, design directors, research directors); adequate budgets consistently directed at UX growth; a specialized design-system team continuously iterating a well-vetted system, which is what keeps quality consistent at scale; a culture treating UX as an equal business-operations partner, reinforced through employee training and development paths; a process that is established yet malleable and applied across all teams and departments; and outcomes measured by success metrics aligned with a user-centered strategy [[2022-01-02_ux-maturity-stage-6]]. Development operations embrace user-driven iterative design, and teams plan for change and innovation from unmet market needs [[2022-01-02_ux-maturity-stage-6]].

The source is explicit that this stage is hard to reach and harder to hold. Culture is the linchpin: it deteriorates as soon as employees make decisions not aligned with user-centered design, so shared values need constant nurturing through leadership messaging, accountability, and celebration of UX successes. Organizations arrive there by different routes — new companies with UX from inception, small established companies with forward-thinking leadership, larger organizations committed to design innovation — each with its own sustainability challenge. Regression to stage 5 is not failure; the memory of having performed well can be used to motivate a renewed effort [[2022-01-02_ux-maturity-stage-6]].

### The long view

The field's trajectory is presented as evidence that user-centered design is becoming structural rather than optional [[2017-12-24_100-years-ux]]. It began in the 1950s at Bell Labs, where psychologist John Karlin designed the touchtone keypad; Don Norman coined "user experience" for his group at Apple in 1993, but the field predates the term. Growth came in three phases: 100x from 1950 to 1983 (ten to a thousand professionals, in the mainframe era with limited incentive), 1,000x from 1983 to 2017 (a thousand to a million, driven by the PC revolution where the user became the buyer and the web revolution where experience became the gatekeeper to revenue), and a projected 100x from 2017 to 2050, reaching a hundred million — around 1% of the global population. What drove the acceleration was structural business pressure, not cultural recognition alone. The field is now global, with practitioners in more than 74 countries and every sector needing UX, and the projection rests on the estimate that overall experience quality is still less than 10% of what it should be, with future growth focused on augmenting human cognition and knowledge-economy productivity [[2017-12-24_100-years-ux]].
