# Design Leadership

## Definition

Across these sources, design leadership is described less as a position in a hierarchy than as a way of operating: the designer takes responsibility for the quality of the conversation around a product, not for having the final say on it. [[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_06-chapter-5-get-in-the-right-mindset]] frames the designer as a facilitator who guides discussion toward good solutions and champions great design, explicitly giving up ultimate control in exchange for influence. [[2025-07-18_the-product-triad-designs-role]] places the same posture inside a cross-functional team: the designer becomes the authority on user needs and makes sure the team prioritises work that solves real user problems, while ownership of decisions stays collective.

The sources also treat it as an organisational condition, not only an individual skill. [[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_12-chapter-11-how-executives-can-help-designers]] argues that design leadership has to exist at the executive level: without designers in positions of authority, even talented teams work under constraints that prevent the best outcome. And when a designer becomes a manager, [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_29-mia-blume-skills-which-future-design-managers-should-work-on]] describes the shift as moving from providing solutions to coaching others to find their own, with the same underlying move of letting go of control.

A later source adds a scarcity argument and a second track. [[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_01-why-i-wrote-this-book]] holds that many organisations lack design leaders capable of building career development plans, and describes a self-perpetuating cycle: designers pushed into management to grow, whose real desire is to focus on products rather than people, have little motivation to build paths for others, while managers who are not designers lack the knowledge to guide them. [[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_03-1-design-career-paths]] separates the two things that "leadership" usually conflates, describing people management and the individual-contributor track (staff, principal) as distinct paths that large organisations increasingly support in parallel, and reporting that Square's internal levelling document states outright that becoming a manager is a lateral move rather than a promotion. Read alongside [[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_11-9-growing-your-impact]], which treats leadership as a widening of impact — from improving yourself, to mentoring a team, to influencing the organisation; from a single task to a whole product line — the sources converge on leadership as a competency an individual contributor can grow into before, or instead of, a title.

## Practice

### Lead the conversation, not the decision

- Act as a facilitator rather than a defender or a salesperson: guide the conversation toward a solution instead of absorbing whatever is thrown at you ([[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_06-chapter-5-get-in-the-right-mindset]]). See [[Design Critique]].
- Accept that you do not have the final say. That mental shift removes defensiveness and lets you concentrate on communication, which is what actually moves the outcome ([[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_06-chapter-5-get-in-the-right-mindset]]).
- Prepare stakeholders to carry your work into rooms you are not in: give them the vocabulary and the confidence to explain the thinking, so they become advocates rather than messengers ([[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_06-chapter-5-get-in-the-right-mindset]]). See [[Design Rationale]] and [[Stakeholder Engagement]].
- Watch your language: lead with "yes" to keep the conversation open, and avoid "you're wrong", "from a design perspective", and talk of liking or not liking, in favour of language about effectiveness and function ([[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_06-chapter-5-get-in-the-right-mindset]]).

### Hold the line on changes without refusing them

- Do not accept every requested change indiscriminately, and do not simply refuse either: treat a stakeholder request as a new constraint and a design challenge, find the underlying need, and communicate the reasoning that balances stakeholder needs against user needs ([[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_11-chapter-10-dealing-with-changes]]).
- Maintain a positive balance in the "emotional bank account" with stakeholders, a term Greever takes from Stephen Covey: refusing a change you believe is wrong for the user is a withdrawal even when you turn out to be right, so the credit built by earlier deposits is what lets you choose your battles ([[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_11-chapter-10-dealing-with-changes]]).
- Greever states that the ability to set, adjust and communicate expectations matters more than the ability to produce the perfect solution every time; his own account of taking over a struggling project describes fixing it purely by managing conversations, with a publicly visible prioritised list and advance warning when he could not deliver ([[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_11-chapter-10-dealing-with-changes]]).
- Recognise when you are genuinely wrong. Three red flags: the problem still exists, users do not understand the design, or the majority of stakeholders disagree. Admitting it directly rebuilds trust faster than defending ([[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_11-chapter-10-dealing-with-changes]]).
- Greever explicitly rules out manipulation tactics such as "painting a duck" and the Disney "hairy arms": the situation is better avoided upstream by setting context and fidelity correctly and telling stakeholders what feedback is useful ([[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_11-chapter-10-dealing-with-changes]]).

### Lead inside a cross-functional team

- In a product triad, the designer's leadership is expressed as being the authority on user needs, articulating user problems through research and steering the team toward work that creates user value ([[2025-07-18_the-product-triad-designs-role]]). See [[Team Collaboration]].
- That authority is not ownership of one leg of the stool: effective triads collaborate on desirability, viability and feasibility together, with one person leading on getting decisions made while the team owns them jointly ([[2025-07-18_the-product-triad-designs-role]]).
- Autonomy comes from evidence, not assertion: using user-centred insight to drive stakeholder alignment rather than acting on assumptions is what keeps the team from building the wrong thing ([[2025-07-18_the-product-triad-designs-role]]).

### Leading other designers

- The transition from individual contributor to manager turns on letting go of control and becoming a coach rather than a solution provider ([[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_29-mia-blume-skills-which-future-design-managers-should-work-on]]).
- Listening comes first: when a teammate comes for an answer, understand their problem before offering direction, then hand the problem back with empowering questions such as what the goal of the exercise is, or how they would prioritise with two more weeks ([[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_29-mia-blume-skills-which-future-design-managers-should-work-on]]).
- Avoid making the decision yourself unless the project is high-risk or the deadline tight; say explicitly that you trust the person's judgement and offer support and perspective instead of direction ([[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_29-mia-blume-skills-which-future-design-managers-should-work-on]]).
- Blume argues designers already carry the foundational leadership skills, creativity, vision, storytelling, empathy and systems thinking, so management training should add coaching and delegation rather than start from scratch ([[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_29-mia-blume-skills-which-future-design-managers-should-work-on]]). See [[Empathy]] and [[UX Career]].

### Growing into leadership without a title

The chapters of *The Path to Senior Product Designer* treat leadership as something to practise inside an existing IC role, each naming a different entry point.

- **Mentorship first.** [[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_20-18-mentorship]] calls mentoring the first step in building leadership competency and the path toward management. It distinguishes mentorship (sharing knowledge and experience) from coaching (the more advanced form that teaches someone to find solutions themselves, which it ties to senior and management roles) — the same distinction Blume's chapter turns on. It argues the mentor gains as much as the mentee: teaching a subject forces you to understand it well, and it improves communication, visibility and leadership capability. Practically: rate teammates against your top competencies to find gaps and offer to mentor, and watch passively for the signals — a teammate asking the same question repeatedly, another department asking for something they could learn to do, someone still struggling after feedback. Set outcome-focused SMART goals rather than tool-shaped ones, so the manager can approve them, the mentee can agree to them, and the impact shows at review time. Use Explain–Demonstrate–Imitate–Practice for hard skills; for soft skills, reverse-engineer your own approach by reflecting on the inputs (context, who gave the feedback, why), your outputs and your reasoning, so you teach the mental model rather than examples. Alternative formats it offers: documentation, which lasts and suits introverts, curated education hubs that avoid redistributing copyrighted material, and formal mentorship programmes built with your manager. See [[Mentorship]].
- **Own a process.** [[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_12-10-tools-and-processes-craft]] argues that identifying the problems in documentation, design systems, processes and tools is a much more valuable skill than executing within them, since whoever initiates and defines a process has more leverage than whoever follows it. Its four steps: check whether the process is documented and volunteer to create or update it if not; map it by listing steps, collaborators and tools; find inefficiencies through your own observation and by asking colleagues about frustrations, tool pain points, repetitive tasks, bottlenecks and what they would change with a magic wand; then solve them with documentation, tools, design systems or policy, prioritising with an impact/effort matrix. It suggests pitching the work in company terms — onboarding efficiency, standardisation, a reference point for future changes, better collaboration — and approaching process improvement the way you would build a product, by observing how people solve the problem today.
- **Take ownership before it is granted.** [[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_18-16-independence-ownership]] puts it as "if you want to be an owner of areas of work, act like one": look for projects with no owner, ask PMs which responsibilities they would delegate, watch for openings when people leave or during reorganisations, and note that owning several domains makes you harder to replace. To initiate work, build the case — frame it as a business problem, present alternatives with trade-offs, support it with data and business context, explain how success will be measured, justify the timing, and propose concrete next steps that do not add work to your manager. It also counts outsourcing as leadership practice: identify work that can go out (illustration, video, development, content), make the business case, vet a vendor, and volunteer to own the relationship. See [[Design Ownership]].
- **Get into hiring.** [[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_21-19-hiring-leadership]] presents hiring as a leadership competency open to individual contributors, and one of the best ways to build organisational impact and taste the management role. Designers have better access to talent than recruiters through their own community, so keep a running list of portfolios of people you would like to work with and introduce strong candidates to HR or the hiring manager. Offering to run initial portfolio screening is the way in — it establishes credibility to expand into other steps, such as suggesting exercise ideas, answering candidate questions during a take-home, or giving candidates feedback. On onboarding, it recommends interviewing hires from the last six months about what confused them and what they wish they had known, documenting team structure and goals, design processes, design systems, handoff standards, product and competitor knowledge and customer insights, and connecting new hires to domain experts; the stated goal is to shorten the period during which a new hire draws a salary without yet being able to create value. It acknowledges the substantial time commitment and complexity. See [[Design Hiring]].

Underneath all four sits the measure from [[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_11-9-growing-your-impact]]: recognise the leverage available at each level and upgrade an activity to the next one — from a task toward a product line for product work, from learning toward managing for your own work, from yourself toward the industry for people.

### Leading product from a design background

*Product Management for UX People* looks at the same posture from the product side, where the absence of authority is structural rather than chosen.

- Product managers rarely have direct reports among the people doing the work, designers, developers, customer support or marketing, so leadership is "demonstrated, not bestowed" and happens through persuasion, influence and making everyone else's life easier rather than through hierarchy ([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_06-chapter-3-ux-skills-that-carry-over]]). Crumlish presents this as one of the four UX strengths that carry over directly, alongside information architecture, intense customer fascination and solving problems iteratively.
- The chapter also warns against reading that influence as a licence to decide alone. UX leader Peter Boersma notes PMs have no monopoly on final decisions, designers owning design choices and engineers technical architecture; Matt LeMay adds that people who move into product because they want to be "the decider" often lose their team's trust by deciding in a siloed way, and hopes someone coming from UX would be "sensitive to what it feels like to be excluded from a decision" ([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_06-chapter-3-ux-skills-that-carry-over]]). What the PM brings instead is synthesis, holding competing forces together, with an end result frequently better than any single strong point of view.
- A design-trained leader who moves to product has to stop doing the design. [[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_12-chapter-9-healthy-collaborative-tension-on-the-product-ux]] says a product leader with a UX background must resist the temptation to still be the designer, because that is a different job now, and Crumlish describes his own version of the boundary as functioning like an art director without overstepping into design solutions. It also holds that where UX sits in the org chart, reporting to product, to a design director, or as a peer under a head of product, matters less than the quality of the relationship, and that the hybrid "frankenjob" combining both roles is a golden opportunity for aligning the disciplines that is at the same time incredibly difficult: bring in a peer as soon as the team scales. Caio B. Nishihara, design lead at Ambev Tech, pushes back on the reporting point in the same chapter, arguing that a company that sees design as a strategic driver should keep it out of the product report line to avoid "top-down effects."
- **Head of product as chief information architect.** The phrase is Crumlish's own, arrived at after his product mentor Matte Scheinker asked him at AOL, where the title had vanished from the HR system, how many IAs a company needs; his answer was "at least one" ([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_14-chapter-11-chief-information-architect]]). The coherence question he quotes from information architect Jorge Arango, on Arango's *Informed Life* podcast in 2020: where in the product management framework is there a place for looking after coherence between things that belong to an ecosystem or family of products, Kindle across dedicated reader, iOS and Mac being Arango's example of its absence. Crumlish argues someone must own that coherence at the highest level, mapping the problem and opportunity space and holding the product family together across platforms, device experiences and organisational silos. Dawn Russell, an information architect at CVS Health, adds in the same chapter that decoherence is also born from org structure and internal funding models, her example being the flu shot bookable through both the pharmacy and MinuteClinic, two ways to schedule one shot on one domain. Crumlish's conclusion is that top-down imposition is almost impossible where everything constantly shifts, but a product leader can gradually bring a complex system into harmony. See [[Information Architecture]].
- **Speak the language of business.** Leadership consultant Harry Max, quoted in the same chapter, says designers are often not all that great at communicating with executives: "If you want to be taken seriously and you want a seat at the table and you want to start dealing with executives, you have to start speaking the language of business. You've got to talk about making money. You've got to talk about saving money. You've got to talk about avoiding unnecessary costs." Around that, the chapter asks product leaders to communicate in terms of business outcomes, money, brand, market share, hiring and retention rather than design details, with precision, deep listening, and answers to the questions executives are actually asking ([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_14-chapter-11-chief-information-architect]]).
- **Treat your own team as a product.** Understand colleagues' needs, craft deliverables and communications that solve their problems, overcommunicate, and build trust; partner with customer support, customer success and community managers rather than letting them be siloed, since they carry signal about what customers want; the chapter's precedent is Craig Newmark of Craigslist, who focused on customer support instead of making himself CEO and argued a business only learns by channelling external feedback straight to the decision-making centre ([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_14-chapter-11-chief-information-architect]]).
- **Build the next generation.** Identify product instincts in people already in other roles, engineers, designers, marketers, support staff, and transition them formally with training, coaching and mentorship; pair less experienced product people with experienced practitioners to cross-train skills such as analytics and experimentation, which distributes expertise and makes the team more flexible ([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_14-chapter-11-chief-information-architect]]). See [[Mentorship]] and [[Team Scaling]].

### What the organisation owes design

Chapter 11 is addressed to executives rather than designers, and states the conditions from the other side ([[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_12-chapter-11-how-executives-can-help-designers]]):

- **Realize** the expertise: designers solve problems strategically, and their recommendations deserve trust grounded in their domain knowledge.
- **Prioritize** their needs: clear business requirements, timelines, technical access, stakeholder contact, and budget for usability research.
- **Authorize** quick decisions: projects move at the speed of decision-making, not the speed of talent; daily reviews beat weekly meetings, and empowering designers to decide (with accountability) creates momentum.
- **Recognize** their humanity: genuine relationships and kind, problem-focused language rather than language that attacks the designer.
- Establish vision, goals, metrics, user understanding, workflow cadence, technical requirements and decision-makers at project start, using the design checklist as a flexible guide rather than a fixed list. See [[UX Maturity]].
- Put a designer at the executive table: the chapter's core claim is that companies which consistently ship great products do so because design leadership sits at the highest level.

Two later sources say what happens when that is missing, and what closes the gap. [[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_01-why-i-wrote-this-book]] names the shortage of capable design leaders as one of the systemic reasons designers cannot advance, and warns that promoting product-minded designers into management to give them a path produces leaders whose real desire lies elsewhere. [[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_25-final-thoughts]] proposes the bottom-up counterpart: when individual designers set career goals, build growth plans and demand growth opportunities from their organisations, companies improve their design maturity, which raises standards across the industry and creates more opportunity for every designer. See [[UX Maturity]] and [[UX Career]].
