# Jakob's Law

## Definition

Jakob's Law states that users spend most of their time on other sites, and
prefer a new site to work like the ones they already know
([[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_03-1-jakobs-law]]). People build [[Mental Model]]s of
how a system works from their cumulative experience with similar products, then
transfer those expectations to anything that looks comparable. When a design
matches the model a user already carries, they can be productive immediately;
when it does not, the mismatch shows up as confusion and frustration.

The value of the law is economic rather than aesthetic: the less mental energy
a person spends working out how the interface behaves, the more they have left
for the goal that brought them there
([[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_03-1-jakobs-law]]). Yablonski is explicit that the law
is not an argument for sameness — it does not say every product should be
identical. It says designers should start from the common pattern and depart
from it only when the departure clearly improves the core experience.

## Practice

### Start from the convention

Reduce [[User Friction]] by leaning on familiar patterns in page structure,
workflows, navigation, and element placement, so attention goes to the content
and the task rather than to the mechanics of the interface
([[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_03-1-jakobs-law]]). Familiarity is what lets someone
know immediately how to move through a navigation, find content, and read the
layout's visual cues to see what choices are available.

### The dominant player sets the expectation

The mental model does not come from your product; it comes from wherever the
user spends their time. For search, that is Google, or whichever engine
dominates in the user's country: users' expectations about how [[Search]] and
search features behave are shaped there, so an internal site search should
cater to those expectations and incorporate SERP-feature designs of the same
kind ([[2020-04-26_key-serp-features]]).

### Breaking the pattern has a measurable cost

Snapchat's 2018 redesign combined stories and messaging in unfamiliar ways and
failed to align with users' established models. The result was user backlash,
migration to competitors, dropped ad revenue, and a shrinking user count
([[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_03-1-jakobs-law]]). Google's counter-example is
graduated change: letting users opt into the redesigned version of YouTube,
Gmail or Calendar allowed them to acclimate rather than be dropped into a
mismatch.

Innovation is still allowed, and breaking conventions can differentiate a
product, but the source attaches a condition: test with actual users, and
confirm both that the novel approach is understood and that its value exceeds
the friction of unfamiliarity ([[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_03-1-jakobs-law]]).

### Turning the law into team rules

In the framework Yablonski proposes for applying psychological principles, a
design principle ([[Design Principles]]) is paired with the law that supports it
and then translated into concrete rules. Jakob's Law is the law behind the
principle "familiarity over novelty," which in turn generates rules such as
using common design patterns and avoiding flashy animations
([[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_13-11-applying-psychological-principles-in-design]]).
