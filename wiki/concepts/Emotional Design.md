# Emotional Design

## Definition

Emotional design is the deliberate shaping of the affect a product produces.
Its central term in these sources is delight, defined as any positive emotional
affect a user experiences during interaction with a device or interface
[[2017-03-05_theory-user-delight]], [[2022-11-27_pillars-user-delight]]. Two
structural models organise it. Aarron Walter's hierarchy of user needs, mirroring
Maslow's, puts functionality, then reliability, then usability underneath
delight, so delight is what becomes available once the foundations hold
[[2017-03-05_theory-user-delight]]. Don Norman's three levels of emotional
processing give the second model: visceral (immediate aesthetic response),
behavioral (ease of use and task completion) and reflective (alignment with
personal values), which must be balanced rather than traded off
[[2022-11-27_pillars-user-delight]].

Emotion is not confined to decorative flourishes. It is produced by interaction
qualities — clear labels and consistent behavior triggered joy, while
inconsistency and unexpected behavior triggered surprise and anger
[[2016-07-17_interaction-branding]] — by which moments of a journey a user
remembers [[2018-12-30_peak-end-rule]], and by whether a design respects the
person at all, since deliberately hostile copy is itself emotional design in the
negative direction [[2017-04-30_shaming-users]]. Small interface details created
to provoke an emotion are collected as a genre of their own, for instance in the
Design Spells library [[2025-04-15_373_Top_5_outils_de_benchmark_UXUI_gratuits]].

## Practice

### Build on usability, never instead of it

- A product can be delightful only if it is usable. Functionality is essential,
  reliability is what keeps usability meaningful, and only above those can
  pleasure be appreciated; beautiful but non-functional products fail
  [[2017-03-05_theory-user-delight]].
- Negativity bias raises the stakes: users remember bad experiences more than
  good ones, so delightful features on an unreliable product give no sustainable
  benefit [[2017-03-05_theory-user-delight]].
- Surface delight applied to an unusable interface backfires — forced humour or
  animation reads as non-genuine, can damage brand trust, and wastes the
  investment without improving outcomes [[2017-03-05_theory-user-delight]].
- [[2022-11-27_pillars-user-delight]] states the same dependency from the other
  side: usability is necessary but not sufficient. Aesthetics alone cannot
  overcome poor function, and usability without emotional resonance produces
  delight that is superficial at best; a two-legged stool cannot stand.

### Surface delight and deep delight

- Surface delight is local and contextual: animations, transitions, microcopy,
  high-resolution imagery, sound. It is the stereotype of "delightful" design
  [[2017-03-05_theory-user-delight]].
- Deep delight comes from flow and pain-point removal — the interface working as
  expected, meeting the need at the right time and place, letting the user stay
  on the task. Users who reach it become passionate advocates, but it demands
  streamlined workflows and exceptional UX and is much rarer
  [[2017-03-05_theory-user-delight]].
- The route to it is unglamorous: identify pain points through user research,
  then ideate and engineer solutions. Good UX does not require revolutionary
  technology — biometric login replacing passwords, adding a stop mid-route,
  voice input when hands are occupied, offline downloads, device handoff — and
  delight comes from attention to detail, tone of voice and thoughtful
  formatting at opportune moments [[2016-11-20_ux-thanks]].

### The three pillars and the long view

- Balance visceral, behavioral and reflective; neglecting any one undermines
  lasting delight [[2022-11-27_pillars-user-delight]].
- Define the product's function around deep, long-term user needs rather than
  short-term goals: a fitness app that builds healthy habits and self-confidence
  reaches deeper than one that merely improves fitness
  [[2022-11-27_pillars-user-delight]].
- Use qualitative research — contextual inquiry, interviews, diary studies — to
  reach values, motivations and emotional reactions; quantitative metrics alone
  cannot capture delight [[2022-11-27_pillars-user-delight]].
- Do not impose delight where it is emotionally inappropriate. Cheerful
  interactions during a distressing situation, such as filing a car insurance
  claim after an accident, backfire; the aim is to meet users where they are in
  their emotional journey [[2022-11-27_pillars-user-delight]].

### Interaction qualities as emotional triggers

- Prototypes with clear labels suggesting the action triggered joy and
  associations with sincerity and competence, while inconsistent or unexpected
  behavior produced surprise and anger [[2016-07-17_interaction-branding]].
- Carefully designed unexpected interactions can create pleasant surprise but can
  equally make users feel powerless, so they must be tested with real users
  [[2016-07-17_interaction-branding]].
- Interface micro-details built to spark an emotion can be studied as a
  reference genre, though the benchmark warning applies: an idea observed
  elsewhere should be reworked for your own context and objectives rather than
  copied [[2025-04-15_373_Top_5_outils_de_benchmark_UXUI_gratuits]].

### Designing peaks and endings

- Memory is not an average of an experience but a snapshot weighted by its most
  emotionally intense moments and its final ones; in Kahneman and Frederickson's
  cold-water study, 80% of participants chose to repeat the longer, more painful
  trial because its last moments were slightly warmer
  [[2018-12-30_peak-end-rule]].
- Negative peaks are disproportionately memorable: a difficult navigation menu
  met early in a visit coloured a user's overall assessment and willingness to
  recommend the company [[2018-12-30_peak-end-rule]].
- Design positive peaks through moments of convenience, comfort and delight, for
  example Duolingo's playful feedback, and invest in endings: progress
  indicators that accelerate toward completion feel faster, and a celebratory
  endpoint such as TurboTax's completion screen leaves a lasting positive
  impression [[2018-12-30_peak-end-rule]].
- Handle errors gracefully, especially near the end. Unexpected technical
  failures create negative peaks, and error messages that blame the user or
  offer no resolution path make them worse [[2018-12-30_peak-end-rule]].

### Negative emotional design, on purpose or by neglect

- [[2017-04-30_shaming-users]] distinguishes three levels of bad emotional
  design: mistakes (unthinking design), degradation (something pleasant turning
  unpleasant through repetition), and deliberate hostility — the worst category,
  where manipulinks belong.
- Manipulinks, or confirmshaming, word the rejection option as an undesirable
  self-description ("No thanks, I hate saving money") to make declining feel
  bad. A/B tests showing higher micro conversions may reflect dishonesty rather
  than cleverness, for instance by not disclosing that an email signup follows
  [[2017-04-30_shaming-users]].
- The cost lands later: negative brand perception, lower NPS, reduced
  credibility and lost trust. Research shows users prefer direct, polite modals
  with an X and a plain "No thanks" over condescending rejection copy; if it
  would be rude in person it is rude in copy, and treating users as trickable
  offends them [[2017-04-30_shaming-users]].

## Sources (13)

- [[2016-07-17_interaction-branding]] — Careful interaction design can evoke positive emotions like joy and pleasant surprise, while poor interaction design triggers frustration and anger.
- [[2016-11-20_ux-thanks]] — Going beyond meeting minimum usability to create unexpected moments of joy requires attention to user pain points and thoughtful, empathetic design.
- [[2017-03-05_theory-user-delight]] — the central concept defining emotional affect and distinguishing surface vs. deep delight, and how emotions relate to usability and design strategy.
- [[2017-04-30_shaming-users]] — Explores how deliberately negative emotional design violates the principle of creating pleasant user experiences, damaging brand relationships.
- [[2018-12-30_peak-end-rule]] — Strategic use of visual feedback, celebratory elements, and graceful error handling to create positive emotional peaks and endings that improve users' overall memory of experiences.
- [[2022-11-27_pillars-user-delight]] — Emotional delight combines visceral (aesthetic), behavioral (usable), and reflective (value-aligned) elements; lasting delight requires balance across all three and a long-term perspective that aligns with users' values, aspirations, and current emotional states rather than imposing forced joy.
- [[2025-04-15_373_Top_5_outils_de_benchmark_UXUI_gratuits]]
- [[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_08-6-peakend-rule]] — Chapter focuses on designing to create positive emotional peaks and endings that leave lasting impressions on users.
- [[2019-12-17_storytelling-in-design_01-preface]] — Dahlström emphasizes the emotional core of storytelling: great stories evoke emotions, move audiences, and hold them captive through anticipation and connection.
- [[2019-12-17_storytelling-in-design_02-chapter-1-why-storytelling-matters]] — The chapter establishes that stories persuade and move people to action primarily through emotional connection; without emotional resonance, there is no compelling story, and this emotional power is what makes storytelling so effective as a tool in design and business.
- [[2019-12-17_storytelling-in-design_03-chapter-2-the-anatomy-of-a-great-story]] — The power of great stories, across all mediums examined (Disney parks, Pixar films, video games, public speaking), stems from their ability to create emotional connection; designing for emotional resonance—not just functionality—is what transforms a product experience into a memorable story.
- [[2019-12-17_storytelling-in-design_05-chapter-4-the-emotional-aspect-of-product-design]] — Dahlström draws on Don Norman's *Emotional Design* (2004) to argue that products must evoke emotion to create connection and loyalty. She covers Norman's three emotional levels (visceral, behavioral, reflective) and shows how positive emotions build trust and forgiveness while negative emotions narrow cognition and reduce problem-solving flexibility. Emotional design is not a luxury detail, but in stressful contexts Dahlström stresses that pleasing aesthetics alone won't be enough: Norman and Walter both require the design to be usable as well.
- [[2019-12-17_storytelling-in-design_10-chapter-9-visualizing-the-shape-of-your-product-experience]] — The chapter shows that mapping experience statements across hygiene-feel good-delight levels creates a visual representation of emotional progression; Dahlström's argument against putting everything at the delight level is one of contrast, not of user fatigue: "delights" and "feel goods" wouldn't exist unless they were put in contrast with each other and with the hygiene factors.
