# Studio Economics

## Definition

Studio economics is the financial logic that makes a [[Venture Studio]] viable: a single organization pays upfront for a core team, shared infrastructure and the building of several startups in parallel, and expects to recover that outlay years later through equity in the companies that survive. Both books describe the same asymmetry. Costs are front-loaded and paid in cash (payroll, infrastructure, validation, initial builds), while the return arrives late, as exit proceeds on equity obtained at near-zero valuation. Szigeti frames the model as a more efficient way to build startups: a larger equity stake than a VC's minority position, cost efficiency from spreading specialised talent across a portfolio, and failure that is recoverable because the team is reassigned rather than lost ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_03-startup-studios-in-a-nutshell]]). Kannan and Peterman reach a sharper formulation from their Stanford interviews and financial model: studios "tend to be a story of economic ownership levels, not superior ideation", and their attractive returns come from high founder-level ownership in many companies, not from better ideas ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_24-studios-and-innovation]], [[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_23-implications-for-stakeholders]]).

The concept therefore covers a set of interlocking variables rather than a single number: how much capital the studio raises for its own operations, what it costs to produce one launched company, the pass-through rates of the [[Studio Funnel]], the equity it keeps and how that stake dilutes, whether it generates intermediate revenue, whether it runs a separate investment fund, and when exits arrive. Kannan and Peterman build an explicit spreadsheet around these levers ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_15-model-overview]]); Szigeti offers a shorter method for constructing one ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_19-a-preview-on-financial-modeling]]) and a set of first-hand numbers from Drukka, Studioone.hu and TechnoSpark. The two works differ in vantage point (a practitioner's playbook from 2019 versus a 19-studio research project from 2022) and in several of their figures, notably on equity, and this page keeps both.

## Practice

### The structural problem: front-loaded costs, late returns

Every source that touches the topic starts from the same cash-flow gap. Company-building expenses and salaries are paid upfront, while exits typically materialise years later ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_10-revenue-generation]]). Kannan and Peterman's base case, a single entity with no fund and no revenue, funded by equity alone, runs at a loss for five or more years, burns $1.3M to $3.2M a year until exits begin, and needs about $10M of upfront capital; exits start in year 6 and generate roughly $22M per company once they commence ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_15-model-overview]]). Once exits roll in, the cash generated can be enough to sustain operations and be reinvested in the portfolio.

Szigeti describes the same horizon from the founder's chair: the point of the model is to work out how much money is needed monthly to keep the core team engaged and building ventures until the first startup success brings home a big return, which "can take years" ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_19-a-preview-on-financial-modeling]]). Kannan and Peterman add that the challenge is more widespread than expected: even studios that had fundraised still struggled to maintain funding ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_10-revenue-generation]]), and that securing enough capital to launch operations is the most stressful part of a studio founder's job, with upfront capital from patient LPs the option most aligned with the mission ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_23-implications-for-stakeholders]]). The strategy chapter names the third ingredient: studios absorb front-loaded costs while needing patient capital, and the cheap founding equity that justifies the model is heavily diluted before exit ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_05-strategy]]).

### Why the model is supposed to work

Szigeti's chapter on the model in a nutshell lists the economic arguments for studios ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_03-startup-studios-in-a-nutshell]]):

- **Larger stakes, larger returns.** Studios take stakes often exceeding 50%, so an exit returns substantially more than a VC's minority position.
- **Talent allocation.** Centralised teams are distributed across startups as needs evolve; a studio has a proportionally larger core-team budget than any single startup and can afford senior people whose skills it spreads across the portfolio.
- **Recoverable failure.** With several startups in parallel, failure is an expected outcome; when one fails, the team and expertise move to another concept instead of being lost.
- **Broader entrepreneur pool.** The model motivates people who would not otherwise launch a startup, including those who cannot afford to go without a salary for a year or two.

The same chapter lists the risks: capital intensity, cap-table skepticism (founders with lower ownership), investor wariness of first-time studios, doubts about founder authenticity, and internal competition for resources between parallel startups.

Kannan and Peterman describe the mechanism differently. The original studio advantage is cheap founding equity at a near-zero valuation, which can produce strong returns even on exits of 50 to 100x ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_05-strategy]]); high initial ownership at low cost means exits of only $50M or $100M may still return 50x or 100x ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_24-studios-and-innovation]]). One interviewed studio frames the investor pitch as a higher "batting average" and fewer zero outcomes, which reduces reliance on a single monster winner and, it claims, produces better risk-adjusted returns; whether that advantage persists at scale is, in the authors' words, unproven ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_23-implications-for-stakeholders]]).

The economics also shape what studios build. Because the model depends on failing fast and preserving runway, studios cannot afford the time and cost of disruptive innovation; they design within the existing technology frontier, apply proven business models to new problem areas, and concentrate on B2B SaaS and niche needs ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_24-studios-and-innovation]]). See [[Innovation]] and [[Portfolio Strategy]].

### How to build the model

Szigeti's method is a two-level spreadsheet ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_19-a-preview-on-financial-modeling]]). First model one startup's cash flow, then aggregate startups into a funnel model. Before either, decide the studio's focus, the typical startup profile, regional wage expectations, and the financial agreement between studio and portfolio companies about cost tracking and resource charging. The inputs he lists:

- Time and resources for idea generation, idea validation (landing pages, market research) and the initial build, including core-team and infrastructure cost.
- Pass-through rates at each stage: ideas to validated ideas, validated ideas to built products, built products to seed funding. Small changes in these rates move overall cash flow dramatically.
- Funding strategy: whether the studio or external investors finance seed rounds.
- Success rates and pass-through rates applied to portfolio value over time, cap-table progression, dilution across rounds and typical ESOP sizes.
- Monthly burn for core team and infrastructure, multiplied by the time expected before the first major exit, which gives the capital to raise or self-fund.

He also lists the ways a founder can fund those years: self-fund from personal wealth, raise from VCs, use agency revenue to subsidise ventures, or require portfolio companies to raise seed before the studio exits (see [[Team Compensation]]).

Kannan and Peterman's model is built around a base case and four independent levers: revenue generation, raising a fund, equity ownership, and exit timing and distribution ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_15-model-overview]]). The base case assumes a single entity launching 7 companies a year by year 10 (4 from internal ideation, 3 from EIRs), a 30% exit success rate and a $280M average exit value. Fundraising, equity level and exit timing are the highest-impact levers on IRR and cash balance. The book's introduction describes the same variables at a higher level, funding, team size, ideation rate and pass-through rates, and invites readers to modify the model themselves ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_01-introduction]]). The two methods agree on the structural inputs (funnel rates, burn, funding strategy, dilution); Kannan and Peterman go further by attaching modelled dollar outcomes to each lever.

### Capital requirements and unit costs

The numbers on capital come from different scales and years, and the page keeps them side by side.

**Szigeti (Drukka, Hungary, mid-2010s)** ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_13-stretching-our-limits]]):

- EUR 100,000 of initial capital, with 10,000 to 20,000 per initiative (the record reports this figure in both euros and dollars). This frugality forced the studio to begin with low-risk copycat ideas in the sharing economy before proprietary concepts.
- Burning most of that capital launched a dozen initiatives, of which almost half reached a seed-ready stage.
- Studioone.hu, the pre-seed fund launched in 2017 with outside co-investors, invests 20,000 to 40,000 per startup with Drukka providing all other resources; from EUR 1 million raised, the fund could build 30 to 40 startups while holding 65 to 75% equity.
- Scaling from 6 or 7 concurrent ventures to a dozen a year came from batching startups by industry, joint mentoring for related CEOs and code-base reuse.

**Kannan and Peterman (19 studios, mostly US, 2022)**:

- Operating companies typically raise $10M to $20M, with a few outliers at $50M, from a small LP base of high-net-worth individuals and family offices who accept no exit guarantee and patience over 5 to 7 years; the model is a new asset class that investors used to VC structures find hard to back ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_13-studio-financing]]).
- The fully-loaded cost is $250K to $700K per launched company including failed ideas, with spin-out costs of $350K to $415K ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_16-revenue-generation]]).
- The base case needs $10M upfront ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_15-model-overview]]).

The gap between Drukka's tens of thousands per initiative and the research model's hundreds of thousands per launched company reflects different geographies, eras and what is being counted (a validation initiative versus a fully-loaded spin-out), and neither source reconciles them.

### Cost reduction

Both books treat headcount as the main cost and both point to ways of shrinking it.

- **Outsource development.** Personnel costs for full in-house engineering, product and design teams can exceed what management fees support, or strain operating budgets, and technical talent is hard to move between studio and ventures. Many studios therefore outsource development to third-party agencies, often partner networks at discounted rates, funding it from the operating budget for pre-venture work or from a venture's invested capital afterwards, and some have moved to leaner teams focused on ideation, recruiting and administration ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_09-third-party-agencies]]). See [[Studio Structure]] and [[Studio Team]].
- **Gate spending early.** Add go/no-go gates that cap spending on an idea at $50K to $100K before a go-ahead, outsource MVP development, or replicate a proven business model across geographies ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_10-revenue-generation]]).
- **Share the core team.** Szigeti's argument is that a studio's per-startup payroll shrinks because specialised roles are spread across ventures ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_03-startup-studios-in-a-nutshell]]); Drukka's cheap validation (3 to 5 bare-minimum concept tests per incoming CEO, offer only, no product) filtered ideas before any build ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_13-stretching-our-limits]]).
- **Hardware: a dual structure.** TechnoSpark's stated aim is to radically reduce the end cost of building a startup by cutting the 15 to 20 years an average hardware company takes by 2 to 3 times; it puts 70% of investment into contract-service companies (high CapEx, immediate revenue, supplying manufacturing and components) and 30% into product companies (low OpEx, revenue reinvested), with hyperspecialised companies each solving a narrow problem faster than a vertically integrated competitor ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_06-technospark-a-conveyor-of-hardware-startups]]).

### Intermediate revenue

Kannan and Peterman document three ways studios bridge the gap between upfront costs and exits ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_10-revenue-generation]]); Szigeti's list of funding options overlaps on the agency route ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_19-a-preview-on-financial-modeling]]). The detailed treatment lives at [[Revenue Model]]; the economics are summarised here.

- **Billing back launched companies** is the preferred approach because it does not distract leadership from venture building. Studios bill at one of three stages: not before incorporation, after incorporation but before spin-out (partial services), or only after spin-out. The authors found no correlation between aggressive billing back and a lower equity stake, suggesting founders do not treat service fees as a major factor. In the model, billing post-incorporation services at cost cuts capital needs from $10M to $7M (35%) if incorporation follows validation, and to $5M (50%) if billing starts pre-incorporation, a variant rarely adopted because founders resist it; billing only post-incorporation without MVP-stage costs recouped just $68K ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_16-revenue-generation]]). Incorporation timing is therefore both a financial and a founder-relations decision.
- **Corporate co-builds** charge 30 to 50% margins above cost according to the interview chapter, with the studio taking 30 to 35% equity in the co-built company ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_10-revenue-generation]]); the modelling chapter uses a 40% margin, ramping from 1 to 4 co-builds a year by year 5, which reduces capital needs from $10M to $9M (17%) while diverting team time from internal spin-outs ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_16-revenue-generation]]). Because most studios use the same people for corporate and internal work, co-builds are a direct trade-off against launching internal companies. See [[Corporate Venture Building]].
- **Corporate consulting** was pursued by only 1 of 19 studios; it earns about 40% margins but runs counter to the company-building mission and requires sourcing and executing hard contracts ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_10-revenue-generation]]).
- **Combined**, billing back after validation plus co-builds saves 39% of capital and caps annual burn at $2.1M, at the price of exit-profile uncertainty and team-capacity management ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_16-revenue-generation]]).

Szigeti's chapter on charging models sets the constraint from the other side: if a studio bills its startups for resources, the rate must be low enough that using the studio is cheaper than hiring full-time employees, yet high enough for the studio to sustain itself without investors seeing overcharging ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_19-a-preview-on-financial-modeling]]).

### Raising a separate fund

The strongest lever in Kannan and Peterman's model is a dual-entity structure with an investment fund alongside the operating company. In the strategy chapter, 11 of 19 interviewed studios had already raised a fund and 4 more planned to; management fees offset operating expenses and extend runway, and pro-rata participation lets a studio hold a target stake (often 20%) through later rounds, which, despite paying higher valuations, yields significantly higher absolute returns, potentially $22M per exit. About half of studios also use their fund for external investments, a convergence toward VC economics ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_05-strategy]]).

The modelled version assumes four close-ended funds over a decade, from $45M to $120M, with 3-year horizons, 2% management fees and 20% carry ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_17-raising-a-fund]]):

- The operating company's incremental capital need falls from $10M to $4M and it stays near break-even through year 5, because fees subsidise operations.
- Carry, if reinvested in the holding company, accelerates growth without more external fundraising.
- Fund plus operating company return 37% IRR and 24x MoM, against 24% IRR and 9x for the operating company alone.
- With one early exit, cumulative capital required drops to $24M and the operating company reaches 50% IRR and 57x MoM.

The operating company and the fund are financed from separate LP pools, the fund usually commanding the larger and more diverse base ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_13-studio-financing]]). Szigeti's Studioone.hu is the same idea at a much smaller scale: a pre-seed fund co-financed by Drukka and outside investors, offering co-investors deal flow and portfolio exposure ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_13-stretching-our-limits]]). See [[Studio Fundraising]] and [[Venture Capital]].

### Equity and dilution

This is where the two books diverge most, partly because the market moved between them. The full treatment is at [[Equity Ownership]]; this section keeps the economic consequences.

- **Szigeti (2019)** presents stakes often exceeding 50% as a defining feature of the model and the reason for its higher returns, justified by the studio's role as active co-founder ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_03-startup-studios-in-a-nutshell]]); Studioone.hu's structure holds 65 to 75% ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_13-stretching-our-limits]]). He records that early investors feared cap-table dilution and CEO motivation problems, and that Drukka answered with traction: spin-offs achieving 13x valuation increases at seed and 6 to 8x during Series A negotiations.
- **Kannan and Peterman (2022)** report that majority ownership is now the exception: 25 to 40% is the most common range, only 4 of 19 studios take a majority, and studios that kept high stakes struggled because they could not recruit top founders and their cap tables confused VCs. Stakes vary per venture from 5 to 50% depending on who generated the idea, founder experience, founder capital, salary and role; studios with large funds can hold 20 to 35% through Series A and B, but most see dilution ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_14-equity-ownership]]). The strategy chapter puts founding stakes at 30 to 45% and warns they can fall to 5% at exit ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_05-strategy]]).

The modelled cap table starts at 30% pre-seed and dilutes to 19% at seed, 15% at Series A, 12% at Series B, 9% at Series C and 8% at Series D ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_18-equity-ownership]]). Its sensitivities:

- A 10% increase in round sizes across all stages cuts exit returns from $22M to $19M per company and the year-10 cash balance by $15M (17%); a 20% increase drops exits to $16M and the gap to $28M (31%).
- Without a fund, a studio needs at least 10% founding equity to justify the model. At 10% it can operate but cannot return money to investors; at 8%, the break-even point, dilution reaches 1% by Series D and each exit brings only $3.6M, just covering costs.
- The conclusion restates the floor and adds an exit condition: at 10% founding equity, companies must exit at about $60M on average for the studio to break even in perpetuity ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_22-part-5-conclusion]]).
- As competition for EIRs and founders intensifies, founding stakes are expected to decline toward the 10% floor, with established studios keeping a premium ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_18-equity-ownership]]).

For the entrepreneur, the trade is founding equity and early autonomy against expertise, capital and structured ideation; Kannan and Peterman note that founders may grant co-founder-level shares for services that are paid for at pre-seed and often cease to be available later in the company's life ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_23-implications-for-stakeholders]]).

### Exits and the best-in-class case

Exit timing and distribution are a high-impact lever in their own right ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_15-model-overview]]; see [[Exit Strategy]]). The best-in-class scenario combines every lever: billing back post-incorporation costs, corporate co-build revenue, one intentional early exit via secondary sale ($75M in year 4), realistic exit distribution and a dual-entity fund investing 20% of follow-on rounds through Series B at 2% fees and 20% carry ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_20-best-in-class-case]]). The results:

- The studio needs to raise only $200K in year 1, because the early exit covers operations through year 6.
- Operating-company IRR reaches 100% with over 1000x MoM if all profits were distributed, while keeping enough cash to run.
- The four funds ($45M, $89M, $117M, $117M) add $91M of cash by year 10 and reach 26% gross IRR and 10x MoM by year 13.
- Combined cash reaches $255M by year 10, against $90M in the base case.

The conclusion compares those figures with venture capital: studios such as High Alpha, Atomic and Founders realised 100% IRR at the operating company and 26% at the fund, versus an average VC IRR of 21.9% for 2010 to 2016 and a top quartile of 25.6% ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_22-part-5-conclusion]]). Szigeti's evidence is at portfolio level rather than studio level: seed-funded companies (Rendi.hu, Neatly.hu, Tickething.com, EzExpress.hu) reached seed rounds within 18 to 24 months, and the 13x and 6 to 8x valuation steps were what convinced investors ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_13-stretching-our-limits]]).

### Caveats the sources attach to their own numbers

- Kannan and Peterman's data comes mostly from successful studios; returns from failed studios are scarce. They estimate an annual studio failure rate of about 1.3% (18 of 700) but note that many surviving studios may not be delivering sustainable returns, so public performance reports are likely biased upward ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_22-part-5-conclusion]], [[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_01-introduction]]). Their sample is 63% US-based.
- They find no single superior structure: studios succeed with open- or closed-ended funds, single or dual entities, structured or ad-hoc founder programs, and the right choice depends on philosophy, local environment and fundraising constraints ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_23-implications-for-stakeholders]]).
- Whether the asset class is attractive depends on who is asking; top performers offer outsized returns but success is not guaranteed ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_22-part-5-conclusion]]).
- Szigeti's figures are one studio's history in one market, and his modelling chapter is explicitly a preview, a short guide to estimating costs, revenues, valuations and returns rather than a finished model ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_19-a-preview-on-financial-modeling]]).
