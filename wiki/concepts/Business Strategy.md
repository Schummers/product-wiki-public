# Business Strategy

## Definition

Business strategy, across these sources, is the set of choices that fix what an organisation is trying to become, which market it attacks and with what advantage, and how its resources are then allocated. The design-side sources treat it as the frame a product or UX team must plug into. Parlons Design, discussing Richard Rumelt's *Good Strategy, Bad Strategy*, describes strategy as a diagnosis, a guiding line and an action plan, and argues that the transition from UX designer to product designer consists precisely in integrating the company's global objectives into design work ([[2024-12-31_358_Top_3_des_livres_pour_Designers_en_2025]]). Nielsen Norman Group adds two organisational mechanisms: a vision statement that informs UX strategy and guides which initiatives get resources ([[2022-02-06_ux-vision-statements]]), and a customer-experience transformation that requires vision, strategy and leadership to be aligned around the customer ([[2021-03-14_framework-cx-transformation]]).

The venture-studio books use the term for a different object: the strategy of a company-building machine. *Startup Studio Playbook* frames studio planning as designing a factory whose product is the startup, and prescribes market focus where founders hold insider knowledge and customer access, high-margin segments, and solutions for proven market needs rather than untested ideas ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_15-vision-and-strategy]]). *Venture Studios Demystified* shows business-model selection, chiefly B2B SaaS, acting as a strategic foundation because it is scalable, recurring and repeatable ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_07-industry-focus]]), and describes studios as deliberately applying business models proven elsewhere to new problem areas, a choice that reduces innovation risk and fits their economics of failing fast and preserving runway ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_24-studios-and-innovation]]). A design team's business strategy is thus the context it aligns to; a studio's business strategy is the portfolio and market bet it makes.

*The 10x Method* puts the same word at a third level, the founding team's own commitments before the company exists. Chapter 2 defines a startup idea as a three-dimensional framework, problem, solution and go-to-market strategy, and holds that excelling in any one of those dimensions, go-to-market included, can create substantial market value and differentiation ([[2026-03-05_10x-method_02-finding-and-validating-the-idea]]). Chapter 1 makes geography one of those early commitments rather than a later expansion decision, listing global thinking from day one among the ten founder traits Hexa observed across more than a hundred founders ([[2026-03-05_10x-method_01-becoming-a-10x-founder]]). Strategy here is neither the frame a design team plugs into nor a portfolio bet, but the set of choices a founder fixes while the idea is still being scoped.

*Product Management for UX People* adds a fourth level again, the individual product's business: Christian Crumlish argues that sustainable value has to accrue to both the customer and the business, since a business that goes under takes the product with it, and that the balance is missed in both directions, by raking too much value off the top and by leaving too much on the table ([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_08-chapter-5-the-business-of-product-is-business]]). The strategic material there is market sizing, go-to-market and the specific rules of B2B; in the money chapter it becomes P&L literacy, revenue lifecycles and the trade-off between growth and profitability ([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_11-chapter-8-getting-the-money]]).

## Practice

### Strategy as diagnosis, guiding line, action plan (podcast)

[[2024-12-31_358_Top_3_des_livres_pour_Designers_en_2025]] recommends *Good Strategy, Bad Strategy* to designers because Rumelt's structure (diagnosis, guiding policy, plan of action) resonates strongly with design methodology. The episode's claim is that product design differs from UX design by integrating the company's strategy and its financial objectives on top of user experience, and that stepping outside pure design reading (into AI, strategy, alternative growth models) is where designers find useful material. The same episode points to *Company of One* (Paul Jarvis) as a challenge to the pursuit of infinite growth, proposing small, healthy and stable companies or teams as an alternative model, especially applicable to freelancers and autonomous teams. See [[Product Design]] and [[Sustainable Growth]].

### Vision statements as the link between strategy and UX priorities (NN/g)

[[2022-02-06_ux-vision-statements]] positions the vision statement as the instrument through which strategy reaches UX work:

- **Mission versus vision.** A mission statement answers what collective value the organisation generates now; a vision statement answers what it wants to become in n years. Both inform UX strategy and should be checked against goals and objectives.
- **Two kinds of vision.** A product vision describes the impact and value a product will provide to users; a team vision describes the value a team provides to the company, useful during rapid growth or cultural shifts.
- **A three-stage process.** Stage I gathers research on the current state (stakeholder interviews on processes and obstacles, usability testing if no research exists, competitive analysis). Stage II envisions in collaborative workshops: sharing findings, brainstorming and prioritising future-state attributes with coloured dots (agree, disagree, surprising, essential), then crafting the statement from the top attributes. Stage III plans the resources needed to bridge the gap (processes, people, tools and technology, governance) with the "Requisite Roundup" method.
- **Communication decides whether the vision lives.** Even a well-built vision fails if it is not shared and visible; engaging artefacts (documents, videos, vision boards, prototypes) build momentum and hold the team accountable. A strong facilitator is required to drive the process.

### Aligning the organisation around the customer (NN/g)

[[2021-03-14_framework-cx-transformation]] treats strategy as one of four organisational areas that must change together for customer experience to be delivered at scale: vision and strategy, employees, operations, and technology. Four building blocks implement the change: leadership and governance (a clear customer-focused vision statement, committed leadership, defined governance), experience-design operations (shared standards, processes and playbooks), customer insights and metrics (ongoing behaviour data, feedback and sentiment feeding decisions), and culture management (training and change management, because silos persist without intentional cultural change). The article's diagnosis is that most companies were built for in-person or phone business, and decades of operational investment created silos that a customer-focused strategy has to break. See [[Customer Experience]].

### Planning a studio like a factory (book)

[[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_15-vision-and-strategy]] lays out six strategic steps for a studio: core principles and personal constraints; market focus and startup profile; funnel processes (ideation, validation, build, scaling); core team and entrepreneur-in-residence recruitment; financial modelling and fundraising strategy; and a reality check before pitching investors. Its strategic prescriptions:

- **One metric that matters.** Cumulative revenue of the startups plus the studio, not vanity metrics such as startup count or user numbers. Revenue signals that products solve real needs, that the studio can sell, that the platform scales, that prioritisation is disciplined, and that fundraising leverage is strong.
- **Insider markets.** Focus on markets where founders have personal experience, network and potential customer access; entering an unknown market without industry understanding is the riskier path.
- **High margins.** Choose segments where the studio can differentiate and avoid competing on price; high margins fund team scaling beyond agency workload and create capacity to build the studio's own startups.
- **Proven needs, first paying client.** Follow client money and build for demonstrated problems, knowing who the first paying customer will be, rather than idealistic "Uber for X" concepts. Commitment runs 3 to 5, up to 7 years before profitability.
- **Four characteristics of a good studio.** Sustainability (multiple startups over years), efficiency (economies of scale and knowledge reuse), viable startups (revenue and growth), and antifragility (resource recycling from failures).
- **Reality check.** Before fundraising, confirm alignment with personal preferences, an unfair advantage, access to key people, initial capital, a realistic financial model, knowledge of follow-on investors and acquirers, and genuine willingness to spend 5, 10 or 15 years building ventures.

See [[Studio Strategy]], [[Portfolio Strategy]] and [[Idea Validation]].

### Business-model focus: B2B SaaS and industry agnosticism (book)

[[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_07-industry-focus]] reports that roughly half of studios declare a specific focus and half remain industry agnostic. The constraint driving agnosticism is the ideation funnel: 30 to 100 ideas per launched company, sourced only from the studio's own staff, so a narrow specialisation exhausts opportunities quickly. Focus follows founder expertise: founders with deep domain knowledge specialise, others stay generalist. Among specialised studios, B2B SaaS is the most common choice (4 of 19 interviewed) because subscriptions and business contracts lock in recurring revenue and, once fit is reached, scaling follows a playbook. The authors expect de-specialisation to increase as competition for ideas rises. See [[Venture Studio]] and [[Studio Funnel]].

### Execution over innovation (book)

[[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_24-studios-and-innovation]] argues that studios succeed through execution within economic constraints rather than through superior innovation. Because their model rests on failing fast and preserving runway, they cannot afford the cost and time of disruptive technology; they design within the technology frontier and apply business models proven in other industries to their target problem areas, concentrating on B2B SaaS and niche needs. High initial ownership at low cost means exits of 50 to 100 million dollars can still return 50x or 100x, which is what makes this strategy pay. The limiting factor on the market is the supply of quality entrepreneurs, not studio capacity or ideation. See [[Innovation]] and [[Studio Economics]].

### Thinking globally from day one (book)

[[2026-03-05_10x-method_01-becoming-a-10x-founder]] treats internationalisation as a decision taken at the start, not an expansion phase reached later: hire internationally, run English as the internal language, design products for cross-border scalability, and seek international design partners rather than optimising for the local market alone. The chapter's other strategic instruments are temporal rather than geographic. A clear five-year vision serves as a compass but must be grounded in specific execution this week, each week acting as a sprint that tests and validates one hypothesis, with quarterly OKRs to reset direction. And the chapter asks founders to be missionaries driven by impact and long-term value creation rather than mercenaries chasing a quick exit, on the argument that purpose is what fuels resilience and attracts team members and investors who believe in the same vision. See [[Founder Mindset]] and [[Operating Rituals]].

### Go-to-market as a dimension of the idea (book)

[[2026-03-05_10x-method_02-finding-and-validating-the-idea]] refuses the conventional "1% idea, 99% execution" split and makes strategy part of the idea itself. A startup idea is not a one-liner but a framework combining the problem (a specific pain point), the solution (the product to build) and the go-to-market strategy (how customers find and use it), which evolves through research, feedback and testing. Every successful startup, the chapter argues, innovates in at least one of three areas: problem discovery, solution creation, or go-to-market strategy. A venture can therefore win primarily on go-to-market while the problem and the solution are already known.

- **Go-to-market is written down at scoping time.** The chapter's scoping document covers eight dimensions: summary, problem, solution (MVP plus long-term vision), implementation, go-to-market, market size, opportunity (why now), competition, and hypotheses. Go-to-market at that stage means personas, the acquisition channels for the first 100 customers, the business model, and how go-to-market should evolve in the longer term. Writing is what surfaces gaps and unexamined assumptions.
- **Three categories of ideas, each with its own search.** Problem-solution fit drawn from personal frustration or industry expertise; a market or technology shift enabling a dramatically better solution to a known problem; and category creation through behavioural change. Each requires a different search strategy and different evidence.
- **Ideas belong to their time.** The chapter holds that ideas emerge at the intersection of cultural shifts, new technologies and evolving user behaviours, so the strategic act is recognising when an idea's moment has arrived and moving fast, not being first to think of it.

See [[Idea Validation]], [[Ideation]] and [[Innovation]].

### Sizing a market, and the illusion of "a mere 1%" (book)

[[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_08-chapter-5-the-business-of-product-is-business]] treats the market side as the part of strategy a UX practitioner most often refuses, and works it in four moves:

- **Define the target market specifically.** "Adults in the US aged 18–35 thinking about buying their first new car", not "everyone". A specific definition is what lets you craft experiences that actually reach the segment.
- **Size it with humility.** Reaching a million customers out of a hundred million is "not significantly easier (or even necessarily easier at all)" than reaching a million out of four million; the ease of capturing "a mere 1%" is an illusion produced by a huge denominator.
- **Go-to-market is orchestration, not a launch event.** It promotes awareness, enables discovery, casts the customer as the hero of their journey, coordinates with partners, produces marketing materials, sets pricing, and aligns personnel and budgets. The chapter's worked example is Jay Zaveri, then chief product officer at CloudOn, who validated demand for "Word on the iPad" with a cheap Google AdWords campaign ("Word is coming to the iPad! Sign up to be notified when it's here") and collected tens of thousands of email addresses before the product existed, a smokescreen test that also handed him a go-to-market advantage at launch.
- **Customer obsession is organised, not felt.** Formal syncs with customer support, customer success and community managers, informal back-channels, and survey, review, feedback and behaviour data treated as organisational gold.

The chapter also separates two modes that recruit different people: launching new products into the unknown, and molding and improving an existing one. Crumlish says a PM should know which of the two they are, while insisting the two tendencies are not mutually exclusive; Hà Phan of Pluralsight describes long seeing herself as someone who only starts things, until building a platform made it a long game in which she does both. See [[Product-Market Fit]] and [[Product Strategy]].

**B2B changes the rules.** In the same chapter: the end user is often not the payer; customers are specific named people at specific companies rather than the general public; user delight does not directly translate into ability or willingness to pay; and change management and training matter enormously, because the customer's employees have to be able to work with the product.

### The money side: P&L, revenue lifecycles, and the innovator's dilemma (book)

[[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_11-chapter-8-getting-the-money]] argues that money is a material a product manager works with, alongside code and pixels, and that this holds in nonprofits and government agencies too, which also spend money to build features. Most PMs do not control the P&L, but they must be acutely aware of the constraints it sets.

- **Revenue lifecycles shape strategy.** Products move through establishing (R&D costs, no revenue), growth, optimisation and plateau of an established stream, and eventual sunsetting when they no longer break even and have no viable future. Each stage demands a different proficiency: scaling revenue at launch, fine-tuning a well-oiled machine once established, managing legal and financial obligations during wind-down.
- **Sunsetting is a skill of its own.** Identifying decay patterns, winding down commitments, preserving legal and security obligations, and removing the product entirely from the business.
- **The innovator's dilemma, credited to Clay Christensen.** Crumlish frames it as a long-term risk of complacency: established revenue streams create risk aversion, incumbents cannot afford to cannibalise what they already earn, and that opening is what disruptive upstarts take.
- **Break-even is a pivot point, not an ending.** Reaching it at 7 Cups through paid therapy made the company "ramen profitable" and broke its venture-capital dependency, but the chapter says that was not sustainable long term, and opened three directions: organic growth with small bets, optimisation of the core model toward healthy sustainability, or a swing at a 100x breakthrough.

See [[Revenue Model]] and [[Sustainable Growth]].

### Where the sources diverge

- **Object of the strategy.** The podcast and NN/g describe how a product or design team reads and serves the company's strategy; the studio books describe how a studio sets its own. The word is the same, the level is not.
- **Growth.** Parlons Design relays *Company of One*'s challenge to infinite growth ([[2024-12-31_358_Top_3_des_livres_pour_Designers_en_2025]]); *Startup Studio Playbook* asks for resilient startups over fragile unicorns but still measures success by cumulative revenue and expects multi-year scaling ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_15-vision-and-strategy]]); *Venture Studios Demystified* frames strategy around venture-scale exits ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_24-studios-and-innovation]]). *The 10x Method* adds a fourth position, mission over exits: be a missionary driven by impact and long-term value creation rather than a mercenary chasing a quick exit ([[2026-03-05_10x-method_01-becoming-a-10x-founder]]). These are different growth ambitions, not one position. *Product Management for UX People* carries the argument inside a single chapter: Crumlish tells the story of reaching break-even as an achievement, and his own "From the Trenches" sidebar has B. Pagels-Minor, then a product manager at Netflix, calling break-even "pretty illogical in modern tech companies" given that Amazon and Netflix were deliberately unprofitable for years, and arguing the goal is more likely market penetration. Crumlish accepts the objection and asks the reader to treat his story as one particular path rather than the success goal ([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_11-chapter-8-getting-the-money]]).
- **Specialise or not.** *Startup Studio Playbook* prescribes focus on insider markets ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_15-vision-and-strategy]]); *Venture Studios Demystified* observes that half of studios stay agnostic for funnel reasons and predicts more will ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_07-industry-focus]]). The first is advice, the second is an observed trend, and they pull in opposite directions.
- **Innovation.** NN/g's transformation framework and the studio "execution over innovation" chapter agree that operating discipline beats novelty, but from different premises: organisational silos in one case, studio economics in the other. *The 10x Method* takes the opposite side: it explicitly challenges the "1% idea, 99% execution" wisdom and requires an idea to innovate in at least one of problem, solution or go-to-market before it is worth pursuing ([[2026-03-05_10x-method_02-finding-and-validating-the-idea]]).
