# Cognitive Psychology

## Definition

Cognitive psychology is treated in this corpus as the empirical foundation under interface design: the study of how attention, memory, perception, decision-making and sensemaking actually work, applied to predict and support how people behave in front of a system [[2024-01-10_psychology-study-guide]]. The framing is that UX is people, that no psychology degree is required to use the basics, and that most relevant principles are easy to understand yet make a large difference when applied correctly — with the stated commitment that designs should be built for people as they really are, not as designers wish they were [[2024-01-10_psychology-study-guide]]. The areas that study guide enumerates: attention (selective focus, change blindness), Gestalt principles, memory (recognition versus recall), sensemaking (mental models, information foraging), decision-making (loss aversion, satisficing, choice overload), motor processes (interaction cost, Fitts's Law), motivation (autonomy, competence, relatedness), cognitive biases (anchoring, availability heuristic, confirmation bias), persuasion (social proof, scarcity, authority), and emotion at the visceral, behavioral and reflective levels.

*Laws of UX* makes the same case from the designer's side: psychology is where empirical evidence for a design decision comes from when quantitative or qualitative research data is unavailable [[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_01-preface]]. That source is careful about the status of what it offers — the principles are guidelines informed by observed patterns of human behavior, not absolute laws to be followed rigidly, and they complement user research rather than replace it [[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_01-preface]]. Its organizing metaphor is a human "blueprint" for how we perceive and process the world, which studying psychology helps decipher and which lets designers build products adapted to people's actual capabilities and limitations rather than forcing users to adapt to the design [[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_01-preface]]. It also argues the payoff is durable: technology and interaction methods keep changing — the second edition names LLMs, AI-powered image generation and spatial computing — while the underlying principles governing human perception and behavior stay stable across eras and mediums [[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_01-preface]].

The connection is not new, and one chapter traces its lineage [[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_02-a-brief-history-of-psychology-and-design]]. Gestalt psychology in the early twentieth century established that humans perceive organized patterns rather than isolated sensory elements, giving design figure-ground, similarity, proximity and closure [[Gestalt Principles]]. Human factors engineering was founded by psychologists during the Second World War — Paul Fitts and Alphonse Chapanis among them — on the recognition that humans are error-prone, especially under stress, so machines should be designed to align with human capabilities instead of requiring users to adapt [[Human Factors Engineering]]. During the Cold War, psychology, systems analysis and computer science combined into the study of human-computer interaction; Card, Moran and Newell's *The Psychology of Human-Computer Interaction* (1983) established cognitive psychology as central to personal computing design at Xerox PARC, where Smalltalk's metaphorical desktop mimicked the way people organize physical documents on a desk. Douglas Engelbart's work at the Stanford Research Institute produced the mouse, hypertext and precursors to graphical user interfaces, while Alan Kay drew on educational psychology — Seymour Papert's Logo project, itself shaped by five years alongside Jean Piaget, and Jerome Bruner's study of learning mentalities in children. Donald Norman then coined "user experience" for all aspects of a person's interaction with a system, and Jane Fulton Suri pioneered ethnographic research and human-centered design at IDEO [[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_02-a-brief-history-of-psychology-and-design]].

In practice the corpus uses it two ways. First as explanation: why a given design works or fails is traced to a documented mechanism — dual-coding theory for why pictures are remembered better than words [[2024-04-26_picture-superiority-effect]], working-memory capacity for why an interface overloads people [[2018-04-29_working-memory-external-memory]], selective attention for why ads and anything resembling them are skipped [[2018-04-22_banner-blindness-old-and-new-findings]], evolutionary wiring for threat detection behind negativity bias [[2016-10-23_negativity-bias-ux]]. Second as leverage: priming, authority, and commitment-and-consistency are presented as effects a designer can deliberately use — each with an explicit ethical boundary attached [[2016-01-24_priming]] [[2018-02-04_authority-principle]] [[2018-03-04_commitment-consistency-ux]].

The same psychology is not aimed only at end users. It is also turned on the colleagues and stakeholders who review a design: Parkinson's Law of Triviality explains why they spend their energy on the visible and trivial rather than the complex, and the need to feel heard explains why a change is requested at all [[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_11-chapter-10-dealing-with-changes]].

## Practice

### Attention is limited and selective

People direct attention only to a subset of the stimuli around them, usually those related to their goals [[2018-04-22_banner-blindness-old-and-new-findings]]. Banner blindness is the canonical case: users have learned across three decades to skip top banners and right rails, and the blindness is triggered by three cues — location, ad-like visual treatment (animation, fancy formatting, colored backgrounds, embedded text), and proximity to real ads. The design consequences from that source: legitimate content must not look like an ad or sit where ads live, because it will be ignored regardless of its value; and the "hot potato" effect means a single ad can poison an entire page section, since users assume everything there is advertising. Mobile is worse, because screen size makes ads harder to distinguish from content while large ads catch the eye despite the user's intent to ignore them.

The same limit produces change blindness: changes occurring outside the region receiving focal attention go unnoticed [[2024-01-10_psychology-study-guide]]. Tunnel vision and selective attention are named as the general condition [[2024-01-10_psychology-study-guide]].

*Laws of UX* frames the same limit as a capacity constraint with a design counter-measure: our ability to focus is limited in both capacity and duration, so we attend to relevant information to the detriment of the rest, which is what produces banner blindness and change blindness alike [[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_10-8-von-restorff-effect]]. Its consequence is that a change the user needs to notice will not be noticed for free — the designer has to draw attention to it deliberately, through contrast in color, shape, size, position or motion [[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_10-8-von-restorff-effect]] [[Von Restorff Effect]]. That source attaches two limits to the technique. Too much contrast is worse than none: it dilutes the very elements meant to stand out and visually overwhelms people [[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_10-8-von-restorff-effect]]. And contrast has to be accessible — WCAG asks for at least 4.5:1 between text and background, 3:1 for large (18pt+) or bold (14pt+) text; color must never be the only differentiation, for people with color vision deficiency; and motion-based contrast has to account for vestibular disorders [[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_10-8-von-restorff-effect]] [[Accessibility]].

### Memory: working memory, chunking, and offloading

Working memory is described as a limited-capacity buffer or scratchpad where the mind deposits information relevant to the current task; when an interface demands more than fits, users dump information, which produces errors or longer task times [[2018-04-29_working-memory-external-memory]]. Capacity is given as roughly seven chunks held briefly, varying by individual, education and age. That source raises a specific bias risk: programmers tend to have larger working-memory capacity than typical users through self-selection, so a feature that feels easy to a developer may overload most people. Its prescription is external memory — comparison tables, shopping carts, open tabs, note-taking, page parking — as virtual scratchpads that let users offload rather than hold, and the general rule that users should be able to reach all information a task needs without committing it to memory. Mobile carries a penalty here, since smaller screens show less external memory at once and force scrolling to retrieve what a larger screen would have kept visible.

Chunking is the complementary technique: grouping related information into distinct meaningful units improves comprehension and memorability compared with an undifferentiated mass of atomic items [[2016-03-20_chunking]]. That source insists the "magical number seven" is widely misread — Miller's finding was that people remember about seven chunks, not that interfaces should be limited to seven options, and menus with more than seven items work fine when well structured; what determines retention is whether the grouping is meaningful, not the count. Its concrete guidance: mark chunk boundaries visually with headings, white space, highlighting and background colors; keep line lengths at 50–75 characters and paragraphs short to support scanning; and chunk data in conventional formats (credit cards as 4111 1111 1111 1111, phone numbers as +65-5555-5555), with auto-formatting in input fields.

Images enjoy a memory advantage the corpus attributes to Paivio's dual-coding theory: a picture is stored twice, as image and as a word or phrase describing it, while a word is stored only once — the mind can generate an image from a word, but not automatically and only at significant cognitive cost [[2024-04-26_picture-superiority-effect]]. The strength of the effect depends on discoverability (how long the visual is visible), clarity (concrete beats abstract), familiarity and uniqueness. The strategies given: put important visuals where users spend time, such as the homepage; place information-carrying visuals above the fold; make them persist rather than auto-rotate; prefer literal, realistic images over abstract ones that cost effort to interpret; and choose imagery distinct both from competitors and from surrounding elements while keeping visual cohesion. Text is not displaced by this — the source says labels add redundancy that strengthens comprehension and memorability, and that text remains essential to usability. The study guide states the general memory rule as recognition over recall, with chunking, priming and spatial memory as supports [[2024-01-10_psychology-study-guide]].

Isolation is a third memory lever. Hedwig von Restorff's 1933 study found that participants best remembered the item that was distinctly different within an otherwise categorically similar set, and the corpus carries that into interfaces as the claim that visual isolation improves recognition and recall [[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_10-8-von-restorff-effect]] [[Von Restorff Effect]].

### Automatic processing and first impressions

Two systems are distinguished: System 1, fast, automatic and pattern-based, operating largely outside conscious awareness; System 2, deliberate, slow and effortful [[2017-10-01_first-impressions-human-automaticity]]. System 1 makes snap judgments about relevance, aesthetics and credibility from initial visual cues — users scan for what looks relevant using color, size and position, and ignore information that looks irrelevant even when it would have helped. Aesthetic judgment is reported as made within 50 milliseconds and as rarely changing afterwards, with users more forgiving of beautiful designs and inclined to remember an attractive site as more usable than it was. The source also invokes BJ Fogg's prominence-interpretation theory: the more prominent an element, the greater its effect on overall credibility, so a distracting ad diminishes trust while an endorsement raises it. The design implications: identify what each persona needs at each stage and make that information *look* important through hierarchy, contrast and positioning; keep the design visually simple; and state clearly who you are and what you do, because forcing users into System 2 to work out whether they are in the right place makes them scrutinize and distrust the site.

*Laws of UX* corroborates that account and supplies the evidence behind it [[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_09-7-aestheticusability-effect]]. It attributes the System 1 / System 2 framework to Daniel Kahneman — System 1 working quickly and automatically to identify relevant information, System 2 engaging for complex problem-solving — repeats the 50-millisecond figure for forming an opinion of a website, and locates the aesthetic-usability effect squarely at the System 1 level: the visceral judgment of whether something is beautiful is made almost immediately and rarely changes as users spend more time. Its origin study is Kurosu and Kashimura at Hitachi in 1995, who tested 26 ATM layout patterns with 252 participants on a 10-point scale and found apparent usability less correlated with inherent ease of use than with apparent beauty. Sonderegger and Sauer (2010) reproduced the effect with two functionally identical mobile phone simulations: 60 adolescents rated the more attractive version as more usable and completed tasks faster [[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_09-7-aestheticusability-effect]] [[Aesthetic-Usability Effect]].

Priming is the related effect that exposure to one stimulus shapes the response to a later, different one [[2016-01-24_priming]]. In interfaces, elements prime behavior even when the user had no such intent — a prominent coupon-code field primes users to abandon checkout and go hunting for promo codes, costing sales. Images and page content prime expectations about what the site is: when those expectations are confirmed, the experience feels smooth and intuitive; when violated, users perceive poor usability even though the interface itself has not changed. Aesthetic treatment primes unconscious inferences about the business, with a less polished design reading as local authenticity and a polished one as corporate establishment. The source's own position is that priming should be used deliberately, to bias people toward behaviors and expectations that match both the site's goals and the users' needs. The 2016 year-in-review restates priming in the same terms: initial design elements shape perception of later content [[2017-01-01_ux-quiz-16]].

Priming also cuts against the researcher [[2016-01-24_priming]]. Task wording that uses terminology already present in the interface primes participants to hunt for those exact terms, which can produce a false conclusion that information is easy to find; and facilitator behavior — excessive friendliness, verbosity, word choice, demonstrations, physically redirecting a participant — primes how people behave and degrades reliability. Controlling this is treated as a validity requirement, not a nicety.

The aesthetic-usability effect contaminates research the same way, and from the opposite direction: because a beautiful interface leads participants to under-report problems, usability testing can miss the very issues it exists to find [[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_09-7-aestheticusability-effect]] [[Usability Testing]]. Observer bias compounds it — participants who know they are being watched behave as they think is expected — and the mitigation offered is to emphasize that the test evaluates the design and not the user, and that honest feedback is what improves the product [[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_09-7-aestheticusability-effect]].

### Perception-action compatibility

A second body of laboratory findings concerns the relationship between a control and its outcome [[2018-10-14_natural-mappings]]. Stimulus-response compatibility is defined there as the degree to which the physical arrangement of stimuli matches the location of the expected responses; when that relationship is aligned, people respond faster and make fewer mistakes than when it is violated, so the psychological payoff is measured in reaction time and error rate, not preference. Natural mapping is the design consequence: controls that represent or correspond to the outcome they produce are faster to learn and easier to remember. Three routes are described — spatial similarity (arranging controls to mirror the physical layout they govern, such as an on-screen monitor arrangement matching the actual desk), metaphorical and cultural association ("up is more", "green is go", "red is stop", whose effectiveness depends on cultural context), and behavioral similarity (raise-to-wake on a smartwatch mimicking the way people tilt a watch to check the time). Complex multi-finger gestures are singled out as violating the principle, being hard to discover and remember because nothing connects them to their result. The source frames good mappings as bridging the gulf of execution, closing the gap between what a user intends and the action required to achieve it.

### Biases and decision heuristics

**Negativity bias.** People pay more attention to, and give more weight to, negative experiences than neutral or positive ones — attributed to evolutionary wiring for hazard detection that persists even when the threat is trivial [[2016-10-23_negativity-bias-ux]]. Its practical form is that a single usability flaw outweighs many positive features built at great effort, and that users notice breakage but not smooth operation. That source reports website satisfaction staying flat despite designs improving, because users judge sites comparatively and failures count far more than successes. What it recommends: follow familiar navigation patterns and conventions so users can lean on existing knowledge; match workflows to users' mental models rather than presenting information out of sequence; use microcopy strategically to pre-empt concerns; write courteous, clear error messages, since harsh or obscure ones turn a slight inconvenience into an antagonistic encounter; and counteract the inevitable negatives with deliberate moments of delight. The year-in-review draws the same conclusion in stronger operational terms: prevent bad interactions rather than relying on good design to outweigh them [[2017-01-01_ux-quiz-16]].

**Authority.** People tend to comply with authority figures and to treat their opinions as more reliable than their own, an efficiency heuristic that skips the cost of research [[2018-02-04_authority-principle]]. The evidence cited is Milgram (65% of participants administering what they believed were dangerous shocks when directed by a scientist) and Bickman 1974 (92% compliance with a uniformed requester, dropping to 42% in civilian clothes). The UX applications named are photos of authority figures, symbols of authority, logos of reputable organizations and expert endorsements; the effect claimed is that customers who trust these signals are more confident and more likely to buy. The ethical line is drawn explicitly: authority persuasion is acceptable only when honest and helpful, endorsements from unrelated experts and false authority claims are out, and the test offered is to ask whether your technology persuades users to do something you would not want to be persuaded to do yourself.

**Commitment and behavioral consistency.** Once people have made a choice or taken a stand, personal and interpersonal pressure push them to behave consistently with it; inconsistency is read as irrationality, deceit or incompetence [[2018-03-04_commitment-consistency-ux]]. The design pattern is to start with a small, low-stakes commitment and escalate as trust builds — the example given is that users who write a review before logging in are more likely to create an account than users asked to register up front. Public commitments involving others exert more pressure than private ones. Two constraints are attached: the size of the request must match the current level of trust, since early in a relationship even entering an email feels high-stakes while a strong brand can ask for more; and even low-stakes commitments are refused if they require real work, so the commitment path must be frictionless. The source's summary of the failure mode is that an all-or-nothing design delivers nothing from most users.

**Parkinson's Law of Triviality.** Applied to design feedback rather than to end users, the same tendency to take the cheap cognitive route shows up as bike-shedding: stakeholders spend disproportionate time and energy on trivial, visible issues instead of the complex ones, because an accessible issue is easier to grasp and easier to comment on [[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_11-chapter-10-dealing-with-changes]]. That source treats the bias as predictable, and reports two tricks it has produced for exploiting it — "painting a duck", deliberately including an obviously flawed element so criticism lands there, and the 1940s Disney animators' "hairy arms" — only to rule them out: designers should not deceive or manipulate stakeholders this way. What it draws from the bias instead is prevention (set the context and the fidelity, say which feedback is useful now) and planning for the changes that will come anyway, with pre-planned real estate, an extra user study or a reevaluation of the approach.

**The need to be heard.** The same source argues that the reason behind a change request is frequently not that the suggestion is better, but that the person needs reassurance that their voice matters and is being taken seriously [[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_11-chapter-10-dealing-with-changes]]. Its consequence is that a stakeholder who reads as unreasonable is usually unheard rather than irrational, and that showing an idea was genuinely considered does work that arguing its merits does not.

The study guide adds the broader inventory of biases that predictably deviate judgment from rationality — confirmation bias, anchoring, availability heuristic, negativity bias, false-consensus effect — along with decision-making effects such as loss aversion, satisficing and choice overload, and the principle that interaction cost (the total mental and physical resources an action demands) determines whether users will attempt it at all, so cost should be minimized for the actions you want [[2024-01-10_psychology-study-guide]].

### Response time and flow

Speed is treated as a psychological variable rather than an engineering one. The Doherty Threshold puts the figure at 400 milliseconds: productivity soars when a computer and its users interact at a pace where neither has to wait on the other, and past roughly one second users begin thinking about other things and lose focus on the task [[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_12-10-doherty-threshold]] [[Doherty Threshold]]. What responsiveness buys is flow — the state of complete immersion in which users are absorbed by their task, free from inner criticism, and reported as up to five times more productive [[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_12-10-doherty-threshold]].

Because the constraint is perceptual, that source treats perceived performance as a legitimate substitute when the processing genuinely takes longer: skeleton screens, blur-up image loading, progress bars and optimistic UI make a system appear faster than it is, and animation during a wait keeps users focused and reassured, with progress bars making waits feel more tolerable regardless of their accuracy [[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_12-10-doherty-threshold]] [[Feedback Design]]. The pressure is rising, since the average desktop page weighed 2,286 KB in 2023 against 634 KB in 2010–2011 [[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_12-10-doherty-threshold]] [[Web Performance]]. The effect also runs backwards: purposefully adding a delay to a process can increase its perceived value and instil trust, as in a privacy scan where users need confidence that the check was thorough, even when the process itself takes much less time [[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_12-10-doherty-threshold]].

### Emotion, perceived brand, and the limits of delight

Interaction qualities are shown to feed brand perception directly [[2016-07-17_interaction-branding]]. Research by Tolstrup and Heyer measured eight interactivity attributes — responsiveness, direct versus indirect manipulation, precision, pliability, continuous versus discrete behavior, clear labels, expected versus unexpected behavior, and consistency — against brand attributes and emotions. Prototypes with clear labels suggesting their actions triggered joy and associations with sincerity and competence; prototypes behaving as expected or consistently produced the same associations, while inconsistent or unexpected behavior produced surprise and anger. The caution attached is that carefully designed unexpected interactions can produce pleasant surprise but can equally make users feel powerless, so they must be tested with real users. The source's larger claim is that interaction design, usually less scrutinized than visual design, communicates brand traits just as visuals and content do.

Delight is placed in a hierarchy modelled on Maslow: functionality, then reliability, then usability, and only then delight [[2017-03-05_theory-user-delight]]. Two kinds are distinguished — surface delight (animations, transitions, microcopy, high-resolution imagery, sound), which is local and contextual, and deep delight, described as flow-like immersion where the interface behaves like a surgeon's knowledgeable assistant, handing over the right instrument at the right moment without getting in the way. Deep delight is presented as far harder to achieve, requiring streamlined workflows and removed pain points, and as producing passionate advocates. The psychological argument for the ordering is negativity bias: because users remember bad experiences more than good ones, delightful features cannot compensate for missing functionality or reliability, and forced humor or animation layered on an unusable interface reads as non-genuine, risks brand trust, and wastes the investment.

### Getting psychology into a team's decisions

Knowing the principles is not the same as a team using them, and one chapter is about that gap [[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_13-11-applying-psychological-principles-in-design]]. Three moves are offered. Build awareness: posters of the psychological principles kept constantly visible in the workspace, plus regular show-and-tell sessions, to develop shared understanding and a shared vocabulary. Establish design principles as a North Star, guiding values embodying what good design means for this team, which removes the bottleneck of a design gatekeeper and holds consistency as team size and decision volume grow. And connect the two with a three-tier framework: the psychological law is the observation, the design principle is the team's stance, and the rules are what people actually follow [[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_13-11-applying-psychological-principles-in-design]] [[Design Principles]].

The principles themselves are defined in a workshop — identify the team, align on success criteria, diverge into ideas, converge on themes, refine, circulate — which that source presents as the route to adoption and buy-in rather than a formality [[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_13-11-applying-psychological-principles-in-design]]. Its quality bar: direct and actionable rather than bland truisms, answering a real design question, opinionated with clear prioritization, and memorable enough to be used regularly. The worked example is "clarity over abundance of choice", connected to [[Hick's Law]] and translated into rules such as limiting choices to three items and keeping explanations under 80 characters; "familiarity over novelty" is mapped the same way onto [[Jakob's Law]], producing rules about using common design patterns and avoiding flashy animations [[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_13-11-applying-psychological-principles-in-design]].
