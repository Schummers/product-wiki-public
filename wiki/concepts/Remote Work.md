# Remote Work

## Definition

Remote work, in this corpus, means doing UX work — research, facilitation,
design, collaboration — without the participants being in the same room. The
sources treat it as viable rather than second-best: Nielsen Norman Group has
operated as a fully distributed team across the United States for over twenty
years, and reports that remote work succeeds when communication practices and
team building are deliberately prioritised ([[2019-07-07_remote-ux-work-nng-case-study]]).
Remote formats also bring their own advantages, including lower expenses, greater
inclusiveness and more convenience for participants
([[2020-03-29_remote-ux]]), and access to a broader talent pool and wider research
geography ([[2019-07-07_remote-ux-work-nng-case-study]]).

What distance actually costs is communication, and the sources locate the damage
precisely: delayed feedback on designs, the loss of informal hallway
conversation, and unclear project direction
([[2021-02-07_remote-design-challenges]]). Distance also amplifies existing
weaknesses rather than creating new ones — journey mapping already fails from
lack of buy-in and static artefacts, and geographic dispersion makes each of
those worse ([[2017-02-05_remote-customer-journey-mapping]]). The recurring
remedies across the corpus are structural: choose deliberately between
synchronous and asynchronous modes, pick familiar tools over powerful ones, and
replace what proximity used to supply with scheduled routines.

## Practice

### Team structure and communication norms

[[2019-07-07_remote-ux-work-nng-case-study]] recommends choosing a team structure
explicitly — full remote, hybrid or split-time — and enforces an equal
communication rule for hybrid teams: if any one participant is remote, everyone
joins remotely, including colleagues in the same building, so the remote person is
not talked over and left out. It evaluates people on output and impact rather than
observable presence, which it argues can be assessed as effectively remotely as
in person. Growing new talent needs deliberate scaffolding: assigned mentors
distinct from formal managers, shadowing on real projects, welcome gifts and
swag, Slack channels, company retreats and periodic in-person gatherings.
Research need not be remote merely because the team is: geographically spread
members can travel to field-study locations cost-effectively, which can widen
rather than narrow coverage.

[[2021-02-07_remote-design-challenges]], drawing on interviews with 17 remote
workers and a survey of 306 UX practitioners (70% of whom went remote because of
the 2020 pandemic), proposes a fix per challenge. For slow feedback: use tools
with commenting built in (Figma, InVision, Google Drive), keep feedback in a
single channel rather than scattering it across PDF, Figma and Slack, and put
recurring feedback on the calendar so stakeholders treat it as routine. For lost
hallway conversation: schedule recurring hour-long collaborative working sessions,
including full-day ones, and leave 5–10 minutes of social space at the start of
meetings. For unclear direction: maintain a single source of truth for
requirements, documentation and designs so people can self-serve, hand off
designs over video rather than email alone to prevent misinterpretation of
interactions and expectations, and check in frequently.

### Synchronous, asynchronous, or both

[[2019-07-28_synchronous-asynchronous-ideation]] states that remote ideation can
be as effective as in-person and lays out the trade-off. Synchronous sessions give
fast back-and-forth, focused attention inside a time limit and team bonding, and
suit quick decisions, complex problems needing discussion, and tight deadlines;
they suffer from cross-time-zone scheduling, simultaneous talking, awkward
silences, and a shortage of tools that support several people sketching at once.
Asynchronous ideation removes the scheduling problem and gives ideas time to
incubate, suiting longer timelines and dispersed teams; it costs team-building,
loses attention over days or weeks, and forces participants to re-read prior
contributions each time. Neither is inherently better — choose on deadline,
time-zone spread and complexity — and the source explicitly recommends blending
them: a synchronous kickoff to frame the problem and capture first ideas, an open
document or thread for a designated period, then synchronous convergence on the
final choice.

[[2020-03-29_remote-ux]] gives the same advice for collaboration generally: mix
both modes to shorten meetings, use pre-structured shared documents for
asynchronous contribution and then meet to discuss, and structure activities so
everyone actually participates.
[[2020-03-08_tools-remote-ux-workshops]] adds the operational requirements —
synchronous workshops need calendar time, videoconferencing and a real-time
collaborative tool; asynchronous ones need firm deadlines against procrastination
and a tool with version history to track contributions.

### Choosing tools: familiar beats powerful

Three sources converge on the same rule. [[2020-03-08_tools-remote-ux-workshops]]
advises favouring familiar, low-complexity tools over specialised but unfamiliar
ones, especially for a team's first remote workshops, and notes that the best tool
is probably one already in use; it matches features to workshop type — generative
workshops need drag-and-drop whiteboarding that organises large quantities of flat
items; evaluative workshops need democratic voting not skewed by seniority, with
results shown as matrices or lists; mapping workshops need alignment that survives
the addition of new objects, plus optional nesting for context.
[[2020-03-29_remote-ux]] repeats the principle: keep tools few and familiar, do
not make participants switch between several, think creatively about repurposing
existing tools, and never let the technology become the focus.

[[2020-05-31_journey-map-digital-template]] is the most literal application: a
spreadsheet template for journey mapping, columns for journey phases and rows for
actions, thoughts, emotions, insights and highs/lows, plus persona, scenario and
goals. Its argument is accessibility — most organisations permit spreadsheets under
their data-protection policies, no onboarding is needed, and familiarity raises
team ownership and contribution. It treats the map as a living artefact needing
version numbers and dates, invites teams to take liberties with the template, and
insists the process of building the map together matters as much as the artefact.

[[2017-02-05_remote-customer-journey-mapping]] approaches tools from the other
end, tiering them by capability rather than familiarity: Level 1 (Canvanizer,
OmniGraffle, Gliffy, Lucidchart) gives a blank canvas for visualisation and
sharing, cheap but without real-time collaboration, and suits a UX team of one;
Level 2 (Mural, Miro, Smaply, UXPressia) allows simultaneous contribution and
mirrors in-person workshop benefits for distributed teams; Level 3 (Touchpoint
Dashboard, IBM Journey Designer, CFN Insight) integrates personas, metrics and
voice-of-customer data, expensive but justified where multiple data sources and an
ongoing CX programme exist. Choice depends on team size, available data and
budget. It notes that digital formats fix a specific failure mode — the static,
stale document teams mistake for final. Between these two, there is a difference
of emphasis rather than contradiction: [[2020-05-31_journey-map-digital-template]]
argues for the lowest-barrier familiar tool, while
[[2017-02-05_remote-customer-journey-mapping]] scales the tool to the team's
resources and data needs.

### Running remote research

[[2020-03-29_remote-ux]] covers setup: practise the technology beforehand, run dry
runs and pilots with colleagues even when familiar with the tools, and get
technical help for a first complex session; assume technology will fail and keep a
backup such as phone dial-in; prefer a platform participants need not download;
recruit extra users as a safety net; give clear instructions for complex tools;
and adjust consent forms for remote recording. When facilitating: turn the camera
on to build rapport, use icebreakers, set ground rules, and assign homework so
participants practise with the tool beforehand. On method, it holds that moderated
sessions allow follow-up and probing while unmoderated ones are faster but yield
less detail, and that both beat no research at all.

[[2020-07-26_remote-usability-testing-costs]] quantifies that trade-off for a
five-participant qualitative study in the US: moderated costs $415–$1,680 plus
32–48 researcher hours, unmoderated $250–$1,250 plus 11–27 hours, so unmoderated
saves 20–40% — less than commonly assumed. Its hidden costs: overrecruiting to
absorb cheaters and professional participants (some platforms replace them free,
others charge), analysis time that merely shifts from facilitation to watching
recordings, and session length capped around 20 minutes against 60 for moderated,
which makes long multi-part tasks a poor fit and may force more participants each
doing a subset. A skilled facilitator probes ambiguous answers, clarifies
misunderstandings and surfaces motivations, which the source says often justifies
the difference in cost.
