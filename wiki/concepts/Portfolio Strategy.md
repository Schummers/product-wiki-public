# Portfolio Strategy

## Definition

Portfolio strategy is the set of decisions a venture studio makes about the collection of startups it builds rather than about any single one: how many ventures to run at once, how much to budget per bet, which markets and business models to concentrate on, how to fund the pipeline, how to share resources and knowledge across ventures, when to kill or spin off a company, and what counts as success for the whole. The *Startup Studio Playbook* frames the studio as a factory whose product is the startup, so that failure of one venture is a dispersed risk rather than a portfolio-destroying loss, and the leader's job is portfolio management rather than building one unicorn ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_16-the-studio-funnel]], [[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_14-studio-leadership]]). *Venture Studios Demystified* comes at the same object from the outside: studios pursue deeper engagement with fewer companies and higher founding ownership than accelerators, choose between industry specialisation and agnosticism under a tight ideation constraint, and, once their own operations hit a ceiling, decide whether to grow through follow-on investment or by seeding other studios ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_03-comparison-to-other-models]], [[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_07-industry-focus]], [[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_25-industrys-path-forward]]).

The two books supply different kinds of evidence. The Playbook gives worked examples from named studios (Innonic, Drukka) with capital figures and venture counts; *Venture Studios Demystified* gives proportions across 19 interviewed studios. Where they touch the same question, notably market focus, they lean in different directions, and this page keeps both. Related pages: [[Studio Funnel]], [[Studio Strategy]], [[Studio Economics]], [[Stage Gate]], [[Venture Studio]].

## Practice

### The portfolio is the risk-management unit

The Playbook's central argument is that a studio manages a portfolio of parallel startups with shared resources, deciding intentionally how many to pursue at the same time (12-15 per year is given as an example) based on available capital and core-team capacity, so that failure is a dispersed risk. The advantage over a standalone startup is that the studio can move from a failed concept to the next without losing the core team ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_16-the-studio-funnel]]). The same book's strategy chapter names four characteristics of a good studio, three of which are portfolio properties: sustainability (multiple startups over years), efficiency (economies of scale and knowledge reuse), and antifragility (recycling resources from failures), with viable startups as the fourth ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_15-vision-and-strategy]]).

*Venture Studios Demystified* draws the contrast with the accelerator's version of a portfolio: accelerators play a volume game (Y Combinator's summer 2021 batch was 377 companies) with 5-10% stakes, while studios take substantially higher founding equity in fewer companies and stay involved across the whole lifecycle ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_03-comparison-to-other-models]]).

### How many ventures, and at what cost per bet

The Playbook's case studies give a range of throughput and budget figures:

- **Innonic** funds 2-3 new ventures a year from its own cash flows. Its funnel prices each stage: pre-validation takes 2-4 weeks and a couple of thousand dollars, full validation with a dedicated CEO costs EUR 20,000 over 3-6 months, and formation allocates EUR 200,000 for about 18 months ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_12-changing-ecommerce-one-startup-at-a-time]]).
- **Drukka** started with EUR 100,000 and allocated 10,000-20,000 USD per initiative, launching a dozen initiatives and getting almost half of them to a seed-ready stage. Its later pre-seed fund, Studioone.hu, invests EUR 20-40,000 per startup with Drukka supplying everything else; from EUR 1 million raised it could build 30-40 startups, with the fund holding 65-75% equity ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_13-stretching-our-limits]]).
- **The generic funnel** budgets strictly per idea (e.g. 10k-20k EUR and 3-6 months for sharing-economy concepts) so that killing one idea never threatens the portfolio, and feeds it with 300+ viable concepts a year from daily 60-minute ideation workshops, on the reasoning that abundant alternatives make sunk-cost mistakes easier to avoid ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_16-the-studio-funnel]]).

*Venture Studios Demystified* gives the conversion ratio rather than the budget: studios need roughly 30-100 ideas to produce one launched company ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_07-industry-focus]]). Its innovation chapter adds that the studio economic model of failing fast and preserving runway is what rules out expensive, slow disruptive innovation, so studios design within the existing technology frontier and apply proven business models to new problem areas ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_24-studios-and-innovation]]). The Playbook's Drukka story is the practitioner's version of that rule: extreme frugality meant starting with low-risk copycat ideas in the sharing economy before graduating to proprietary concepts ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_13-stretching-our-limits]]).

### Market focus: the point where the books diverge

The Playbook recommends focus. Studios should attack markets where the founders have personal experience, network and potential customer access, because going into unknown markets without industry understanding is riskier than exploiting insider knowledge; they should aim for high-margin segments where they can differentiate rather than compete on price; and they should build for demonstrated market needs, knowing who the first paying customer will be, rather than idealistic "Uber for X" concepts ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_15-vision-and-strategy]]). Innonic's ecommerce concentration is the worked example ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_12-changing-ecommerce-one-startup-at-a-time]]).

*Venture Studios Demystified* finds that only about half of studios actually do this. Against the authors' expectation, roughly 50% of interviewed studios declare a specific focus and 50% remain industry agnostic despite the operational advantages specialisation appears to offer. The explanation is the ideation constraint: at 30-100 ideas per launched company, a narrowly specialised studio can quickly run out of opportunities, and unlike a VC that sources deals globally a studio can only draw ideas from its own employees' creativity. Focus follows founder expertise: founders with deep domain knowledge specialise, founders without it stay generalist. The authors predict the agnostic share will grow as competition for limited idea sources increases ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_07-industry-focus]]).

The two positions are not strictly contradictory (both tie focus to founder expertise), but the Playbook prescribes specialisation as the safer choice while *Venture Studios Demystified* documents a market where half the studios reject it and expects that half to grow. A reader should take the Playbook's advice as one practitioner's judgement and the Demystified figure as the observed distribution in 2022.

### Business model: B2B SaaS and margins

Where studios do specialise, both books converge on the same kind of business. *Venture Studios Demystified* finds B2B SaaS to be the most common focus (4 of 19 studios), chosen for its subscription-plus-contract model, locked-in recurring revenue, and playbook-like scalability after product-market fit ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_07-industry-focus]]). Its innovation chapter confirms that studios concentrate on B2B SaaS and niche-need ventures rather than consumer or bleeding-edge technology, a choice reflecting both founder expertise and the economics of minimising losses on failed ideas ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_24-studios-and-innovation]]). The Playbook does not name SaaS as a rule but argues for high-margin segments on the grounds that margin is what lets the team scale beyond agency workload and creates capacity to build its own startups ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_15-vision-and-strategy]]), and Innonic's quoted rule for its SaaS products is to be "awesome" in at least one way, either a unique solution to a painful problem or markedly better usability ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_12-changing-ecommerce-one-startup-at-a-time]]).

### Funding the pipeline

The Playbook shows two ways of paying for a portfolio without depending on external investor timelines:

- **Legacy businesses as the engine.** Innonic funds every venture from the profits of ShopRenter.hu (30% ecommerce market dominance), OptiMonk.com (ranked #1 on-site messaging toolkit) and Ecommerce Expo, which removes investor and founder dependence; portfolio companies such as CodersRank.io and Conversific.com went on to raise external venture funding ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_12-changing-ecommerce-one-startup-at-a-time]]).
- **A pre-seed fund with co-investors.** Drukka's Studioone.hu formalised the studio-plus-external-capital model, offering co-investors deal flow and portfolio exposure; the studio's own credibility came from spin-offs achieving 13x valuation increases at seed and 6-8x during Series A negotiations ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_13-stretching-our-limits]]).

For the fund-based approach that *Venture Studios Demystified* documents as the norm, see [[Studio Fundraising]] and [[Venture Capital]].

### Synergies across ventures

Drukka's route from 6-7 concurrent ventures to a dozen a year was to batch startups by industry (sharing economy in its case): running joint mentoring sessions for related CEOs, discovering code-base reuse, and sharing learning across portfolio companies ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_13-stretching-our-limits]]). The Playbook's strategy chapter generalises this as efficiency through economies of scale and knowledge reuse ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_15-vision-and-strategy]]).

### Governance: conflicts, go/no-go, killing

Running several companies in parallel creates decisions that a single-startup founder never faces. The Playbook's leadership chapter lists them: when multiple startups need the same person and one approaches a breakthrough, the leader must make go/no-go decisions that reprioritise the portfolio; the leader also balances three constituencies at once, the studio organisation, the portfolio startups (founders and investees simultaneously), and external investors, with cap-table structure and management fees reflecting those competing interests. The required mindset is factory operations: letting mature companies become independent and killing unsuccessful initiatives to recycle resources ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_14-studio-leadership]]).

The funnel chapter turns this into rules: fixed success and failure criteria with deadlines, fast and transparent kill decisions (postponing them keeps the team frustrated and burns cash), emotional distance from ideas before traction, and a go/no-go at the end of the initial build based on a working MVP, active users and a scalable growth engine, or the higher bar of product-market fit ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_16-the-studio-funnel]]). Drukka's CEO validation process is one implementation: each incoming CEO develops 3-5 bare-minimum concept validations in parallel, and the viable 1-2 survive ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_13-stretching-our-limits]]). See [[Stage Gate]] and [[Idea Validation]].

### What to measure

The Playbook is explicit that the one metric that matters is cumulative revenue of the startups plus the studio, not vanity metrics such as startup count or user numbers. Revenue signals that products solve real needs, that the studio can sell, that the platform scales, that prioritisation is disciplined, and it gives leverage for future fundraising. The horizon is long: 3-5 to 7 years before profitability, and a founder should be willing to spend 5-10-15 years building and releasing ventures ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_15-vision-and-strategy]]). *Venture Studios Demystified* does not propose a portfolio metric; its economic argument is that high initial ownership at low cost means exits of only $50M or $100M can still return 50x or 100x, so strong execution matters far more than game-changing ideas ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_24-studios-and-innovation]]).

### Exits and independence

The Playbook offers two exit postures: quick, low-hanging-fruit acquisitions that build an experienced local CEO pool and recycle capital into new ventures, or long-term unicorns that capture whole markets ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_16-the-studio-funnel]]). Its stated preference is to build resilient startups over fragile unicorns and to commit to results over promises ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_15-vision-and-strategy]]). Independence is a stage before exit: Drukka's seed-funded companies (Rendi.hu, Neatly.hu, Tickething.com, EzExpress.hu) reached seed rounds within 18-24 months, and the studio stayed active in them afterwards ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_13-stretching-our-limits]]); Innonic's growth stage ends in independence or VC fundraising ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_12-changing-ecommerce-one-startup-at-a-time]]).

### Scaling the portfolio beyond the team

*Venture Studios Demystified* identifies a structural ceiling. Follow-on investment in successful companies can scale indefinitely, but launching and managing more ventures cannot: a larger team slows ideation and decision-making, partners resist diluting their carry and ownership by adding partners, and founder-level equity has to be justified by sustained high-touch involvement. Two portfolio responses follow: expand follow-on investment in the winners, or, for established studios raising larger funds, allocate capital to seed new studio leaders rather than expanding in-house capacity ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_25-industrys-path-forward]]). The same book notes that the binding constraint on the market is the supply of quality entrepreneurs, not studio capacity or ideation ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_24-studios-and-innovation]]). The Playbook's answer to the same ceiling is organisational, self-scaling leadership through standardised processes, delegation and an executive team with portfolio-management skills ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_14-studio-leadership]]); see [[Studio Leadership]] and [[Team Scaling]].
