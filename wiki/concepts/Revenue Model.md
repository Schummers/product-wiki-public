# Revenue Model

## Definition

A studio's revenue model is the set of ways it brings in cash before, and apart from, the equity returns that arrive when its portfolio companies exit. The problem it answers is a timing gap: company-building costs and core-team salaries are paid upfront, while exits come years later. Kannan and Peterman found this gap far more widespread than they expected, even among studios that had already raised money; their base-case model has a studio running at a loss for five or more years and needing about $10M of upfront capital before exits begin ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_10-revenue-generation]], [[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_15-model-overview]]). Szigeti approaches the same gap from the fundraising side and lists a hybrid model, running a profitable agency alongside the studio, as one way to pay for venture building without outside capital ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_20-fundraising-and-investor-relations]]).

The research book is the more detailed of the two on this concept. It documents three revenue strategies from its 19 interviews (billing launched companies back for services, corporate co-building partnerships, and corporate consulting), models the first two, and ranks them by how much they reduce capital needs and how much they distract from the core mission. Its conclusion is that billing back is the preferred lever because it does not pull leadership away from building ventures, while every form of corporate work trades revenue for team attention. The playbook reaches a parallel conclusion about agency work: it funds ventures, but balancing short-term revenue against long-term venture commitment is the organisational cost.

Christian Crumlish widens the term past the studio case: for him a revenue model is simply how any product brings money in, money being a material product managers work with alongside code and pixels, in nonprofits and government agencies as much as in commercial companies. Most product managers do not control profit and loss directly, but they have to be aware of the financial constraints and opportunities that shape what is possible for their product, and his position is that the model is testable and pivotable rather than fixed: if you have created a valuable product, you can find a business model to sustain it ([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_11-chapter-8-getting-the-money]]).

## Practice

### The cash-flow gap the revenue model has to close

- Studios face high upfront expenses and long timelines to liquidity; intermediate revenue exists to bridge that interval ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_10-revenue-generation]]).
- In the base-case model (single entity, no fund, no revenue, equity-funded), a studio launching 7 companies a year by year 10 burns $1.3M-$3.2M annually until exits begin in year 6, and needs $10M in upfront capital. Revenue generation is one of the four levers the authors identify to change that profile, alongside raising a fund, equity ownership levels, and exit timing ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_15-model-overview]]).
- Once exits materialise, the model shows exit proceeds can sustain operations and portfolio growth on their own; the revenue model matters most in the years before that ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_15-model-overview]]).

### Product revenue models, and how they get chosen

[[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_11-chapter-8-getting-the-money]] surveys the common models a product can run on: beta-to-paid and free trials with paid conversion, freemium paywalls, subscriptions, in-app purchases, pro tiers, and enterprise licensing by seat or by usage. Which one is right depends on customer needs and constraints, and the chapter's practical stance is that a model is something you test and change rather than something you pick once ([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_11-chapter-8-getting-the-money]]). Multiple revenue streams and business lines running side by side, on different pay cycles, are treated as normal rather than as a failure to choose ([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_11-chapter-8-getting-the-money]]).

#### Crumlish's five revenue experiments at 7 Cups

He tells this as the story of one company, not as a method to copy. At 7 Cups the team ran five distinct experiments ([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_11-chapter-8-getting-the-money]]):

1. Donations through a "compassion jar" interface, which generated minimal "pin money".
2. Upgrades to a premium "Growth Path" subscription (monthly, yearly, lifetime), which produced substantial revenue but could not reach profitability on its own.
3. White-label SaaS for universities, which brought larger transactions but demanded resources and created tension with the consumer roadmap.
4. A therapist directory competing with Psychology Today, which failed for lack of competitive leverage.
5. Paid professional therapy as a freemium tier, which is what finally unlocked profitability.

Break-even, when it came, was "ramen profitable": expenses kept to a bare minimum, everything done "scrappy, not crappy", and the team paying itself well below market rates, which Crumlish says is not real sustainability in the long run. It broke the cycle of venture-capital dependency and opened three possible directions, organic growth with small bets, optimisation of the core model towards healthy sustainability, or the pursuit of a breakthrough 100x opportunity ([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_11-chapter-8-getting-the-money]]).

#### Is break-even even the goal?

The chapter carries its own dissent rather than resolving it. B. Pagels-Minor, then a product manager at Netflix, calls break-even "pretty illogical in modern tech companies", pointing at Amazon and Netflix being deliberately unprofitable for years and arguing the goal is more likely market penetration; Crumlish accepts the point and asks the reader to treat his 7 Cups story as one particular path rather than as the success goal ([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_11-chapter-8-getting-the-money]]). Pagels-Minor also pushes back on the chapter's premise that UX designers are uncomfortable with money, saying the designers they work with are as savvy about monetisation as many product managers, and Crumlish concedes it is probably true ([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_11-chapter-8-getting-the-money]]).

#### Revenue lifecycles and sunsetting

A revenue stream has stages, and each demands a different proficiency: establishing (research and development, costs and no revenue), growth, then optimisation and plateauing of an established stream, then sunsetting when a product no longer breaks even and has no viable future. Scaling revenue at launch, fine-tuning a well-oiled machine, and managing legal and financial obligations during a wind-down are three different jobs ([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_11-chapter-8-getting-the-money]]).

Established revenue is also a constraint on what the company can do next. Crumlish attributes the "innovator's dilemma" to Clay Christensen and reads it as a long-term risk of complacency: incumbents cannot afford to cannibalise the revenue they already have, which is exactly the opening disruptive upstarts use. Sunsetting a product takes skills parallel to building one, identifying decay patterns, winding down commitments, preserving legal and security obligations, and removing the product entirely from the business ([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_11-chapter-8-getting-the-money]]).

### Billing back launched companies

Charging portfolio companies for the services the studio provides them.

- **Three timing options.** No bill-back before incorporation; bill-back after incorporation but before spin-out (partial services); or bill-back only after spin-out (post-separation services only). Which one a studio chooses reflects its philosophy and its cash needs ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_10-revenue-generation]]).
- **Modelled impact.** Billing post-incorporation services at cost, with incorporation following validation, reduces capital needs from $10M to $7M (35% savings). Billing from pre-incorporation would cut requirements to $5M (50%) but is rarely adopted because founders resist it ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_16-revenue-generation]]).
- **Timing is a relationship decision, not only a financial one.** Earlier incorporation allows more billing back but can create friction with founders; later incorporation postpones revenue but preserves the relationship ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_16-revenue-generation]]).
- **What it actually recoups.** Studios spend $250K-$700K per launched company once failed ideas are counted, with fully-loaded spin-out costs of $350K-$415K; post-incorporation billing that excludes MVP-stage costs recouped only $68K ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_16-revenue-generation]]).
- **No equity penalty observed.** The authors expected aggressive billing back to come with a smaller studio equity stake and found no such correlation, which they read as founders not treating service fees as a major factor when choosing a studio ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_10-revenue-generation]]).
- **Why it is preferred.** Billing back does not distract leadership from core venture-building; the other two strategies do ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_10-revenue-generation]]).

### Corporate co-builds

Building a company jointly with a corporate partner, charging a margin on the work and taking equity in the result.

- **Economics.** Chapter 10 reports partnerships charging 30-50% margins above cost with the studio taking 30-35% equity in the co-built company ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_10-revenue-generation]]); the modelling chapter uses a typical 40% margin ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_16-revenue-generation]]). The two chapters state the figure differently, so both are kept here.
- **Modelled impact.** Ramping from 1 to 4 co-builds a year by year 5 at 40% margin reduces capital needs from $10M to $9M, a 17% saving, smaller than billing back ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_16-revenue-generation]]).
- **Benefits.** Corporate partners bring domain expertise and a potential acquisition path ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_10-revenue-generation]]).
- **Costs.** Most studios use the same people for corporate engagements and internal ventures, so every co-build is a direct trade-off against launching an internal company; co-builds also add coordination complexity and make exit profiles less predictable. Allocating the team entirely to co-builds is described as costly ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_10-revenue-generation]], [[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_16-revenue-generation]]).

### Corporate consulting

- Only 1 of the 19 studios interviewed pursued consulting for corporations as a revenue model. That studio makes 40% profit margins on its services, but the authors note that consulting runs counter to the company-building mission, diverts the core team, and requires sourcing and executing difficult contracts ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_10-revenue-generation]]).

### Agency revenue: the playbook's hybrid

- Szigeti lists "bootstrapping with agency revenue" among the ways to fund a studio: operate a software-development or marketing agency in parallel and use its profitable work to finance internal ventures. The stated challenge is organisational, balancing short-term revenue against long-term venture commitment ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_20-fundraising-and-investor-relations]]).
- This is the same tension Kannan and Peterman describe for corporate consulting and co-builds. The two books do not use the same vocabulary: the playbook treats agency work as a funding source discussed next to founder capital, VCs, corporates, angels and pre-seed funds, while the research book treats corporate work as a revenue lever inside a financial model.

### Combining levers

- Pairing post-validation billing back with corporate co-builds yields a 39% reduction in capital needs and brings maximum annual burn down to $2.1M, at the price of more uncertain exit profiles and a team-capacity problem that has to be managed deliberately ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_16-revenue-generation]]).
- In the best-in-class scenario, billing back post-incorporation costs and corporate co-build revenue, combined with an intentional early exit ($75M in year 4) and a dual-entity fund structure, reduce the studio's initial raise to $200K in year 1, with the early exit covering operations through year 6. Realised IRR for the operating company reaches 100% with a 1000x+ multiple if all profits are distributed ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_20-best-in-class-case]]).
- The modelling chapter ranks revenue generation as a measurable but not top-tier lever: fundraising, reducing equity stakes and exit timing have the highest impact on IRR and cash balance ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_15-model-overview]]).

### The alternative: spend less instead of earning more

Kannan and Peterman list cost reduction as a substitute for revenue generation: outsource MVP development, add early go/no-go gates that cap spending per idea at $50K-$100K before a go-ahead, or replicate a business model that already worked across geographies ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_10-revenue-generation]]).

### Where the books differ

- **Which revenue is being modelled.** Kannan, Peterman and Szigeti model the revenue of the *studio*, the entity that builds companies; [[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_11-chapter-8-getting-the-money]] models the revenue of a *product*. The two do not compete: the studio question is how to survive the years before exits, the product question is which model turns users into customers, and only Crumlish treats sunsetting a revenue stream as part of the job.
- **Scope.** The research book covers three named strategies with modelled dollar impacts; the playbook covers one (agency work) qualitatively, inside a chapter about fundraising. Any number on this page comes from Kannan and Peterman.
- **Framing.** Szigeti presents agency revenue as a way to avoid or delay outside capital altogether. Kannan and Peterman present revenue as a lever that reduces how much capital a studio must raise (from $10M to $7M, $9M or $5M depending on the strategy) rather than as a way to avoid raising.
- **Preferred lever.** The research book explicitly favours billing back over any corporate work. The playbook does not rank; it names the agency hybrid and its organisational cost and moves on.
