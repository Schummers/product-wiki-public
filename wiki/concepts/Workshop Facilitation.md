# Workshop Facilitation

## Definition

Workshop facilitation is the act of providing unobtrusive, objective guidance
to a group as it works collaboratively toward a goal: the facilitator plans and
leads activities that let participants contribute fully and equally, while
staying neutral on the content itself, an expert in process rather than in
subject matter [[2020-04-19_workshop-facilitation-101]]. It is a transferable
skill, not a workshop-specific one — the same competencies (start from goals,
follow a guide, improvise when needed, make participants *do* things, use
silence) apply equally to moderating a user test
[[2021-11-28_facilitate-ux-workshop-user-test]].

The object being facilitated — the workshop — is distinct from a meeting.
Meetings share information across many topics with passive participation;
workshops solve a predefined problem through deep, hands-on collaboration and
produce decisions and action items during the session
[[2020-02-16_workshops-vs-meetings]], [[2021-09-12_facilitating-ux-workshops-guide]],
[[2019-12-08_5-ux-workshops]]. That difference is why facilitation exists as a
discipline: gathering people in a room produces nothing on its own, and a
workshop run without a real problem, real collaboration needs, or adequate
planning wastes everyone's time and costs the facilitator the attendance of
senior stakeholders next time [[2020-02-16_workshops-vs-meetings]]. Four goals
define what good facilitation is aiming at: full and equal participation,
mutual understanding, inclusive decision-making, and shared responsibility for
what happens after [[2020-04-19_workshop-facilitation-101]].

That distinction between workshop and meeting is about the format, not about
whether facilitation is needed. One source turns the same craft on the ordinary
stakeholder design meeting, arguing that the designer should "design the
meeting" itself — its structure, chunking, transitions and pauses are treated as
facilitation decisions, made so that the group's attention lands on the design
decision rather than on the way it was presented
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_04-chapter-3-design-the-meeting]].

Jeff Patton gives the same craft its own vocabulary for a specific format, the
**story workshop**, which he distinguishes sharply from an ordinary meeting: a
small, tightly scoped conversation, filled with "productive discussion, hand
waving, whiteboard drawing, and sketching," that closes only once the group
reaches agreed acceptance criteria
[[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_21-16-refine-define-and-build]].

## Practice

### Principles and the facilitator's stance

- Listen with an open yet discerning mind, and guide participants toward better
  expression when needed; listening is the core of everything else
  [[2020-04-19_workshop-facilitation-101]].
- Stay objective, like a referee. Avoid giving advice; keep any advice oriented
  around process, not content [[2020-04-19_workshop-facilitation-101]]. During
  execution, step back rather than contributing — a facilitator holds authority,
  so their contributions carry disproportionate weight, which is why playing
  facilitator and participant simultaneously is a mistake
  [[2018-07-22_ux-workshop-exercises]].
- Be authentic. Effectiveness does not require a particular personality type; be
  honest about what you know [[2020-04-19_workshop-facilitation-101]].
- Plan ahead but welcome improvisation: adapt to time, dynamics, attitudes, and
  goals, since no single technique always works
  [[2020-04-19_workshop-facilitation-101]]. Deviate when an activity is not
  working and needs more direction [[2021-11-28_facilitate-ux-workshop-user-test]].
- Embrace constructive conflict — uncomfortable but necessary for transparency,
  trust, and buy-in, and workshops have tools for working through disagreement
  productively [[2020-04-19_workshop-facilitation-101]].
- Assume good intent: most dominating participants are not malicious, merely
  unaware of their impact [[2021-02-21_dominating-workshop-participants]].
- Iterate on your own practice: document what went well and where it was hard,
  and observe other facilitators to learn different styles
  [[2018-07-22_ux-workshop-exercises]].

### Preparation

- Begin from goals, not activities. Define what you want to achieve before
  choosing exercises, and post and review the goals to keep participants aligned
  [[2021-11-28_facilitate-ux-workshop-user-test]]. Workshop preparation is on a
  different scale from meeting preparation: brief, objectives, intentional
  activity sequencing, detailed agenda, materials, stakeholder alignment,
  documentation [[2020-02-16_workshops-vs-meetings]].
- Prepare a detailed guide — agenda, timing, questions, backup activities.
  Preparation is what makes facilitation look effortless
  [[2021-11-28_facilitate-ux-workshop-user-test]].
- Plan realistic durations. A UX roadmapping workshop runs 2–4 hours depending
  on scope; when roadmapping is new to the organization, split it into a
  preliminary 1.5-hour session for goals and inputs and a 2.5-hour primary
  session for themes and prioritization [[2021-05-23_roadmapping-workshop]].
- Gather and consolidate existing research into a shared repository beforehand,
  and give attendees background reading and thought-starter questions as
  homework to prime them [[2020-07-05_journey-mapping-workshop]],
  [[2020-09-20_service-blueprinting-workshops]],
  [[2021-05-23_roadmapping-workshop]].
- Hold a day-0 call: a one-hour-or-less virtual session a few days to a week
  before, covering the agenda, the foundational assumptions that could derail
  the workshop (primarily goals and scope), and participant expectations. Invite
  the entire workshop group plus invested executives — excluding people judged
  less critical risks a later veto — and obtain explicit verbal buy-in from the
  person with veto power [[2022-08-28_day-0-calls]].
- Select tools and materials in advance: sticky notes and posters in person,
  familiar digital tools for virtual sessions
  [[2020-09-20_service-blueprinting-workshops]]. Templates exist for
  facilitation, team norms, and specific activities and can shortcut the
  planning work [[2024-09-13_free-ux-templates]].
- Assign roles and responsibilities before the day
  [[2020-09-20_service-blueprinting-workshops]], [[2021-05-23_roadmapping-workshop]].

### Who is in the room

- Keep the group to 6–12 participants: larger groups demand more facilitator
  effort and risk passive virtual participation, smaller ones may miss critical
  perspectives [[2022-08-14_workshop-participant-lists]]. Note the sources scale
  this to context: scenario-mapping workshops are described as 4–6
  crossfunctional people [[2021-03-28_scenario-mapping-personas]],
  service-blueprinting workshops as 4–6 stakeholders with power to act
  [[2020-09-20_service-blueprinting-workshops]], and first-time blueprinting
  efforts as small groups of 2–4 [[2020-03-22_blueprint-fails-fixes]]. Plan one
  facilitator per twelve attendees, with a backup facilitator for technical
  issues in virtual workshops [[2020-09-20_service-blueprinting-workshops]].
- Diversify deliberately across departments, domain knowledge (mix experts with
  novices, whose questions force fresh thinking), and seniority — homogeneous
  seniority risks biased thinking [[2022-08-14_workshop-participant-lists]].
  Diverse participation generates more creative solutions and exposes blind
  spots [[2017-07-02_facilitating-design-studio-workshop]].
- Designate one person as facilitator, focused solely on facilitation rather
  than participation, and name a decider if major decisions will be made — never
  exclude that person to avoid conflict, since they can veto the work afterwards
  [[2022-08-14_workshop-participant-lists]]. Where possible, split facilitator
  responsibilities between two people, or bring in a neutral external
  facilitator to preserve objectivity [[2020-03-22_blueprint-fails-fixes]].
- Sources disagree on how to handle senior participants. One argues for
  considering the exclusion of high-ranking members from ideation sessions, or
  running pre-sessions where junior staff can share ideas more safely
  [[2017-08-13_group-ideation]]; another says never exclude the decider and
  invite known dominators only to the segments where they are needed
  [[2022-08-14_workshop-participant-lists]],
  [[2021-02-21_dominating-workshop-participants]].
- Include the user perspective: real users, or proxies such as customer-support
  representatives, sales representatives, and researchers who know users better
  than managers do [[2022-08-14_workshop-participant-lists]],
  [[2023-06-11_including-users-workshops]]. When recruiting actual users, prefer
  those who took part in earlier research and are known to be articulate; guard
  against following users blindly by teaching participants the difference
  between user needs and design solutions, and by scheduling critical
  discussions after the users leave [[2023-06-11_including-users-workshops]].
  Users can also validate an assumption map inside the workshop, using stickers
  and sticky notes to mark agreement or disagreement
  [[2020-07-05_journey-mapping-workshop]].

### Running an activity: explain, execute, examine

- **Explain** — describe the steps, define roles, explain the context, answer
  questions. Do not state what will be learned and avoid biased framing that
  steers participants [[2018-07-22_ux-workshop-exercises]].
- **Execute** — let participants work while you observe and stay out of focus
  [[2018-07-22_ux-workshop-exercises]].
- **Examine** — present outcomes, reflect on the experience, and connect the
  output to previous work and future activities so there is continuity
  [[2018-07-22_ux-workshop-exercises]]. Playback (individuals or subgroups
  sharing progress and insights back to the whole group) is the corresponding
  foundational activity [[2020-03-01_workshop-activities]],
  [[2020-09-20_service-blueprinting-workshops]].
- Set expectations up front: the session is interactive, everyone contributes,
  the mapping covers the most important interactions rather than an exhaustive
  list, knowledge gaps are expected, and the artifact will not leave the room
  complete or polished [[2020-09-20_service-blueprinting-workshops]].
- Frame tasks as commands rather than questions so participants act instead of
  discussing [[2021-11-28_facilitate-ux-workshop-user-test]].

### Diverge and converge

- The dominant structural pattern: divergent activities generate many ideas or
  contributions (sketching, brainstorming, brainwriting), then convergent
  activities find patterns and prioritize (affinity diagramming, forced ranking)
  [[2020-02-16_workshops-vs-meetings]], [[2021-09-12_facilitating-ux-workshops-guide]].
- Concretely: individuals work alone first, then the group synthesizes. This
  avoids groupthink, lets introverts contribute equally, and prevents dominant
  voices and the HiPPO effect from shaping the option set
  [[2024-05-30_diverge-converge]], [[2020-04-19_workshop-facilitation-101]].
  A worked timing example: ten minutes of individual generation, then group
  discussion [[2020-09-20_service-blueprinting-workshops]]; affinity
  diagramming typically allows 5–10 minutes of independent sticky-note
  generation [[2024-04-26_affinity-diagram]].
- Diverging can happen before the workshop, at participants' own pace, or inside
  it as dedicated solo time [[2024-05-30_diverge-converge]].
- *UX Research* names the same two poles from the research side: convergent
  workshops seek consensus on a single design direction, while divergent
  workshops explore many avenues through methods such as participatory
  design, paper prototyping, and card sorting, both treated as
  improvisational activities where facilitation skill applies directly
  [[2016-11-04_ux-research_11-chapter-10-improv-in-research]]. Participatory
  design itself spans a range from simple brainstorming to formal sketching
  exercises where stakeholders and customers adapt their mental models
  visually, and it can occur at any phase of a research effort, not only in a
  dedicated workshop
  [[2016-11-04_ux-research_05-chapter-4-qualitative-research-methods]].
- Ground rules reduce bias during diverge: enforce quiet time, ban "no" and
  "but", consider anonymity for sensitive topics, and pick tools that minimize
  distraction; for converge, prefer synchronous tools that enforce timeboxing
  [[2024-05-30_diverge-converge]].
- A design studio compresses the whole arc into a few hours across four
  segments — Sketch, Present and Critique, Converge, Prioritize — using Crazy 8s
  for sketching, Six Thinking Hats to structure critique, SCAMPER to combine
  rather than merely agree, and the $100 Test for prioritization, where monetary
  framing triggers deeper investment than arbitrary points
  [[2017-07-02_facilitating-design-studio-workshop]].

### The activity toolkit

- The facilitator's toolkit has three parts: materials (sticky notes, markers,
  whiteboards, voting dots), activities, and techniques such as intentional
  silence, balancing, and linking [[2020-04-19_workshop-facilitation-101]].
- Seven foundational activities recombine into almost any exercise: post up,
  affinity diagramming, landscape mapping, storyboarding, forced ranking, role
  playing, and playback [[2020-03-01_workshop-activities]].
- **Affinity diagramming** — generate, cluster, prioritize
  [[2024-04-26_affinity-diagram]]. Post the focus question visibly, since
  without one the groupings are ambiguous and strategically useless; enforce
  label rules (for example, labels must be verbs describing the problem) to
  block keyword matching, where items are grouped by shared words rather than
  meaning; run multiple rounds with different focus questions on large datasets;
  and tolerate the early "organized chaos" where participants sort by different
  strategies, because premature alignment behind one framework suppresses better
  approaches [[2023-03-19_affinity-diagramming-pitfalls]]. Small clusters still
  hold value, and the discussion while building the diagram matters more than
  the final artifact [[2024-04-26_affinity-diagram]].
- **Dot voting** — participants get a fixed number of dots and vote quietly
  without lobbying; the distribution is a heat map of group preference and a
  shared artifact. It gives everyone equal voice and prevents HIPPO bias. Tune
  it: give voters research time beforehand, vote digitally or anonymously to
  hide influence, dictate voting order (junior first), rank dots for weighted
  votes, colour-code dots by criteria. Break ties by revoting on the tied
  candidates only [[2019-07-07_dot-voting]]. Colour-coded dots can also encode
  reactions — agree, disagree, surprising, essential
  [[2022-02-06_ux-vision-statements]].
- **Parking lot** — a visible shared artifact capturing off-topic comments,
  questions, and ideas so the discussion stays on track without dismissing
  anyone. Introduce the concept, purpose, and ground rules at the start;
  identify off-scope items quickly and time-box borderline discussions; cluster
  items by affinity as they arrive; apply the "W.W.W." framework (who does what,
  when) to each item; and include everything in the written follow-up. Parking
  items without follow-up insults the contributor and damages team dynamics
  [[2019-09-08_parking-lots]], [[2017-08-13_group-ideation]],
  [[2020-09-20_service-blueprinting-workshops]],
  [[2021-03-28_scenario-mapping-personas]].
- **Structured prioritization** — establish 4–8 standardized, agreed-upon
  criteria mixing user and business objectives, have members rate individually,
  average the scores, then discuss for alignment
  [[2021-05-23_roadmapping-workshop]]. Forced ranking weighs items directly
  against each other to produce a strict order
  [[2020-03-01_workshop-activities]]. Generate needs statements from pain points
  with fill-in-the-blank prompts ("I need _____ so that _____") and dot-vote to
  pick the ones worth ideating on, which also prevents jumping to solutions
  [[2020-07-05_journey-mapping-workshop]].
- **Colour-coded sticky notes** to separate categories of contribution — design
  ideas, open questions, comments [[2021-03-28_scenario-mapping-personas]] — or
  to mark ideas for addressing fail points
  [[2020-09-20_service-blueprinting-workshops]].

### Getting everyone to participate

- Create an inviting space: explicitly invite quiet participants to contribute,
  and watch body language for people wanting to speak, so it is not only the
  eager contributors who are heard [[2020-04-19_workshop-facilitation-101]].
- Use intentional silence. After an open-ended question, waiting creates room
  for varied perspectives; continuous talking signals the facilitator is not
  interested in others' views. Count to seven before speaking — the first four
  seconds feel awkward, the next three are where genuine thinking happens
  [[2019-05-05_intentional-silence-ux]],
  [[2021-11-28_facilitate-ux-workshop-user-test]]. Tolerance for silence varies
  by culture: English speakers become uncomfortable at about four seconds,
  Japanese speakers tolerate 8.2 seconds, and Finnish speakers also value
  silence; respect those norms when facilitating
  [[2019-05-05_intentional-silence-ux]].
- Structured turn-taking equalizes airtime: round-robin gives everyone a turn,
  quota systems request a set number of contributions per participant
  [[2021-02-21_dominating-workshop-participants]],
  [[2016-10-23_design-critiques]].
- Silent activities remove the advantage of louder voices entirely: silent
  post-up and brainwriting ensure introverts' ideas land
  [[2021-02-21_dominating-workshop-participants]]. Offering both written and
  verbal participation options serves the same purpose
  [[2022-07-31_focus-groups-definition]].
- Warm participants up and keep the pressure low; emphasize quantity over
  quality so quieter people feel safe contributing
  [[2017-08-13_group-ideation]], [[2022-07-31_focus-groups-definition]],
  [[2023-01-15_groupthink-in-ux]]. Icebreakers and introductions open the
  session [[2020-09-20_service-blueprinting-workshops]].

### Managing dominance and groupthink

- Three archetypes dominate for different reasons: helpful HIPPOs who intimidate
  unintentionally, eager enthusiasts driven by passion, and enlightened experts
  who are defensive about their authority
  [[2021-02-21_dominating-workshop-participants]],
  [[2023-03-19_affinity-diagramming-pitfalls]].
- Prevent before you intervene: invite known dominators only to the relevant
  segments, balance team composition, establish ground rules, and clarify shared
  goals [[2021-02-21_dominating-workshop-participants]].
- Then escalate through three tactics — **redirect** (point back to a visible
  group-purpose statement, or park the contribution, signalling it is valued but
  not now), **balance** (round-robin, quotas), and **silence** (silent post-up,
  brainwriting) [[2021-02-21_dominating-workshop-participants]].
- Groupthink is the related failure: teams prioritize consensus over
  alternatives, showing up as collective rationalization, illusion of unanimity
  from silence, self-censorship, and direct pressure on dissenters
  [[2023-01-15_groupthink-in-ux]]. Against it, name groupthink explicitly at the
  start, communicate norms that value all ideas and encourage disagreement,
  ideate solo before sharing, and let dot voting democratize the decision rather
  than the facilitator deciding [[2023-01-15_groupthink-in-ux]]. Teams also
  fixate on the first idea proposed; set initial suggestions aside deliberately
  and widen the option set before settling [[2017-08-13_group-ideation]]. In
  virtual settings, which exacerbate groupthink, rotate hosts and use anonymous
  follow-up activities [[2023-01-15_groupthink-in-ux]].
- The same dynamics limit focus groups: dominant personalities skew
  participation, groupthink suppresses disagreement, and under-facilitation
  captures only the loudest voices — which is why recruitment across diverse
  seniority levels, warmup activities, and open-ended questions with the funnel
  technique matter [[2022-07-31_focus-groups-definition]],
  [[2023-01-15_groupthink-in-ux]].

### Getting non-designers to sketch

A four-variable formula lowers the anxiety of participants who say they cannot
draw: fat markers (thick tips prevent granular refinement), tiny spaces (index
cards, sticky notes, 8-up sheets — shrinking the space shrinks the anxiety),
time limits (the most important variable; use a visible countdown, since people
expand to fill available time with no gain in quality or quantity), and ugly
examples (hastily drawn basic shapes; polished examples send the silent message
that sketching belongs to creative roles)
[[2019-12-22_how-to-get-stakeholders-to-sketch]]. The payoff is buy-in, idea
diversity, shared ownership, and a faster process
[[2019-12-22_how-to-get-stakeholders-to-sketch]]. In sketching phases,
emphasize quantity over polish; time constraints prevent overthinking and
self-censoring [[2017-07-02_facilitating-design-studio-workshop]].

### Facilitating critique

- Critique is a conversation aimed at analyzing whether a design meets its
  objectives, not an approval gate; it rests on a clear scope for the
  conversation, agreed design objectives, and conversation rather than
  command-and-control feedback [[2016-10-23_design-critiques]].
- The designated facilitator sets scope, chooses participants, asks reformulating
  questions that connect feedback back to design goals, documents the discussion,
  and follows up with action items [[2016-10-23_design-critiques]].
- Common failures: not agreeing on personas or objectives beforehand,
  over-long sessions, taking feedback personally, rushing to solve problems
  during the critique, and dwelling only on negatives
  [[2016-10-23_design-critiques]]. Structured lenses such as Six Thinking Hats
  make critique more objective than free-form feedback
  [[2017-07-02_facilitating-design-studio-workshop]].

### Facilitating a stakeholder design meeting

[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_04-chapter-3-design-the-meeting]]
applies facilitation to the review meeting rather than the workshop, and its
guidance runs parallel to the sections above:

- **Open by setting the context** — the goal, a summary of decisions already
  taken, the position of the work on the design-process timeline, the kind of
  feedback needed now, then the goal restated. A few sentences or one slide is
  enough; the point is to spare stakeholders the switching cost of arriving from
  other meetings.
- **Structure the session for retention** — break the content into distinct
  chunks separated by visual transitions rather than running one long
  presentation, and use primacy and recency, repetition, surprise and mixed
  media deliberately to decide what people leave with.
- **Clear the distractions before the room does it for you** — placeholder
  content and visual clutter (stock images, lorem ipsum, unaligned layouts) can
  capture an entire discussion, and the source's rule is that a given
  distraction should only ever happen once.
- **Anticipate the reactions** — write down the objections you expect, prepare
  alternative designs that answer them and collect supporting data, on the
  observation that most people react predictably from their role and their
  values.
- **Line up a support network** — align one-on-one beforehand with people who
  agree with the decisions, so someone other than the designer can advocate for
  the work in the room.
- **Rehearse** — a printed agenda, speaking the material out loud in advance and
  a short team huddle before the session, so the facilitator has the capacity to
  be present rather than reconstructing their argument live.

### Facilitating a one-on-one research session

*UX Research* turns the same craft toward moderating an individual research
session rather than a group workshop, and treats it as sharing soft
facilitation skills with the group case:

- Warming up a session, managing the pacing of questions and breaks, taking
  notes unobtrusively, and knowing when to pause or shift topics all make a
  research session feel more natural to the person on the other side of the
  table [[2016-11-04_ux-research_10-chapter-9-managing-people-during-research]].
- Reading a participant's nonverbal cues and managing one's own body
  language — staying open and receptive rather than letting fatigue or
  disengagement show — are core facilitation skills that produce richer data
  and stronger rapport; knowing when to shift topics or probe deeper follows
  from those physical signals
  [[2016-11-04_ux-research_12-chapter-11-facilitating-research]].

### Workshop types

- Five recurring types, each with its own purpose: **discovery** (build
  consensus on current state and milestones), **empathy** (shift the team from
  features-first to users-first), **design** (rapidly generate multiple solution
  ideas), **prioritization** (agree what matters most, counter scope creep), and
  **critique** (evaluate designs against user needs and principles). They can be
  scaled, combined, and adapted [[2019-12-08_5-ux-workshops]], and each admits a
  different degree of real-user participation [[2023-06-11_including-users-workshops]].
- Method-specific formats add their own facilitation demands: journey mapping
  [[2020-07-05_journey-mapping-workshop]], design studios
  [[2017-07-02_facilitating-design-studio-workshop]], service blueprinting
  [[2020-09-20_service-blueprinting-workshops]], roadmapping
  [[2021-05-23_roadmapping-workshop]], scenario mapping
  [[2021-03-28_scenario-mapping-personas]], and vision-setting
  [[2022-02-06_ux-vision-statements]], all catalogued together in
  [[2021-09-12_facilitating-ux-workshops-guide]].
- Scenario mapping asks the facilitator to keep scenarios high-level and free of
  UI or solution specifics, since design detail biases ideation, and to include
  only the few high-priority user needs relevant to the task — excessive detail
  produces overly tailored solutions [[2021-03-28_scenario-mapping-personas]].
- Blueprinting workshops fail for reasons that precede facilitation: scope
  services you control, target 8–12 customer actions, build a core team of 2–4
  influential allies, and skip high-fidelity visualization when the goal is team
  alignment [[2020-03-22_blueprint-fails-fixes]].
- Vision workshops run in three stages — gather research on the current state,
  envision collaboratively (share findings, brainstorm and prioritize
  future-state attributes with coloured dots, craft statements from the top
  attributes), then plan the processes, people, tools, and governance needed to
  close the gap [[2022-02-06_ux-vision-statements]].

### Facilitating a story-mapping session

Jeff Patton describes his own practice of facilitating a story-mapping
exercise in enough detail to read as a distinct, named set of techniques
rather than general workshop advice. Framing discussions before the group
digs into detail, writing down ideas quickly as the group talks so nothing
"vaporizes," and stopping the group to finish telling the big story
left-to-right before diving into vertical detail are practices he applies
directly, alongside creating spatial relationships (left-to-right for
sequence, top-to-bottom for detail) that communicate meaning without needing
to be explained in words
[[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_06-1-the-big-picture]]. He calls the
underlying move **talk and doc**: write ideas onto cards or sticky notes as
the team tells a story, so the artifact becomes something to point back to
rather than a record kept only in memory
[[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_06-1-the-big-picture]].

Running the exercise itself, in Patton's account, is participatory rather
than presented: he has the group build a map together (his teaching example is
mapping a morning routine, then a piece of software, using the same
mechanics), organizing sticky notes left-to-right for narrative flow, adding
vertical depth for alternatives and edge cases by asking what-about questions,
and grouping tasks under higher-level activities
[[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_10-5-you-already-know-how]]. The value Patton
draws from this is squarely a facilitation one: the shared drawing and
discussion surface disagreements the group did not know it had (his example is
whether to brush teeth before or after breakfast) and force the group to
resolve what matters versus what is merely personal preference, revealing what
people actually know and do not know about users and their work
[[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_10-5-you-already-know-how]]. That mapping
practice feeds directly into the **story workshop**, Patton's name for the
small (three-to-five-person), tightly scoped conversation that polishes rough
stories into buildable units. He is pointed about the distinction from an
ordinary meeting: "this is a workshop, not a meeting. Meeting is the word that
has become a euphemism for unproductive collaboration. A story workshop needs
to be filled with lots of productive discussion, hand waving, whiteboard
drawing, and sketching"
[[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_21-16-refine-define-and-build]]. Story
workshops close only once the group reaches agreed acceptance criteria and
frequently reveal that a story needs splitting
[[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_21-16-refine-define-and-build]].

Two further practices are specific to Patton's facilitation of these
conversations rather than to workshops in general. First, running multiple
parallel conversation channels deliberately: physical, face-to-face work at
whiteboards and sticky notes for building shared understanding, and digital
tools such as Cardboard (co-created by David Hussman) for tracking progress
and letting remote participants build the same map concurrently with people
mapping on a physical wall — chosen for the job at hand rather than used
interchangeably [[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_13-8-its-not-all-on-the-card]].
Second, an explicit etiquette rule for the story conversations that make up a
workshop: "be respectful of the expertise of others in the conversation. Don't
tell a highly trained technical person how to do her work. Don't tell someone
intimately familiar with users and their work that he doesn't understand. Ask
questions, and genuinely try to learn from each other"
[[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_12-7-telling-better-stories]]. Patton also
credits Alistair Cockburn's term *information radiator* for the practice of
posting information visibly on the wall so it "radiates" into the room, as
against information kept only in tools, which goes stale unattended
[[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_13-8-its-not-all-on-the-card]]. In the
workshops that finalize story details before building, he names the "three
amigos" — a tester, a developer, and a product/business/design representative
— as the specific composition that does the work, borrowing the term from the
1986 film of that name
[[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_17-12-rock-breakers]].

### Facilitating with generative AI

- Prepare participants for uneven AI literacy: run an AI-based icebreaker to
  build confidence with the interface, upload context documents (company
  description, personas, research, constraints) ahead of time, create a custom
  AI instance pre-loaded with team context and guidelines, and hand out
  adaptable sample prompts following the CAREful framework (Context, Ask, Rules,
  Examples). Have everyone use the same LLM so troubleshooting stays about
  prompting rather than tool differences [[2025-05-30_preparing-ai-workshops]].
- The standard diverge-converge structure breaks down because AI produces
  hundreds of ideas in minutes. Insert an intermediate narrowing step where AI
  ranks ideas against evaluation criteria the group has agreed on (feasibility,
  desirability, viability, or custom) before group discussion
  [[2025-06-06_facilitating-ai-workshops]].
- Budget more time, not less: 8–10 minutes per prompt against 5 without AI, to
  write, read, and iterate. Let small groups co-write prompts and explore
  outputs together, and document digitally, since shared docs and whiteboards
  handle AI's long outputs better than physical sticky notes
  [[2025-06-06_facilitating-ai-workshops]].

### After the workshop

- Help participants identify who is responsible for what next; collaborative
  ownership of next steps is what makes the work continue
  [[2020-04-19_workshop-facilitation-101]]. Document action items, next steps,
  and point persons [[2024-04-26_affinity-diagram]].
- Follow up on parked items and open questions in writing; public accountability
  is what stops the parking lot becoming a graveyard [[2019-09-08_parking-lots]],
  [[2020-09-20_service-blueprinting-workshops]].
- Refine the artifact afterwards: clean it up for shareability, layer in
  quantitative data supporting the qualitative findings, add flow lines,
  emotional indicators and clear language, and test it with someone unfamiliar
  to check comprehension [[2020-09-20_service-blueprinting-workshops]],
  [[2020-03-22_blueprint-fails-fixes]].
- Capture artifacts, build prototypes, and keep momentum
  [[2020-07-05_journey-mapping-workshop]]. Communicate outputs with engaging
  formats — documents, videos, vision boards, prototypes — to hold the team
  accountable and engage the wider organization
  [[2022-02-06_ux-vision-statements]].
- Treat mapped artifacts as living documents: version numbers, update dates, and
  a scheduled review (for example six months after creation)
  [[2020-03-22_blueprint-fails-fixes]].
