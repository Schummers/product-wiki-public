# Studio Fundraising

## Definition

Studio fundraising is the work of financing the studio itself, as distinct from financing the companies it launches. The corpus splits it into two objects. The first is the capital that pays for the studio's operations: the core team, the ideation, validation and MVP work done before any company exists, and the years of losses before exits arrive. The second, which the 2022 research treats as increasingly standard, is a separate investment fund raised alongside the operating company so the studio can follow its companies into Seed and later rounds, earn management fees to subsidise operations, and take carry on exits ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_11-studio-structure]], [[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_17-raising-a-fund]]).

Both books describe the same barrier: studios are a new, unproven asset class, so investors who understand venture capital struggle to understand or trust a studio. Szigeti's playbook (2019) answers with a list of five funding sources and a rule that credibility of the founder plus early traction is what unlocks institutional money ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_20-fundraising-and-investor-relations]]). Kannan and Peterman (2022), drawing on 19 studio interviews and a financial model, quantify the picture: operating companies raise around $10M to $20M from a small base of high net worth individuals and family offices, 11 of 19 studios have already raised an investment fund and 4 more plan to, and a fund is modelled as one of the four levers that decide whether the studio economics work at all ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_13-studio-financing]], [[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_05-strategy]], [[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_15-model-overview]]).

## Practice

### Two things to fund, not one

Kannan and Peterman insist on the distinction between the operating entity and the fund. The operating company's budget covers early ideation, research, validation and testing, funded by a mix of capital raises, external revenue and a share of any fund's management fees; it receives common founder shares for that pre-Seed work. The fund is a separate legal entity that deploys capital in Seed and later rounds, holds preferred equity, and earns management fees and carry ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_11-studio-structure]]). In dual-entity studios the two are financed from separate LP pools, and the fund typically has a larger and more diverse investor base than the operating entity ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_13-studio-financing]]). Revenue earned by the operating company (billing back, corporate co-builds) sits in a third category: it reduces how much has to be raised, but it is not fundraising ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_16-revenue-generation]]).

Szigeti's chapter predates this vocabulary but describes the same separation in one of his five sources: a studio-specific pre-seed fund that finances venture creation, makes cost structures transparent, and separates the studio's runway from the portfolio's funding ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_20-fundraising-and-investor-relations]]).

### Funding the operating company

**Szigeti's five sources (2019).** The playbook lists them with a short judgement on each ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_20-fundraising-and-investor-relations]]):

- **Founder capital.** Most early successful studios were started by established entrepreneurs or institutional founders with deep pockets. Self-funding the first 5 to 10 ventures removes fundraising friction and builds traction before asking outsiders.
- **VC funding to the studio.** Possible but difficult; it requires founder credibility and demonstrated traction, and the pitch is that the studio reduces per-venture risk through operational expertise, diversification and shared resources.
- **Corporate funding.** An increasing trend, where a corporation dedicates funds to align venture production with its strategic goals; often structured so the corporation takes majority returns while the studio keeps operational autonomy.
- **Family offices and angels.** Growing interest in studios as a new asset class; the pitch is a diversified portfolio of new startups with lower failure risk at portfolio level and better equity upside than a single angel investment.
- **Studio-specific pre-seed funds.** An emerging model, described above.

He adds a sixth path that is not fundraising: bootstrapping with agency revenue, running a software or marketing agency in parallel and using its profit to fund internal ventures, at the cost of balancing short-term revenue against long-term venture commitment ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_20-fundraising-and-investor-relations]]).

**Kannan and Peterman's observed profile (2022).** Because studios differ from the VC structures investors know, studio leaders find it hard to convince them to back the operating entity, and the difficulty is amplified in less mature ecosystems. Operating companies therefore keep a small LP base of high net worth individuals and family offices who want direct exposure to early-stage ventures, accept the absence of exit guarantees, and can be patient over 5 to 7 years. Typical raises are $10M to $20M, with a few outliers reaching $50M. The authors expect traditional LPs to join at the operating-company level as studios demonstrate consistent returns and keep high initial ownership ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_13-studio-financing]]). Their base-case model, a single entity with no fund and no revenue, needs $10M of upfront capital and runs at a loss for five or more years before exits begin ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_15-model-overview]]).

The two books agree on the investor types (founders' own money, family offices, angels, corporates) and on the reason it is hard. Szigeti offers VCs as a possible, difficult source; Kannan and Peterman do not list VCs among typical operating-company backers and instead see VC-style LPs entering through the separate fund.

### The credibility barrier, and what it costs when it is not cleared

Szigeti's rule is blunt: the key is credibility, of the studio founder and of the traction. Once one investment closes and trust exists, the second and third deals close faster ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_20-fundraising-and-investor-relations]]). Kannan and Peterman add that established studios such as High Alpha, PSL and Atomic make the model look easy, while studio founders in general face extraordinary stress securing enough funding to launch. Of the approaches available (upfront LP capital, bill backs, secondary sales), upfront capital from patient LPs is the one most aligned with the studio's mission ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_23-implications-for-stakeholders]]).

The Laicos story in the playbook shows the failure mode. Ryan Negri's Tampa studio could not raise: local investors were dated in their practices, wanted $1M+ in revenue before investing, or saw the studio model as a threat to their own venture role. Entrepreneurs supported the concept; investors did not. Investors in Las Vegas and Austin offered to fund a relocated studio, but the team could not uproot. Laicos pivoted to agency work to survive, which demotivated a team that had signed on to build products, and the studio arm was eventually shut down, with Negri moving to a corporate accelerator and later venture roles. The chapter's lesson is that capital access and investor sophistication are prerequisites for a studio's survival, not optional extras ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_11-ahead-of-time]]).

### Investor relations and the deal package

Szigeti's second half of the chapter is practical ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_20-fundraising-and-investor-relations]]):

- Approach investors centrally, as a studio, rather than separately for each venture.
- Educate them on the model: explain portfolio diversification, show early traction, and build trust by delivering quality companies.
- Anticipate the cap-table objection (if the studio holds a majority early, the CEO lacks skin in the game) by showing the post-seed adjustments that raise team ownership, and let the venture CEOs speak to investors directly. The equity side of that objection is on [[Equity Ownership]].
- A minimal fundraising deck covers vision, financial model, team, portfolio and roadmap.

### Raising an investment fund

**Why studios raise one.** The strategy chapter sets out the problem the fund solves. Studios capture cheap founding equity but are heavily diluted in later rounds, from 30 to 45% at founding to as low as 5% at exit. A fund lets the studio invest its pro-rata allocation in subsequent rounds and hold a target stake (often 20%), and its management fees help cover operating expenses and extend runway before exits. Of the 19 studios interviewed, 11 had raised a fund and 4 planned to; about half use their fund for investments outside their own portfolio, which moves them toward traditional VC economics ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_05-strategy]]).

**What the model says it is worth.** With four closed-ended funds over a decade (starting at $45M and growing to $120M, 3-year horizons, 2% management fee, 20% carry), the operating company's incremental capital need falls from $10M to $4M and the studio stays near break-even through year 5. Fund and operating company together return 37% IRR and 24x MoM, against 24% IRR and 9x MoM for the operating company alone. Carry reinvested in the holding company accelerates growth without further external raises, and with one early exit the studio needs only $24M cumulative capital while the operating company reaches 50% IRR and 57x MoM. The authors conclude that most studios are moving toward including a fund, and that having one makes the operating entity itself a more attractive investment because fees subsidise operations ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_17-raising-a-fund]]). In the four-lever framework, raising a fund is rated high-impact ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_15-model-overview]]). In the best-in-class case, a fund investing in 20% of follow-on rounds through Series B, with the same 2% and 20% terms, adds $91M of cash by year 10 and reaches 26% gross IRR and 10x MoM once fully exited by year 13 ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_20-best-in-class-case]]).

**Partner economics.** In a dual-entity studio, partners participate in both the operating company's founder equity and the fund's carry and fees, which the authors call "double dipping" and describe as potentially more lucrative than traditional VC when the studio succeeds ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_11-studio-structure]]).

### Choosing the fund type: closed-ended or evergreen

Kannan and Peterman devote a chapter to this choice ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_12-fund-type]]):

- **Closed-ended funds** are what most interviewed studios chose, because the VC community accepts them and they give LPs a clear picture of portfolio size and time horizon. The typical progression is a small first fund ($10M to $20M), a larger second ($50M to $100M) and a third of $100M to $200M to support follow-ons and protect founder equity through later rounds. Fund size directly limits how far the studio can follow its companies; studios that cannot take their pro-rata often syndicate those rights to LPs, which again aligns them with traditional VC.
- **Evergreen (open-ended) funds**, used by a minority (AlleyCorp, Polymath, Founders, Rule 1), give maximum flexibility: LPs can join and exit as the studio matures, operating budgets can change with venture needs, and strategy can pivot without LP constraints. Some evergreen studios offer guaranteed dividends of 5 to 20% on preferred shares over 5 to 10 years instead of carry. The cost is fundraising difficulty: LPs understand 2% and 20% far better than a reinvestment model, so evergreen structures require exceptionally patient LPs or prior venture success.
- **Cycle length.** Studios raise every 3 to 4 years, shorter than traditional VC, and justify each raise with unrealised valuations and early exits, which creates pressure to show portfolio success quickly.

The stakeholder chapter closes the question without picking a winner: studios succeed with open-ended and closed-ended funds, with dual-entity and single-fund designs, and the right choice depends on philosophy, local environment and fundraising constraints rather than on any structural rule ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_23-implications-for-stakeholders]]).

### Structural decisions that shape the raise

Two further choices from the structure chapter ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_11-studio-structure]]):

- **Should the fund own part of the operating company?** Doing so aligns LP incentives with studio operations, but it makes the fund look less like a traditional VC vehicle and can complicate fundraising.
- **Single entity or dual entity?** A single fund entity is simpler, but covering both operations and investments out of a 2% management fee requires an exceptionally large fund ($100M to $200M), which puts it out of reach for most first-time studio founders. Most studios observed migrated to the dual-entity model by raising a VC fund.

The structures themselves are described on [[Studio Structure]].

### Raising less: revenue as a substitute for capital

The 2022 model treats revenue as the other way to close the funding gap. Billing back post-incorporation services at cost cuts the operating company's capital need from $10M to $7M if incorporation follows validation, and to $5M if billing starts before incorporation, though founders rarely accept the latter. Corporate co-builds at 40% margin, ramping from one to four per year, cut the need to $9M; combined with post-validation billing they yield 39% savings and cap annual burn at $2.1M, at the cost of team time and exit uncertainty ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_16-revenue-generation]]). In the best-in-class case, these revenue streams plus an intentional early exit reduce the year-one raise to $200K, with a $75M exit at year 4 covering operations through year 6 ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_20-best-in-class-case]]). Szigeti's agency-bootstrap path is the 2019 version of the same idea, with the same warning that client work and venture building compete for the team's commitment ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_20-fundraising-and-investor-relations]], [[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_11-ahead-of-time]]). The revenue mechanics are on [[Revenue Model]].

### Where the market is heading

Kannan and Peterman place the studio asset class between the innovation trigger and the peak of inflated expectations of a hype cycle. In this phase LP demand for studio exposure outpaces the supply of studios, so fundraising becomes feasible even for non-premier founders. As the cycle matures, LPs will distinguish the strongest founders and concentrate capital with established studios that have track records. Three expansion paths feed the supply: traditional VCs building their own studios, serial entrepreneurs starting studios, and established studios seeding new ones with part of their larger funds, since scaling a studio's own operations is constrained and partners resist diluting their carry ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_25-industrys-path-forward]]). The financing chapter makes the complementary prediction that traditional LPs will move into operating-company rounds as returns are demonstrated ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_13-studio-financing]]).

### What the corpus does not settle

- The dollar figures ($10M to $20M operating raises, the fund progression, the modelled returns) all come from the 2022 book; the 2019 playbook gives sources and principles but no amounts.
- The 2022 financial model is described by its authors as simplified, and its fund assumptions (four funds, 3-year horizons, 2% and 20%) are inputs, not observations.
- Neither book resolves whether a studio should raise the operating company first and the fund later, or both at once; the observed pattern is migration from single to dual entity over time ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_11-studio-structure]]).
