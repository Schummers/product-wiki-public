# UX Career

## Definition

A UX career is professional work centred on user experience, pursued in a field
that is still young enough to lack standardisation. There are no universal
career ladders, job titles vary enormously (134 unique titles reported by 2019
survey respondents, down from 210 in 2013), and practitioners arrive from widely
varied backgrounds [[2020-04-19_ux-career-advice]] [[2024-08-16_stages-of-ux-career-progression]].
The absence is measurable inside companies too:
[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_01-why-i-wrote-this-book]] reports,
from the 2019 Design Career Index, that 71% of companies have no formalised
career progression, and that the smaller the company the lower the chance it has
a ladder — a company of more than 100 employees is twice as likely to have one as
a company of 25 to 49. It gives the stakes on both sides: McKinsey found lack of
career development and advancement to be the number one reason people quit their
jobs (41% of those surveyed, ahead of inadequate compensation at 36% and
uncaring or uninspiring leaders at 34%), and Gallup puts the cost of replacing an
employee at between 50% and 200% of that employee's annual salary.
Long-run, the field has grown exponentially — from roughly 10 practitioners in
1950 to about 1 million by 2017 — and its growth has been driven by structural
business pressure (the PC revolution made the user the buyer; the web made
experience the gatekeeper to revenue) rather than by cultural recognition alone
[[2017-12-24_100-years-ux]]. Growth has also been geographic: survey responses
came from 65 countries in 2019 versus 38 in 2013
[[2020-04-19_ux-career-advice]].

That youth cuts both ways. It gives practitioners unusual freedom to define
their own roles, specialise, and move across industries, and satisfaction is
stable and high (5.4 on a 1–7 scale, identical to 2013), driven by impact,
learning, autonomy and community [[2020-04-19_ux-career-advice]]
[[2024-07-05_best-things-about-working-in-ux]]. It also leaves UX politically
weak inside organisations: research and design are treated as long-term
investments rather than immediate revenue drivers, so they are cut first when
budgets tighten [[2025-03-21_ux-layoff-guide]] [[2025-01-10_ux-reset-2025]]. An
earlier source puts part of that weakness inside the designer's own remit:
[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_01-introduction]] holds that the most
successful designers have a deep understanding of how design affects business and
its value within the organisation, and that design schools teach visual skills
while leaving out what tech companies need — how design affects business, how to
work with engineers, how to present work to non-designers, how to research and
understand customers, how to optimise design for KPIs and how to measure a
design's performance. The lack of standardisation shows there too, and early: as
of 2018 the Product Designer title already meant different things at different
companies, with different expectations of the role's scope
[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_02-why-i-wrote-this-book]].
[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_01-why-i-wrote-this-book]] carries
that diagnosis into the organisation five years later, and names the same
cultural cause — the "dribbblization of design", years of emphasis on aesthetics
that left designers short of the communication, stakeholder management,
ownership, strategic thinking and hiring skills that would change how design is
perceived. Its other systemic factors are structural: individual contributors who
resist management are left choosing between leaving for a larger company with
staff or principal roles, staying stuck at senior, or moving reluctantly into
management where design work gradually disappears; design leaders capable of
building development plans are scarce; and overlapping competencies between
product managers and designers can shrink design's strategic role back to visual
work.
The individual counterpart of that weakness is communication: the success of
design work is said to depend on getting support from everyone on the team,
without which "our designs will never see the light of day", and feeling
overruled by stakeholders who know little about design is described as a common
designer experience — which makes articulation a career variable rather than a
soft extra
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_01-preface]]
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_02-chapter-1-great-designers-are-great-communicators]].
The mid-2020s made this concrete — job listings fell to 70% of 2021 levels,
organisational UX maturity regressed, and hiring power returned to employers
[[2025-01-10_ux-reset-2025]] [[2026-01-16_ux-hiring-insights]]. By 2026 the
sources describe a stabilising but still competitive market where senior and
generalist roles recover faster than entry-level ones
[[2026-01-16_state-of-ux-2026]].

## Practice

### Career stages and progression

Progression is best read through skills, recognition and strategic impact rather
than titles: [[2024-08-16_stages-of-ux-career-progression]] describes five
stages — Novice, Contributor, Practitioner, Specialist, Visionary — and notes
that people frequently hold a title implying one stage while functioning at
another. Each stage has its own obstacle: novices struggle to apply learning to
real work, contributors to integrate feedback, practitioners to balance user
against business needs, specialists to broaden, visionaries to stay relevant.
Organisational context sets the ceiling — hierarchy, UX maturity and role
specialisation determine what growth is available, and flat organisations offer
less vertical movement but more room to specialise. Advancement also depends on
knowledge, experience and responsibilities, not skills alone.

Guidance should be matched to stage: [[2024-07-10_growing-your-ux-career-study-guide]]
organises its resources by level (breaking in, entry level, mid and senior)
precisely because people enter UX at different points in life, and because what
hiring managers look for differs between designers and researchers and between
junior and senior roles. The general advice for newcomers is to keep learning
and stay curious, respect others, stay confident in the face of impostor
syndrome, and take charge of your own progression
[[2020-04-19_ux-career-advice]].
[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_02-why-you-should-read-this-book]]
segments the same way — students and graduates, junior, mid-level, senior, solo
designers and design managers — and singles out the solo designer at a small
company, managed by non-designers and without design peers or design leadership,
as the reader who most has to take ownership of their own development rather than
wait for the organisation to provide it.

The organisational side of that ceiling has its own prerequisites.
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_12-chapter-11-how-executives-can-help-designers]]
argues that a designer's ability to grow depends on conditions the employer
controls: autonomy to make decisions and be accountable for them, access to the
executive suite, and recognition that design expertise is worth as much as
finance or engineering. Its structural version of the argument is that design
leadership has to exist at the executive level, since without designers in
positions of authority even a talented team works under constraints that prevent
the best solution — and it holds that organisations creating those conditions
attract and retain better people.

Whatever the stage, the sources treat development as ongoing practice rather
than consumption. [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_18-chapter-fifteen-whats-next]]
makes the point about the gap between reading and doing: translating individual
learning into team practice is hard alone and easier among peers facing the same
constraints, so it points readers toward a newsletter, a membership community,
skills deep-dives, master classes and one-on-one coaching, each answering a
different learning need — deliberate practice on specific methods, accountability
in a small group, or guidance on a particular organisational context.

An earlier and different ladder runs through skills rather than recognition.
[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_02-why-i-wrote-this-book]] relays Julie
Zhuo's three stages — Craft and Execution (mastering design tools and
aesthetics), Product Thinking (understanding what a good outcome is and how to
design an experience that leads to it), and Influencing Skills (communication and
collaboration) — and is mostly concerned with why designers stall at the first.
It names four systemic barriers: little communication between companies and the
design community, confusion around the Product Designer title, differing
expectations of the role's scope, and the absence of educational resources
teaching product thinking. It adds a cultural one, the "Dribbblisation of
Design": communities that celebrate visual work spread the misconception that
product design is visual design. The consequences it reports are stage-specific —
junior designers over-invest in visual skills instead of problem-solving
portfolios, mid-level designers get stuck in visual-only roles, and companies
never see the value designers could deliver on business problems. This map and
the five stages in [[2024-08-16_stages-of-ux-career-progression]] are not the
same instrument: one tracks recognition and strategic impact, the other the
widening of what a designer is competent to do.

### Choosing a path

[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_03-1-design-career-paths]] maps the
landscape as six paths — individual contributor, people manager, freelancer or
contractor, specialist, business owner, and product manager — and argues the
long-term IC path was considered a failure until recently, because senior IC
positions did not exist; staff and principal roles are now making it viable at
large organisations. It reports that Square's internal levelling document states
explicitly that becoming a manager is not a promotion but a lateral move to the
management track, and that Airbnb positions management the same way, precisely to
avoid pushing talented ICs into people management they do not want. On the other
paths: contracting is growing (it reports half of Google's workers were
contractors in 2018, that flexibility ranked third among job-choice criteria in
2022, and relays Naval Ravikant's prediction that the highest-quality work will
become available in a gig fashion, taken by "free agents"); specialisation in
research, motion or operations allows deeper fulfilment but constrains you to
larger organisations and specific company types and reduces management options;
business ownership carries the highest impact potential, and designers are said
to be well-positioned for it since they understand users' needs, have a grasp of
marketing, and know how to build and ship; and product management has the highest
promotion ceiling, director, VP and Chief Product Officer roles being more common
than their design equivalents. The chapter closes on a comparison of the paths
that the author presents as subjective and drawn from his own experience, and
notes some paths can be combined. Its specialisation caveat sits alongside
[[2025-03-28_return-ux-generalist]] below, which argues the market itself is
moving back toward generalism.

Choosing between them is treated as a values exercise before it is a market one.
[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_04-2-setting-career-goals]] opens on
Stephen Covey's "begin with the end in mind" and offers exercises rather than a
recommendation: score each option against your values (impact, work-life balance,
financial outcome, learning, reputation, leadership) weighted by how much each
matters to you; write your values on one sheet and the outcomes of the change on
another and compare them; visualise a day several years into the chosen path —
who you interact with, what work you do, where you are, how you feel — then
validate those assumptions against reality; score competing job offers on
criteria such as getting things done, quality of life, growth, compensation,
satisfying work and future success; use a coin flip as a psychological trigger
rather than a decision-maker, reading the relief or doubt you feel when it lands;
and apply the ABZ framework, knowing where you are (A) and where you want to be
(Z) while focusing only on the next step (B). The author reports the values
exercise also served him after the decision, when a significant change to the
business made him question whether he still wanted to be part of it. This is the
same starting point as [[2025-01-24_advice-ux-job-search]] and
[[2025-01-24_ux-job-seeker-resources]], which make values the foundation of a job
search rather than of a career path.

Whichever path, [[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_05-3-why-become-a-senior-designer]]
argues senior designer is the level worth aiming at first: it is a required step
to reach staff or principal on the IC track, a near-requirement for most
management positions (with rare promotions straight from mid-level where soft
skills are highly developed), and not strictly required but a much stronger
position for freelancers, business owners and product managers, because it
develops breadth across communication, strategy, collaboration, leadership and
ownership. Its counterweight is the cost of time: spending one or two years
improving skills before a major change is a real cost, so if a specific
opportunity arises, take it even feeling unready, since learning by doing often
yields faster growth than more time at your current company. It adds that working
at a well-known company teaches organisational processes useful to future
freelancers and founders, and that a recognisable name opens doors with clients
and investors — and that if you cannot see past the next step, progressing to
senior is the safe default because it keeps every path open.

### The move from UX into product management

*Product Management for UX People* treats the product-management path not as one
of several ladders but as a fork with its own decision, and Crumlish narrates it
from his own career:
[[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_03-introduction]] goes from the
1990s freelance, agency and consulting web, where the client websites he built
"were not products," to staff interaction designer at Yahoo, where UED reported
into the product org, and singles out the day Larry Cornett moved from VP of User
Experience Design for Yahoo Search to VP of Product for the same team: "You can
do that!?" The questions the book opens on are the career ones, left unanswered
at that point: whether you have to switch teams to get ahead or to influence
product strategy, or whether product and UX can join forces as "superfriends."

The decision itself is the subject of
[[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_05-chapter-2-do-you-want-to-be-a-product-manager]],
which sorts the reasons for moving into three families and calls all of them
legitimate: professional and career (career progression planning, professional
interests), ambition (the realities of the current org structure, "lust for
power"), and evolving interests (waning interest in UX practitioner work,
fascination with the business side). You do not need a good reason to explore a
growth opportunity, it says, but you do have to weigh the trade-offs and
opportunity costs against staying in UX. Career pressure is treated as external
rather than personal: at Yahoo both Larry Cornett and senior director of UED Luke
Wroblewski moved onto the product track, which Crumlish labels the "if you can't
beat 'em, join 'em" approach, the alternative being to fight for better
opportunities on the UX track. Against romanticising the fork he sets a
grass-is-greener test, warning of a fallacy that idealises the road not taken
"and minimizes the mowing, weeding, and manure spreading that produced your
neighbor's green lawn." Ambition alone carries two named unintended
consequences: perpetuating an inferior approach to product management that
dominates UX without using its value wisely, and losing what makes the work
meaningful. Neither necessarily happens, he writes, but the risk is a lot higher
when ambition is the sole reason. One effect is not a risk but a guarantee, the
burden of decision-making. Matt LeMay's foreword,
[[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_02-foreword]], names the same
temptation from the other end: the "sparkle of a redemption narrative in the
making," the belief that becoming a PM will finally elevate UX and get the team
treated with respect, which the deadlines, estimates, high-stakes decisions and
high-pressure stakeholders of the role reliably complicate until "you start to
realize why some of the product managers you've worked with were 'like that.'"

The chapter's screening questions before switching are three: do you love
spreadsheets, are you a good writer, are you deeply curious about interpersonal
dynamics. Crumlish then states that you really do have to like numbers, math,
analysis, spreadsheets and databases to be a great product manager, and his own
sidebar contradicts him without resolving it. Clement Kao (Principal PM at Blend,
cofounder of Product Manager HQ) says truly amazing B2B PMs can get away without
numbers because they are so deeply locked in with each customer's priorities,
while conceding there is no escaping numbers in B2C; Matt LeMay of Sudden Compass
calls the claim "sometimes but not always true" and says he dislikes all of those
things while having the three qualities described. What the job is made of is
stated flatly: "Product management is not a design job," rarely involving pushing
pixels, and consisting instead of specification documents, decomposing epics into
user stories, backlog grooming, sprint planning, answering developer questions on
tickets, reviewing demos and test data, retrospectives, email, Slack, calendars
and quarterly or monthly roadmap reporting to leadership. What is given up is
named too: time in Figma, Sketch or Swift, and the creative flow of long
uninterrupted design days. John Cutler, head of product research and education at
Amplitude, supplies the reality check in a reprinted "From the Trenches" essay,
"44 Signs You Are Becoming a Product Manager," whose signs include being
pressured for estimates you know are worthless, building a roadmap everyone buys
into before everything changes in an instant, shipping a dud feature nobody uses
and an awesome one nobody notices, being "the single wringable neck," and being
empowered on paper while taking orders.

On whether the skills carry,
[[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_01-frequently-asked-questions]]
answers that UX people absolutely can become effective PMs, with no guarantee:
the best product managers Crumlish has worked with had a sophisticated
understanding of UX research, strategy and design principles, an obsession with
customer needs and a deep respect for UX practitioners. What does not carry is
the design work itself ("product managers are not designers," a shift plausible
only in a small start-up needing two jobs from one person) and the authority
model, since a PM focuses and organises engineers' efforts rather than commanding
them. Both the FAQ and the decision chapter also make the case for the reader who
stays: understanding product management is worth the effort even for someone who
has just firmly decided to remain in UX.

### Planning growth deliberately

[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_11-9-growing-your-impact]] supplies
the measure the plan aims at: citing Dropbox, it holds that the main predictor of
a designer's seniority is impact on the organisation, decomposed into consistency
(how often you have an impact), velocity (how large it is) and accountability
(owning the outcome). Its encouraging corollary, illustrated with Intercom, is that at mature
companies impact is looked at individually rather than in relation to the team,
which leaves the designer real control. The method it teaches is leverage rather than hours: identify what an
activity impacts (product, work, or people), identify the level of that impact,
then upgrade it to the next level — from a task toward a product line, from
learning toward managing, from yourself toward the industry. It frames
professional investment in the same terms, treating a career as your largest
financial asset: books, courses and consultants are cheap against the lifetime
income increase from one promotion, and it works the example of a $200
consultation with an interview preparation coach that improves your chances of
getting the job by 10%. It borrows kaizen for the
pacing — 1% improvement a week compounding to 170% over two years — and applies
the same selectivity to learning: read by topic rather than cover to cover,
prefer multi-idea books, use podcasts for niche knowledge and for researching
companies before joining them, and apply what you learn immediately.

Before planning, assess.
[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_09-7-assess-and-prioritize-competencies]]
holds that self-assessment alone is insufficient because of the Dunning–Kruger
effect, where low competence produces overestimation and the gap narrows as
competence grows; the corrective is 360-degree feedback from manager and peers,
or, when the employer offers none, colleagues and ex-managers who know your work.
It reports that companies typically require most competencies at a level rather
than every one, and that promotion follows long-term demonstration — you must
already have performed most competencies at the target level for a while.
Prioritising among them weighs three factors: what the industry requires, your
own career goal (weighted higher the closer you are to leaving), and the opinions
of people who know your work. A side benefit it names is interview-facing: the
exercise gives you a real answer about your weaknesses and a plan for them.

[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_10-8-design-career-growth-canvas]]
turns that priority list into a working document: your top five competencies,
each with current level, target level, action items, the next step you will take
in the next few days, a timeline, and a metric. It concedes that many competency
improvements are hard to measure objectively, so it treats regular one-on-ones
with your manager as the practical feedback mechanism. Sharing the canvas is part
of the method rather than an afterthought — asking "here's what I'd like to
improve and here's my plan, what do you think?" educates the manager about
structured growth planning and puts the responsibility to support it where it
belongs — and the plan should be revisited twice a year or whenever the context
changes (new career goal, new manager feedback, a change in employment status or
personal circumstances). The chapter argues these plans let you take control of
your own growth even when the company does not come on board, since the skills
travel to any future company or career, and that taking the initiative can set a
precedent for the organisation to formalise growth plans of its own.

Two competencies are singled out as accelerators available without a title.
[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_21-19-hiring-leadership]] presents
participating in hiring as one of the best ways to build organisational impact,
because it gives an individual contributor a taste of the management role, sets
them up for the management track if they want it, and makes them a better
interview candidate later; it acknowledges the substantial time commitment
involved. And
[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_19-17-strategy]] argues designers
accelerate by learning product management skills — what to build next, the
end-to-end customer experience — rather than design craft alone; the author's own
account is that getting his craft to a good enough level let him redirect his
learning toward the non-design skills that made him more valuable.

[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_25-final-thoughts]] frames the whole
exercise as non-linear and compounding rather than immediate, expects different
chapters to matter at different moments, and closes on the collective argument:
when designers become conscious of growth opportunities and demand them from
their organisations, companies improve their design maturity, which creates more
opportunities for every designer.

### Business fluency beyond the product

[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_01-introduction]] states the premise
directly: design's value inside a company lies in its connection to business
goals, KPIs and organisational strategy rather than in aesthetics, and it is that
understanding, not visual skill, that drives career progression. It adds an
ambition beyond the individual career — that designers who learn to help
companies solve business problems will build better products and contribute to
the more urgent problems facing the world.

[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_25-bobby-ghoshal-how-designers-should-change-their-mindset]]
pushes the same argument past the product boundary. Its claim is that 90 to 95%
of prospective customers never experience the product at all, blocked by pricing,
reviews, branding, marketing, operations and customer service — all of which it
calls design problems. The shift it prescribes is from "user experience designer"
to "customer experience designer", framing the whole customer journey rather than
the in-product experience alone. Practically, it asks designers to spend the bulk
of their time away from their desk building diplomatic relationships with
marketing, finance, operations and support, and to learn business strategy,
margins and conversion metrics, on the argument that designers with business
sensibilities are the ones positioned to own the field's future.

[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_19-17-strategy]] turns the same
argument into a competency with a reading list of internal artefacts. It holds
that your impact inside an organisation is correlated with how likely you are to
increase revenue or reduce costs, since that is the CEO's primary question about
any design, and that you have to understand how your company makes money and how
it spends money to acquire customers. Practically: ask your manager for the
documentation of the company's KPIs (revenue per customer, conversion rate, host
churn) and OKRs and contribute to them; read the vision statement, the product
roadmap and the organisational chart, which tell you which design conflicts to
avoid, which feature ideas are already planned and who to partner with to
influence a decision; map the differing incentives of PMs, engineers, marketing,
sales and support, since incentives explain behaviour and reveal who holds
authority over what; and track direct competitors and industry leaders using G2,
Product Hunt or Google auto-complete, learning from negative reviews what to fix
and from positive ones what users value. It notes the first business you analyse
is effortful and the next ones are faster, because the exercise leaves a reusable
mental framework. Its illustration of the payoff is comparative: the designer who
suggests how to simplify a task to reduce development effort while maintaining
business impact provides more value than the one who executes the original brief.

### Moving into management or founding

[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_29-mia-blume-skills-which-future-design-managers-should-work-on]]
treats the individual-contributor to manager move as one hard substitution:
letting go of control and becoming a coach rather than a solution provider. Its
sequence starts with listening — when a teammate arrives seeking an answer, the
first job is to understand their problem before offering direction — then
redirects the problem back with empowering questions ("What's the goal of this
exercise?", "If you had two more weeks, how would you prioritize the work?"), and
avoids making decisions for the team unless the project is high-risk or the
deadline tight, saying explicitly that you trust the person and offering support
and perspective instead of direction. Its encouraging half is that the
foundations are already in place: creativity, vision, storytelling, empathy and
systems thinking, honed through years of design practice, transfer directly to
business leadership, so management development only has to add coaching and
delegation.

For the founder route,
[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_26-justin-maxwell-advice-for-designers-who-want-to-become]]
sorts the skills into three categories and insists they be practised while still
employed. Discipline covers prioritisation, organisation and focus, plus the
ability to align an organisation to constraints and objectives without being told
to; it points at B. J. Fogg's Tiny Habits as the method for building it. People
skills are led by negotiation, which it calls the most frequently used skill in
founder life, alongside active listening, critical inquiry, hiring and
interviewing, sales and pitching — and it treats unreliable communication as an
immediate liability. Big-picture thinking means satisfying user need before
cosmetic appeal, choosing words carefully, spending money wisely, triaging
product decisions deliberately and pursuing only opportunities that move toward
stated goals. It also reframes rejection in sales and hiring as resilience
training that helps drop the ego in later interactions, and closes on getting
comfortable with chaos, since perfect adherence to principles is unrealistic.

### Communication as a career skill

One source treats articulation as the skill that decides whether design work
survives contact with the organisation.
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_02-chapter-1-great-designers-are-great-communicators]]
argues the difference between a good and a great designer is not
problem-solving ability but the capacity to explain a solution convincingly
enough to win support, and that communication skills are more likely to advance
a career than artistic talent. The reasoning is that design is subjective —
every design has legitimate alternatives, so work never "speaks for itself" —
and that stakeholders without design expertise influence projects regardless,
producing design-by-committee outcomes such as the "CEO button" when nobody
articulates the counter-argument. The chapter's working test is three questions
applied to every design, all three necessary: does it solve a problem, is it
easy for users, and is it supported by everyone on the team.

[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_01-preface]] frames the same skill
as learnable and habit-forming rather than innate, and reports the author's own
turning point: stakeholders care about what a design does, not how it looks, so
the first question is what the work is trying to communicate. Both chapters
insist the requirement does not expire with seniority — junior and senior
designers alike have to keep practising articulation.
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_02-chapter-1-great-designers-are-great-communicators]]
also asks designers to document their thought process deliberately rather than
design on intuition, precisely so the reasoning can be explained to
non-designers later.

[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_27-helen-tran-the-skill-most-designers-overlook]]
names research and communication together as the two skills designers most
overlook, and treats both as learnable rather than innate — the same position as
the chapters above. Its particular contribution is a list of venues for
practising: group critiques, speaking opportunities inside the company,
retrospectives, Toastmaster groups, improv groups, and deliberately going beyond
the tech scene. Its rule of thumb for choosing among them is to challenge your
bubble, on the argument that discomfort is a good sign of growth. The purpose it
assigns the skill is convincing teams to build what makes sense for both the
business and the user, which requires being able to explain the bridge between
the two.

### Specialisation versus generalism

The 2019 survey already distinguished three profiles: designers (broad
responsibilities, design-related education, often front-end coding skills, most
desired skill data analysis), researchers (focused on research rather than
design or IA, strong writing and public speaking, less likely to code, most
desired skill visual design), and generalists [[2020-04-19_ux-career-advice]].

Several later sources argue the balance is swinging back toward generalism.
[[2025-03-28_return-ux-generalist]] holds that a decade of specialisation is
being reversed by AI: individuals can now accomplish work that previously
required several specialists, low-UX-maturity companies (the majority of the
market) cannot afford specialists and will favour AI-augmented generalists, and
the generalist's value moves toward facilitation, organisational navigation,
business acumen and connecting insights to strategy. It prescribes four
preparations: cultivate a learning mindset, become fluent in collaborating with
AI, focus on transferable "forever skills" (critical thinking, facilitation,
strategic decision-making, intuition), and expand into adjacent fields such as
systems thinking, business strategy and front-end development.
[[2025-12-19_top-videos-2025]] and [[2026-01-16_state-of-ux-2026]] corroborate
that broad skills and adaptability are being valued over deep specialisation.
[[2025-01-24_advice-ux-job-search]] reaches the same practical conclusion from a
different direction: in a tough market, specialisation limits the roles you can
compete for, so diversify.

One caveat sits alongside this: [[2025-01-17_tactics-ux-job-market]] advises
students and recent graduates to prioritise building real strength in one area
over dabbling — a different emphasis from the generalist argument, applied to a
different career stage.

### Working with AI

The sources converge on AI as a tool change rather than a replacement.
[[2025-05-09_ux-job-with-ai]] places it in a 20-year succession of tool shifts
(stopwatches to remote testing platforms to AI transcription), each of which
forced jobs and techniques to adapt, and argues that new technologies prompt new
methods — as mobile prompted customer-journey mapping, generative UIs will
require new methods for evaluating AI-based interactions. What stays human is
problem-framing and judgement: defining problems clearly enough for AI to
address, asking the right questions, judging whether outputs are good, and
deciding which issues matter most. [[2025-10-21_ai-work-study-guide]] frames the
practitioner as a strategist using AI as "gloves", notes that handing whole
workflows to AI has not proved productive, and recommends testing AI selectively
against identified bottlenecks rather than forcing it everywhere or refusing it
outright; it also holds that AI cannot replace research with real people.
[[2025-01-10_ux-reset-2025]] adds that as automation raises the baseline, the
differentiators become critical thinking, creativity, taste, soft skills and the
ability to curate outputs — and reports the post-hype position that 47% of UX
professionals found AI had "some value".
[[2026-01-16_state-of-ux-2026]] extends this to the craft itself: design systems
and AI-assisted tooling have made competent UI easy to produce, so competitive
advantage moves to research-informed understanding, judgement and taste.

### Transitioning into UX

Transition is possible and common; relevant experience from other fields is an
asset, and some practitioners simply started doing UX inside their existing
organisation [[2020-04-19_ux-career-advice]]. That route is explicitly
recommended: attempt a UX pivot at your current employer before searching
externally, since you already have the relationships and resources, and explore
design-adjacent opportunities if no formal UX team exists
[[2025-01-17_tactics-ux-job-market]].

The blocker is the catch-22 of needing UX experience to get UX work. At least
one genuine UX entry on a resume materially improves prospects, obtainable
through freelance consulting, nonprofit work, network-based projects or
apprenticeships [[2022-08-07_resumes-ux-career-changers]]. Real-world work is
what counts: hypothetical redesigns are "nearly worthless", because the
portfolio must show you confronting technology constraints, collaborating with
sceptics and learning from real users [[2025-01-17_tactics-ux-job-market]].
Previous roles should be reframed through a UX lens — interviewing, data
analysis, content strategy, presentations, project management and visual design
all transfer [[2022-08-07_resumes-ux-career-changers]].

### Resumes and application materials

Hiring managers scan resumes in 6–7 seconds in an F-shaped pattern, so content
must be frontloaded, scannable, reverse-chronological and one page
[[2022-08-07_resumes-ux-career-changers]]. Quantified achievements structured as
[verb] [quantitative outcome] [context] [skill/tool] outperform skill lists and
self-rated proficiency charts, which are arbitrary and lack context — a formula
repeated for students and graduates [[2022-08-07_resumes-ux-career-changers]]
[[2023-02-19_resumes-ux-students-and-graduates]]. Errors are costly for
graduates in particular: two errors on a graduate resume reduce interview
chances by 7.3%, and women are penalised more than men for the same mistakes
[[2023-02-19_resumes-ux-students-and-graduates]]. Contact and education details
must be complete (full university location, country, program type, graduation
date, portfolio link) so hiring managers do not have to search
[[2023-02-19_resumes-ux-students-and-graduates]]. Describing relevant coursework
in two to three sentences is rated more favourably than omitting it, and
including non-UX work experience helps — applicants with more work experiences,
even unrelated ones, were more likely to succeed
[[2023-02-19_resumes-ux-students-and-graduates]].

Two points where the sources differ:

- **What to cut.** [[2022-08-07_resumes-ux-career-changers]] says never include
  skill lists, proficiency charts, headshots, mission statements, hobbies,
  volunteering or personal projects, as they waste space and show no real-world
  context. [[2023-02-19_resumes-ux-students-and-graduates]] instead advises
  graduates to include extracurriculars and all work experience, including
  non-UX jobs. The two are addressed to different populations (career changers
  versus students), but the advice on what earns space points in opposite
  directions.
- **How much ATS matters.** [[2022-08-07_resumes-ux-career-changers]] treats
  applicant-tracking systems as a real filter to design around: Word rather than
  complex layouts, standard fonts, no tables or columns, consistent Word and PDF
  versions; [[2023-02-19_resumes-ux-students-and-graduates]] likewise recommends
  supplying both Word (for ATS) and PDF (for humans).
  [[2026-01-16_ux-hiring-insights]] disagrees, reporting from a recruiter that
  ATS tracks data but does not decide interviews — humans review resumes, often
  in real time as they arrive, and quick rejections come from knockout
  questions, real-time review or a filled role. It calls chasing
  "ATS-friendliness" a red flag and reframes the goal as plain legibility
  (standard tools like Google Docs rather than Figma).

Beyond the resume, [[2025-01-31_apply-secure-ux-job]] recommends building a
**work chronicle**: one raw document capturing all education, work history,
skills, achievements and certifications, which can then be paired with a job
posting and fed to generative AI to draft a targeted resume.
[[2025-01-24_ux-job-seeker-resources]] warns against letting AI write the
message itself — use it only as a proofreader or light editor of something you
authentically drafted, since machine-generated outreach has become ubiquitous
and is perceived less favourably.

[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_28-joel-califa-two-tips-for-getting-a-job]]
supplies the reviewer's-eye reason for the care: writing as a former hiring
manager, Califa reports looking at dozens, sometimes hundreds of CVs and
portfolios a week and developing habits, an eye for patterns and a set of pet
peeves — so design, typos and clarity across CV, portfolio and cover letter all
register.

### Portfolio

The portfolio is treated as career infrastructure, not a job-search artefact.
Build it around three to five detailed case studies chosen for varied skills and
alignment with the jobs you want, and show the process — problem or hypothesis,
your specific role and collaborators, research, ideation, iterations,
challenges, business and user impact, learnings — because final screenshots tell
only part of the story [[2019-08-04_ux-design-portfolios]]. Research portfolios
follow the same curation logic (three to five recent, high-impact, methodologically
robust projects) but must communicate methodology, findings and business impact
rather than visual output, span multiple methods and project scales, use plain
language for readers outside your industry, and back claims with metrics and
user quotes [[2019-03-10_ux-researcher-portfolio]]. NDAs, thin experience and
student status are handled by showing process, redacting or genericising
designs, and including internship work with its real constraints
[[2019-08-04_ux-design-portfolios]] [[2019-03-10_ux-researcher-portfolio]].

Maintenance beats reconstruction. [[2023-01-15_maintain-ux-portfolio]] proposes
an annual self-assessment across five dimensions — skills/tools/abilities,
accomplishments, professional activities, career management, disappointments —
producing a roughly 30-slide deck with images and three-to-four-sentence speaker
notes as future source material, rather than an overwhelming portfolio overhaul;
it stresses capturing sketches, whiteboards, research notes, prototypes,
presentations, feedback and metrics throughout the year (including the
before-state, to make impact measurable) and honouring NDA, contract and privacy
obligations. [[2025-03-21_ux-layoff-guide]] gives the defensive reason to do
this: document projects while you still have system access, because layoffs
remove it without warning.

Who the portfolio is for matters as much as what is in it.
[[2026-01-16_ux-hiring-insights]] argues portfolios succeed by addressing the
hiring manager's needs rather than impressing other designers — most first
reviewers are recruiters, PMs or founders, not designers — and that legibility
beats visual complexity. It also notes that "craft" is highly subjective,
meaning invisible detail to one reviewer and visual impressiveness to another,
so part of the job is figuring out which audience you are in front of and when
to set aside closely held design beliefs.
[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_02-why-i-wrote-this-book]] made a
compatible point much earlier and about content rather than audience: junior
designers over-invest in visual skills instead of building portfolios that show
problem-solving, a misdirection it traces to design communities that celebrate
visual work. [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_30-additional-resources]]
notes that guidance on building a UX portfolio without prior work experience is
itself a documented genre.

### Personal brand and advocacy

[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_23-21-advocacy-community]] treats
advocacy — promoting the company and the design team's message externally — as
less critical than the other competencies it covers, while arguing that the
personal brand it builds can have outsized impact on a career, because
company-specific visibility is lost when you switch teams or companies and a
personal brand follows you. Its channels are public speaking, writing, dedicated
design team websites, public mentorship programmes and design awards. On
speaking, it describes a progression from internal events to small meetups to
conferences, and distinguishes being scouted — which it calls rare and dependent
on an established public presence — from submitting to a call for papers, which
it presents as the realistic route; it relays the VP of design at Figma saying
that publishing articles about their internal design work process brings design
candidates to their door. On writing, its idea-generation techniques are to
leverage time you have already spent on a topic, reveal insider information or
unique access, name an emerging industry pattern, examine a convention that has
fallen out of favour, go deep on a small neglected topic, define your own way of
doing something, and make internal documentation public — quoting Linus Pauling,
"The way to get good ideas is to get lots of ideas", while warning that no
technique guarantees an article will land. The mechanism it claims is cumulative:
each article, talk or open-source contribution increases your "luck surface
area", the chance that a job offer, consulting gig or book deal reaches you, and
it reports being approached by InVision leadership as its own example. Along the
way it flags a market blind spot: the author reports analysing 120 companies with
open product designer positions in the San Francisco Bay Area and finding the
majority to be B2B, and argues designers who target only B2C are ignoring most of
the relevant companies and should show more B2B work in their portfolio.

### Job search strategy

Start with yourself. [[2025-01-24_advice-ux-job-search]] makes values the
foundation: value misalignment erodes trust and open communication over time, so
identify what matters before pursuing roles, then define constraints (location,
compensation, employment type, industry) and rate your willingness to trade on
each. [[2025-01-24_ux-job-seeker-resources]] points to research-backed
instruments for this — Life Values Inventory, Career Anchors, Values Sort — and
warns that choosing on title, employer or compensation produces dissatisfaction
within months.

Networks do disproportionate work: many UX hiring managers rely, perhaps
overrely, on referrals, so maintain a LinkedIn presence, notify past
connections, and secure references proactively [[2025-01-24_advice-ux-job-search]]
— at least one reference from a recent employer or mentor, and two secured
before an internship ends for students [[2025-01-24_ux-job-seeker-resources]].
Mentorship is available through ADPList, the HFES Mentorship Program and the
UXPA Mentorship Program, though quality mentorship is a months- or years-long
commitment rather than a few sessions [[2025-01-24_advice-ux-job-search]]
[[2025-01-24_ux-job-seeker-resources]]. Students and graduates should cultivate
professor relationships, attend university career events, connect with alumni
and ask professors to keep them in mind [[2025-01-17_tactics-ux-job-market]].

On applications, prefer quality over volume: customise for roles that match your
values and constraints rather than sending hundreds of generic applications,
apply within two to three weeks of posting, and apply on the employer's own
career site rather than a job board [[2025-01-31_apply-secure-ux-job]].
[[2026-01-16_ux-hiring-insights]] adds that tailoring every single application
is unsustainable — assess your market position, identify your primary audience,
and concentrate effort where returns are highest.

Take-home assessments have returned with the shift of power to employers, and
candidates now spend 10–30x more time on hiring than hiring managers do
[[2026-01-16_ux-hiring-insights]]. Red flags include unclear instructions,
open-ended tasks, real-time problem-solving under observation, or anything
exceeding a few hours; withdrawing politely protects your time and signals
standards [[2025-01-31_apply-secure-ux-job]]
[[2025-01-24_ux-job-seeker-resources]]. With an offer in hand, request one or
two reverse interviews with the hiring manager or future collaborators to probe
UX maturity, management style and project details, and take one to three days to
decide — never accept immediately [[2025-01-31_apply-secure-ux-job]]. Negotiate
pragmatically: entry-level candidates have little leverage, so prioritise title
and growth opportunity, research salary ranges, use precise numbers, clarify
remote/hybrid arrangements and read NDAs carefully
[[2025-01-31_apply-secure-ux-job]]. Thank-you notes are worth writing, and
requesting feedback after a rejection is worth attempting even though it is
rarely granted [[2025-01-24_ux-job-seeker-resources]].

[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_28-joel-califa-two-tips-for-getting-a-job]]
proposes running the search itself as a design process: you are the product and
the hiring manager is the user, so research their background, the team's values,
its previous hires and the language of the posting, and deliver a tailored
experience rather than a generic pitch. It states plainly that "spray and pray"
does not work for competitive roles, which agrees with
[[2025-01-31_apply-secure-ux-job]] on quality over volume and sits against
[[2026-01-16_ux-hiring-insights]], which holds that tailoring every single
application is unsustainable and that effort should be concentrated where returns
are highest. On networking it takes the long view: connect genuinely with
interesting people in design, help others without expecting a return, and
maintain a reputation — a few years of that, it argues, and getting in the door
and getting the job both become easier because people already know you. It
presents this as significant work over years, with a network of genuine
friendships and colleagues as part of the payoff.

On the interview itself,
[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_17-chapter-3-questions-and-answers]] offers
reassurance about design exercises: there is no single right solution, candidates
will arrive at different answers, and what is assessed is structured thinking and
clear reasoning rather than production polish.
[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_30-additional-resources]] adds that the
behavioural questions designers report being intimidated by ("What is your
greatest strength?", "Describe a stressful situation at work and how you handled
it") are not unique to design roles, so general interview material covers them;
the same chapter points to interview collections of designer career stories as
evidence that career paths are diverse and non-linear.

### Resilience: staying employed, layoffs, and pacing

While employed, deliver results your manager values, clarify priorities, and aim
for a notable outcome — measurable improvement on a benchmark such as customer
satisfaction or time on task, or qualitative recognition through a promotion or
award [[2025-01-17_tactics-ux-job-market]]. The same source advises giving even
a disliked job one to two years, since frequent job changes are read as
signalling poor collaboration skills (a perception it expects may shift as
younger generations move into leadership), and building six to nine months of
living expenses as financial resilience.

On layoffs, [[2025-03-21_ux-layoff-guide]] reframes the event as an
organisational failure rather than a verdict on performance, citing research
that layoffs are used for quick financial or strategic gains and frequently fail
to deliver sustained improvement. Its recovery practice: spend the first day
only on notifying loved ones and self-care; treat the experience as grief-like,
with denial, anger, bargaining and depression stages; keep professional composure
and accept help from former managers, since those relationships become
references, recommendations and introductions; gather portfolio material while
access remains; then run about four hours a day across job seeking, networking,
financial management and professional development, balanced with exercise and
social activity — the combination of daily structure and well-being effort is
associated with better mental health and faster reemployment. The same four-hour
ceiling appears in [[2025-01-24_advice-ux-job-search]], with 20–30 minute breaks
between cognitively demanding tasks (job seekers who took them were more likely
to secure interviews) and modest weekly goals for those searching while employed.

At the field level, [[2025-01-10_ux-reset-2025]] counsels perspective and
agency: UX has survived downturns before (the dot-com bust nearly took NN/g
down), renaming a team "insights research" or "experience design" treats the
symptom rather than the problem of demonstrating business value, and the
practical response is to stay flexible about how UX work is done, empathise with
other disciplines, align with business goals, and be honest about what is
outside your control. [[2025-01-17_tactics-ux-job-market]] echoes that a tough
market will not last forever.

### Why people stay

Across 126 practitioners, the dominant source of fulfilment was impact and
meaningful work — 84 of them cited designing solutions that improve people's
lives [[2024-07-05_best-things-about-working-in-ux]]. The other themes were the
user-centric philosophy and advocating for users with stakeholders, the mix of
analytical research with creative design, continuous learning and variety across
industries, collaboration and a supportive community willing to share knowledge,
autonomy to define your own role and specialisation in a still-new field,
visible business impact, and purpose found in ethics and accessibility. The
long-run framing in [[2017-12-24_100-years-ux]] adds a structural reason for
optimism: overall user experience quality is estimated at under 10% of what it
should be, and the field's future lies in augmenting human cognition and raising
productivity far beyond consumer software.
