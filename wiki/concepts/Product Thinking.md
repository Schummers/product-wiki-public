## Sources (14)

- [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_01-introduction]] — Identifies product thinking as the key skill gap between what design schools teach and what companies need from designers.
- [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_02-why-i-wrote-this-book]] — Defined as understanding business outcomes and designing experiences to achieve them; identified as the critical missing skill in most designers' education.
- [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_05-what-is-a-design-exercise]] — the chapter identifies product thinking as one of the main skill categories tested by design exercises, noting that conceptual tasks like "design an ATM for kids" are used when companies want to test this ability
- [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_09-step-1-understand-your-goal-why]] — this chapter teaches the product-thinking approach of leading with vision, mission, and business opportunity when presenting design solutions
- [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_13-step-5-prioritise-and-choose-an-idea]] — Balancing multiple stakeholder concerns (customer satisfaction, business revenue, engineering feasibility) when selecting which solution to pursue.
- [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_15-step-7-measure-success-how]] — Understanding that a successful design must be paired with measurable business outcomes and showing awareness of the need to collaborate with product managers on data-driven validation.
- [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_17-chapter-3-questions-and-answers]] — The emphasis on solution flow and reasoning over aesthetics; candidates are evaluated on their ability to apply structured frameworks and justify their thinking, not on their ability to execute high-fidelity designs.
- [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_20-3-3-a-dashboard-for-freelancers]] — Dashinsky recognizes that different freelancer segments need different approaches: new freelancers need guidance on setup, experienced ones need import and efficiency, and retainer contractors have fundamentally different needs that might require a separate product variant.
- [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_22-3-5-improving-the-atm-experience]] — Connecting design decisions to business goals (increased revenue, loyalty) and customer outcomes (reduced queues, increased accessibility, lower fraud risk).
- [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_23-chapter-4-how-to-use-a-design-exercise-when-interviewing]] — On-site exercises test whether candidates follow a process, make valid assumptions, define goals and metrics, and connect their solutions to company mission and user context rather than jumping to solutions.
- [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_24-chapter-5-tasks-list]] — Exercise prompts require connecting user problems to business metrics (retention improvement, new revenue drivers, underserved segments) and considering systemic impacts beyond single features.
- [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_25-bobby-ghoshal-how-designers-should-change-their-mindset]] — Designers should frame the entire customer journey, not just the in-product experience, to address the 90-95% of prospective customers who never experience the product.
- [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_26-justin-maxwell-advice-for-designers-who-want-to-become]] — Big-picture thinking requires focusing product decisions on satisfying user need first, triage based on stated goals, and avoiding the distraction of shiny features or trends.
- [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_27-helen-tran-the-skill-most-designers-overlook]] — Designers must independently validate assumptions about user and business needs rather than accepting directives at face value; thorough research on both sides is foundational to sound product decisions.
