# Product-Market Fit

## Definition

Product-market fit is the state in which a product answers a real market need well enough that the venture can be sold viably, and potentially profitably. The Parlons Design corpus frames it from the product side: the moment a solution solves a genuine problem well enough that a customer is ready to pay for it ([[2024-11-26_353_3_outils_pour_accélérer_son_Product_Market_Fit]]). There is no formula for reaching it; the only route is to confront the product with the reality of the market as early as possible, then to read what real users do with it. The same corpus treats fit as something that can be lost as well as gained: a product that drifts into hyper-technicality and becomes incomprehensible to its end users puts its fit at risk ([[2026-05-26_415_Le_produit_dans_l_industrie_IA_Adaptabilité_&_lien_humain_-_Celine_Saint-Jean]]).

The two venture-studio books use the term as a **gate** rather than as a product quality. At eFounders, fit must be confirmed during the Build phase (months 1 to 9) before a venture is allowed into the Scale phase (months 9 to 18) ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_07-game-plan]]); at Midealab, fit is the objective of the build phase and a precondition of the growth phase ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_09-building-startups-from-megatrends]]). The studio view also adds a dimension the podcast does not discuss: timing. Midealab argues that product and market timing are inseparable, so a great product launched in the wrong phase of a megatrend (too early, or into saturation) fails regardless of its quality. So when a design team says "PMF" it usually means "does the product answer a need people pay for"; when a studio says it, it means "has this venture passed the checkpoint that unlocks scaling capital and processes". The two readings are compatible, but they are not the same object.

Christian Crumlish supplies the marker most often used to say fit has been reached, and undercuts it in the same breath: there is broad consensus among investors and product people that around 40% of users saying they would be "very disappointed" to lose the product marks fit, but he insists the concept is subjective and somewhat arbitrary rather than an objective yes/no fact, comparing it to the stone in the "stone soup" parable, an anchoring concept that enables good things to happen ([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_08-chapter-5-the-business-of-product-is-business]]).

*The 10x Method* holds a third position: fit is a distant destination with readable early signals. It states that B2B products typically take more than two years to reach true product-market fit, so what a young team can observe is movement towards it rather than arrival at it, chiefly through the engagement of design partners and through a single focus metric ([[2026-03-05_10x-method_04-building-your-mvp]]).

## Practice

### Confront the market early, and read real behaviour

- The recurring instruction across both corpora is to get the product into real hands quickly rather than reasoning about fit in the abstract. In the podcast, the strongest signal is a customer actually paying: until the user has the product in hand and has actually completed a purchase, nothing is certain ([[2024-11-26_353_3_outils_pour_accélérer_son_Product_Market_Fit]]). In the studio, eFounders makes this operational by confronting the MVP with 25 pilot users every day during Build, drawing on its network for pilots and feedback ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_07-game-plan]]).
- [[2026-03-05_10x-method_04-building-your-mvp]] presses the same point harder and adds a distinction between artefacts: users do not react to a prototype the way they react to a real, functional product, because a prototype invites opinions while a working product triggers real behaviour. Hence its instruction to ship the MVP within weeks rather than months, an MVP being something that delivers 1% of the long-term vision while capturing 80% of its core value. Real usage teaches far more than months of internal discussion or polished prototypes, and shipping speed also signals execution ability to the team and to investors. See [[Minimum Viable Product]].
- The biggest risk named by the podcast at this stage is dispersion: trying to answer everything too generally, or optimising flows in fine detail, while the only question that matters is still whether the product answers a need at all ([[2024-11-26_353_3_outils_pour_accélérer_son_Product_Market_Fit]]).

### Three tool families to accelerate fit (podcast)

[[2024-11-26_353_3_outils_pour_accélérer_son_Product_Market_Fit]] proposes three accessible categories of tools, in order:

1. **Validate that the problem is searched for, before building.** Google Keyword Planner (widened with generative AI to enumerate keyword variants) shows the terms the target actually uses, the competition on them, and the likely acquisition cost. See [[Search Intent Analysis]].
2. **Collect qualitative feedback in context.** Tools such as Told.club or Crisp embed micro-surveys inside the product at strategic moments (for example, when a user abandons a payment step), so the team learns the real reasons behind a choice rather than a bare satisfaction score. See [[User Feedback]].
3. **Analyse real usage to find blockers.** Amplitude, Smartlook and similar solutions expose funnels, heatmaps and session recordings. The episode notes a minimum of traffic is needed before the data is representative, but that watching journeys step by step is especially valuable at launch. See [[Session Recording]].

### The 40% marker and the product/market fit engine (book)

[[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_08-chapter-5-the-business-of-product-is-business]] makes the search for fit the first job of early-stage product work, and argues that before fit is found, optimisation and heavily engineered solutions are wasted effort, because you do not yet know how badly people want what you are offering; the work at that stage is to experiment relentlessly and to listen to what customers and non-customers are saying. The process he relays for zeroing in on fit is Rahul Vohra's "product/market fit engine": survey users on how disappointed they would be to lose the product, ask those who love it what they love, segment the market and see which segments are overrepresented among the most dedicated users, then focus on converting more customers like them ([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_08-chapter-5-the-business-of-product-is-business]]).

The disappointment question is the same instrument [[2024-11-12_351_Les_cold_calls,_méthode_d_UX_Research_-_Guide_pratique]] puts inside a cold call; the book adds the threshold (around 40%) and the segmentation step that turns the answer into a target.

### Estimating fit through cold calls (podcast)

[[2024-11-12_351_Les_cold_calls,_méthode_d_UX_Research_-_Guide_pratique]] presents cold calls, short unannounced calls to customers, as one of the ideal methods for estimating product-market fit, alongside understanding purchase and churn reasons. The specific test is to ask the user whether they would be disappointed if the product disappeared. The method's constraints apply: calls last under three minutes, the customer does not have the product in front of them, response rates run at 20 to 30 percent so a list of several hundred customers is needed, the interview holds only three short questions about a recent experience, and GDPR and communication preferences must be respected (explicit consent within the first thirty seconds if the call is recorded). See [[Cold Calls]].

### A worked example: Figma Slides (podcast)

[[2024-12-24_357_Figma_Slides,_une_leçon_de_Product_Market_Fit_-_Podcast_Product_Design]] reads the launch of Figma Slides as a lesson in fit against entrenched competitors (PowerPoint, Keynote). The strategic choices it highlights:

- Reuse the familiar ergonomic standards of classic presentation tools so that non-designers (marketers, analysts) are not disoriented, and strip superfluous features.
- Differentiate on Figma's core assets, chiefly real-time collaboration (shared cursors, technical stability), without complicating the creation experience.
- Provide an expert "design mode" (Command D) so design teams keep Figma's full power and push adoption inside companies.
- Ship modern default themes against visually dated competitor templates.

The episode's takeaway is that Figma designed the tool for its whole ecosystem of potential users, not for designers alone, and that what fits the user's daily workflow should win even when it needs a slightly roundabout justification.

### Fit as a studio gate (books)

- **eFounders' Game Plan** ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_07-game-plan]]): ideas go through a two-month validation (market analysis, technical analysis, prototype testing) before entering Build; roughly 20 ideas a year become 4 projects. In Build (months 1 to 9) the MVP is confronted with pilot users daily and market fit must be confirmed before the Scale phase (months 9 to 18); ventures become operationally independent after 18 months.
- **Midealab** ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_09-building-startups-from-megatrends]]): the build phase aims at a "minimum loveable product", a product that works rather than merely a viable one, and at three things together: product-market fit, business model clarity (where and how to enter the market), and a dedicated core team. Seed funding is secured before growth so the team can focus on execution rather than fundraising. See [[Minimum Viable Product]].

### Timing as part of fit (books)

Midealab's chapter is the only source in this page to make timing a component of fit. It segments a megatrend's adoption into early innovation (uncertain demand, patient capital), growth ("party time", masses adopting, predictable demand) and saturation (favours large, well-capitalised competitors), and deliberately targets the growth phase. Opportunities are highest where two or more megatrends collide (mobile data plus social media giving mobile video, for instance). It also adds an implementability filter: whether the studio has the talent and resources to execute, and whether the venture is "operations-light" (scales with capital and engineering) rather than "operations-heavy" ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_09-building-startups-from-megatrends]]).

### Design partners and one focus metric as early signals (book)

[[2026-03-05_10x-method_04-building-your-mvp]] gives two instruments for reading progress towards fit before fit exists:

- **Design partners, not pilots.** Replace passive beta testers with design partners who actively co-build the product: embedded in development, in daily conversation, iterating on prototypes, with their real behaviour observed. The recruiting itself is the test. Struggling to find partners willing to invest their time signals that the product may not address a strong enough pain point; companies eager to participate and to shape the product indicate a real problem worth solving at scale, and that fit is achievable. See [[Design Partner]].
- **One focus metric.** Pick a single north star among customer retention rate, monthly recurring revenue growth, or active users. It justifies decisions and indicates progress towards fit. The chapter warns against measuring everything: at this stage success comes from conviction and qualitative user feedback more than from dashboards.

The chapter also uses the three-dimensional idea framework (problem, solution, go-to-market) as the pivot test: if the problem is not painful enough, the solution does not resonate, or go-to-market does not scale, a pivot may be justified, and that decision should rest on early metrics and observed user behaviour rather than on vanity metrics or assumptions. See [[Business Strategy]].

### Keeping fit once the product exists (podcast)

[[2026-05-26_415_Le_produit_dans_l_industrie_IA_Adaptabilité_&_lien_humain_-_Celine_Saint-Jean]] adds a caution from an emerging industry (generative engine optimisation): a product manager who becomes too technical acquires a bias, and users who are often novices in AI need pedagogy and a business-oriented approach rather than toxic complexity. Fit is presented here as something that erodes when the product outruns its users' understanding. See [[Product Discovery]].

### Where the sources diverge

- **Whether fit is a measurable state at all.** The studio books treat fit as a gate that is passed or not; [[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_08-chapter-5-the-business-of-product-is-business]] gives the most-quoted number for it (40% "very disappointed") and simultaneously refuses to treat it as a hard test, calling the concept subjective and arbitrary. The number and the caveat come from the same source and the page keeps both.

- **What counts as evidence.** The podcast anchors fit on an individual paying customer and on qualitative signals (a disappointed-if-it-disappeared answer, in-context micro-surveys). The studio books anchor it on a phase gate satisfied by daily pilot confrontation and validation analyses; they do not describe the individual signals a designer would collect. *The 10x Method* anchors it on the willingness of design partners to invest their own time, plus one chosen focus metric, and explicitly ranks conviction and qualitative feedback above dashboards at this stage ([[2026-03-05_10x-method_04-building-your-mvp]]).

- **How long fit takes.** eFounders requires fit to be confirmed inside a Build phase running from month 1 to month 9 ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_07-game-plan]]); *The 10x Method* states that B2B products typically take over two years to reach true fit and treats design-partner enthusiasm as an early sign of movement, not as arrival ([[2026-03-05_10x-method_04-building-your-mvp]]). One sets a deadline, the other sets an expectation, and the two numbers do not reconcile.

- **Pilot users or co-builders.** eFounders confronts the MVP with 25 pilot users every day ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_07-game-plan]]); *The 10x Method* argues that passive beta testers should be replaced by design partners who commit to co-building, because commitment is itself the validation ([[2026-03-05_10x-method_04-building-your-mvp]]). Same daily rhythm, different demand on the user.
- **Viable versus loveable.** eFounders talks of confronting an MVP; Midealab explicitly rejects "merely viable" in favour of a loveable product that works. The podcast's tool-oriented episode sits closer to eFounders (test the bare need first, do not over-optimise), while its Figma Slides reading sits closer to Midealab (polished defaults and an expert mode as fit levers).
- **Timing.** Only Midealab treats timing as a first-class determinant; the podcast episodes do not raise it.
