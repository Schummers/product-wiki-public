# Founder Recruitment

## Definition

Founder recruitment is the studio function of finding, selecting, compensating and onboarding the entrepreneurs who will lead the ventures the studio creates: the CEOs, co-founders and entrepreneurs-in-residence who take an internally generated idea and turn it into an independent company. *Venture Studios Demystified* lists assembling the founding team among the defining activities of a studio, alongside developing ideas internally, testing them, building MVPs and providing initial capital, and identifies it as one of three fundamental processes every studio shares with ideation and company building ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_02-part-1-venture-studio-basics]], [[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_23-implications-for-stakeholders]]). The *Startup Studio Playbook* calls it the fundamental challenge of studio building and, in the eFounders case, the highest-leverage hire a studio makes ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_17-entrepreneurs-in-residence-and-ceos]], [[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_07-game-plan]]).

Both books describe the same tension. The founders a studio most wants, experienced entrepreneurs with a track record and a network, are the hardest to convince, because the studio asks them to give up founding equity and autonomy in exchange for de-risked venture building, and because a young studio has no results to show. The recruiting pitch, the selection criteria, the sourcing model and the equity split are all shaped by that tension. Where the two books differ most is on the equity number, and this page keeps both. Related pages: [[Entrepreneur in Residence]], [[Equity Ownership]], [[Team Scaling]], [[Studio Leadership]].

## Practice

### What a studio offers a founder

The pitch appears in both books in nearly the same terms. The Playbook argues that a studio lowers the risk and impact of failure: a founder proves viability before full commitment, a failed venture does not wipe out the team's knowledge, and people can be reassigned. First-time founders gain the studio's knowledge base and execution capability; seasoned entrepreneurs gain a ready-made team and an investor network for follow-on funding ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_04-are-startup-studios-for-me]]). Drukka's co-founder CEOs valued the model because it let them focus on business building without managing basic infrastructure ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_13-stretching-our-limits]]).

*Venture Studios Demystified* records the same value proposition from the studio side: de-risked venture building with strong administrative support, lower founder distraction and access to shared infrastructure. It adds two qualifications. This appeals most to second-time founders who want to focus on product rather than logistics, and it only works with a strong studio reputation and favourable equity terms ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_08-working-with-founders]]). The book's stakeholder chapter is blunter about the price: entrepreneurs sacrifice founding equity and early-stage autonomy, and the size of that sacrifice depends on the alternatives they have and on the studio's operational depth. For someone whose alternative was not starting a company at all, it may be more than acceptable; for a repeat founder it is a "hefty price tag" for a non-technical co-founder whose services are paid for in pre-seed and often stop later ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_23-implications-for-stakeholders]]).

### Who studios look for

The profiles converge on experienced, coachable operators, with both books noting that studios also develop younger talent.

- **eFounders** sets the bar at entrepreneurs capable of managing billion-dollar companies. Technical founders must be full-stack builders with future-CTO leadership potential; business founders combine analytical rigour, product thinking and the ability to convince stakeholders ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_07-game-plan]]).
- **The Playbook's EIR profile** is part entrepreneur, part manager: able to discover market needs with lean methods, direct a core team toward a viable product, sell and generate traction, and raise follow-on funding, while accepting conflicting priorities across several ventures. Candidates are assessed on five factors: previous track record, leadership style, network in the specific market, coachability, and willingness to work across multiple ventures and eventually release mature companies ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_17-entrepreneurs-in-residence-and-ceos]]).
- **Studio leaders** must attract experienced entrepreneurs as CEOs and EIRs while also identifying and nurturing young talent from within ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_14-studio-leadership]]). Drukka's recruits were young, talented entrepreneurs who had already tried building a startup or a lifestyle business ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_13-stretching-our-limits]]).
- **Across the 19 studios** in *Venture Studios Demystified*, most prioritise second-time founders for their networks and experience, despite the difficulty of recruiting them, and also target first-time founders with domain expertise, technical skills and entrepreneurial spirit. Named traits: catalytic persona, grit, resilience, domain expertise and familiarity with the studio's focus. Structured programs select on the likelihood of being an entrepreneur rather than on idea quality ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_08-working-with-founders]]).

The Playbook warns that ready-made EIRs are rare: candidates are either inexperienced in one or more fields, or busy with their own projects and hard to commit. Juniors need training investment; veterans demand compelling compensation ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_17-entrepreneurs-in-residence-and-ceos]]).

### Sourcing: channels and models

*Venture Studios Demystified* sorts studios into three recruitment models, each with a different trade-off between scale, upfront cost and founder motivation ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_08-working-with-founders]]):

1. **Structured founder programs (37% of studios)**: rolling application cycles, public role descriptions, formal selection, designed to attract many candidates and build a network. Short programs of about 3 months screen ideas and founders cheaply; longer programs invest more and expect an investable concept within 6-12 months.
2. **Limited full-time positions (37%)**: 2-4 founders at a time, hired through the studio's own network.
3. **Assignment to a pre-validated concept (26%)**: internal ideation does the early work and an external founder is brought in as CEO once the idea is validated.

Which model fits depends on the studio's internal capacity: studios with a research and business-design team can assign founders to validated concepts, while studios with lighter internal processes need a program or a small EIR bench and rely on founder-led ideation ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_08-working-with-founders]]). The stakeholder chapter adds that no structure is superior: studios succeed with structured and with ad-hoc founder programs, depending on philosophy, local environment and fundraising constraints ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_23-implications-for-stakeholders]]).

The Playbook's guidance is about widening the top of that funnel. Personal networks are limited and do not scale, so a studio must build brand presence, speak at meetups, build community and experiment with programs such as Hackers-in-Residence to reach candidates beyond the founder's circle. The hardest part is convincing senior entrepreneurs to commit when the studio has no prior results ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_17-entrepreneurs-in-residence-and-ceos]]). eFounders shows what a mature funnel looks like: 100+ applications a month for 4 slots a year ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_07-game-plan]]).

### Selection

eFounders has every applicant meet the entire team individually before any offer ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_07-game-plan]]). Drukka folded selection into validation: each incoming CEO ran 3-5 bare-minimum concept validations in parallel, and the viable ideas, and by implication the CEOs who could run them, emerged from that ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_13-stretching-our-limits]]). The Playbook recommends profiling the ideal CEO persona from the characteristics of the startups the studio wants to build, then assessing against the five factors above ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_17-entrepreneurs-in-residence-and-ceos]]).

### Compensation: cash

*Venture Studios Demystified* is the only source with cash figures. Full-time EIRs often earn $150,000+ in major markets, paid from the studio's operating budget; term-based contractors receive monthly or full-term stipends over 6-12 month contracts; a founder retained to run a venture draws salary either from studio operating funds or from the capital invested in that venture ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_08-working-with-founders]]). The Playbook says only that compensation combines a competitive salary with equity ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_17-entrepreneurs-in-residence-and-ceos]]).

### Compensation: equity, where the books disagree

- **The *Startup Studio Playbook* (2019)** reports studios taking 30-80% of equity, with an average of 50%, justified by the studio's provision of idea, infrastructure, capital and resources. CEO equity can be rebalanced at Series A to keep the CEO motivated and make new investors comfortable, diluting the studio and fund shares ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_17-entrepreneurs-in-residence-and-ceos]], [[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_14-studio-leadership]]).
- ***Venture Studios Demystified* (2022)** reports that the historical majority stake (over 50%) is now the exception: only 4 of 19 studios take majority equity and the common range is 25-40%. The stated reason is recruitment. High studio stakes prevented studios from securing top founding talent and confused VC investors, so the market moved to levels founders and investors would accept. Stakes vary widely by venture (5-50% within a single portfolio) according to whether the studio generated the idea, the founder's experience, any capital the founder contributes, salary arrangements and the founder's role ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_14-equity-ownership]]). Studios with structured programs or a limited EIR bench typically take 35%+, while some explore lighter 10% models to support more ventures without full co-founder involvement ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_08-working-with-founders]]).

The gap is partly three years of market movement and partly vantage point: the Playbook's average is a practitioner's figure from the studios it knew, the Demystified range is a survey of 19 studios. Both books agree on the direction of adjustment, toward the founder, and on the reason, keeping the CEO motivated and the cap table acceptable to investors. Where they differ is on the starting point. See [[Equity Ownership]] for the full treatment.

### Bring founders in early

*Venture Studios Demystified* observed stronger outcomes when the founding team or CEO joined earlier, even at the cost of giving them more founding equity. Assigning a founder to a fully developed concept later reduces their personal investment and their ability to put a stamp on the business, which increases venture risk. One quoted studio removed the equity ask from its earliest-stage program specifically to lower the friction for founders joining before an idea is finalised ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_08-working-with-founders]]). This cuts against the third recruitment model above, which the same chapter reports 26% of studios using; the book presents both the practice and the observation without resolving them.

### After the hire: development and retention

The Playbook treats recruitment as inseparable from development, because handpicked founders do not scale. Studios need a CEO development system covering generic startup know-how from idea to exit, ideation and validation, sales and growth hacking, investor relations, project and people management, and legal, regulatory and financial company management. Ongoing management means one-on-ones that check progress and keep the EIR neither overloaded nor bored, studio participation in seed fundraising meetings, and structured knowledge sharing across the EIR cohort. These practices signal that the studio cares about individual success and motivate people to stay despite eventually releasing their companies ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_17-entrepreneurs-in-residence-and-ceos]]). The leadership chapter names the underlying retention problem: highly talented venture builders are asked to work on multiple ideas they must eventually let go, so the studio has to maintain constant inspiration and address the repetitiveness of the work ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_14-studio-leadership]]). See [[Team Scaling]] and [[Studio Culture]].

### Open questions the sources leave

*Venture Studios Demystified* notes that studios claim a secret sauce in founder recruitment, yet most operate similar mechanisms, and that it is unclear whether process nuances drive differentiated returns; as the market crowds, those nuances may become the differentiator ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_23-implications-for-stakeholders]]). The Playbook's open problem is the cold start: senior entrepreneurs will not commit to a studio without results, and results require senior entrepreneurs ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_17-entrepreneurs-in-residence-and-ceos]]).
