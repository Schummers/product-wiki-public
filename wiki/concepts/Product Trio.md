# Product Trio

## Definition

The product trio is the small cross-functional group that makes product decisions
together instead of leaving them to one role. Teresa Torres, in *Continuous
Discovery Habits*, names it precisely: a product manager, a designer and a
software engineer, working as the core decision-making unit and leveraging each
role's expertise, a group that can be widened with further members as long as the
speed of decision-making allows
([[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_04-chapter-one-the-what-and-why-of-continuous-discovery]]).
The rationale is that no single role holds enough of the picture: the designer
sees where customers get confused, the engineer sees process interdependencies,
the product manager knows the pain points already documented
([[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_07-chapter-four-visualizing-what-you-know]]).

Jeff Patton, in *User Story Mapping*, arrives at a comparable grouping from a
different direction and gives it a different name and a different composition. He
calls the small discovery team a **triad**: two to four people (he describes it as
dinner-conversation-sized) led by a product owner, including someone who
understands users and can prototype and a senior engineer, together looking for
the solution that is valuable, usable and feasible. Patton is explicit that
"triad" names the three concerns, not necessarily three bodies, and that the
product owner leads and makes the final call rather than the group deciding
democratically ([[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_17-12-rock-breakers]]). The two
descriptions overlap but are not interchangeable: Torres's trio is three named
roles jointly owning discovery decisions, Patton's triad is a small team under a
product owner covering three perspectives.

**Patton's "three amigos" are a third, distinct grouping and should not be merged
with either.** Patton numbers them a tester, a member of the core discovery team,
and a user experience designer or business analyst, with a developer from the
building team in the conversation as well; they meet during **story workshops** in
delivery, to finalise story details and acceptance criteria in the last best
conversation before building. He notes the name is shared with a 1986 comedy but
says he does not know who first applied it to Agile. Their
purpose is right-sizing and detailing stories, not exploring the opportunity
space ([[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_17-12-rock-breakers]]).

## Practice

### What the trio is for (Torres)

- Discovery is a whole-team responsibility: Chris Mercuri, writing the foreword
  from his own team's experience, describes most team members taking part in
  customer interviews, journey mapping, ideation and the discussion of results,
  rather than research being handed to one person
  ([[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_01-foreword-chris-mercuri]]).
- The [[Opportunity Solution Tree]] exists in large part to serve the trio: it is
  designed so that the three can build shared understanding and decide together by
  seeing their options laid out
  ([[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_05-chapter-two-a-common-framework-for-continuous-discovery]]).
- The trio is also the unit that catches its own mistakes: Torres presents the
  structure as what lets a team recognise it is going down a troublesome path and
  course-correct collaboratively
  ([[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_15-chapter-twelve-managing-the-cycles]]).

### Assign the outcome to the trio, not to a person

Torres is firm on this: a [[Product Outcome]] goes to the trio as a unit, never to
an individual member, precisely so that product manager, designer and engineer are
forced to collaborate on a shared goal; assigning individual outcomes is one of the
anti-patterns she lists
([[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_06-chapter-three-focusing-on-outcomes-over-outputs]]).

### Work individually first, then merge

When the trio maps what it already knows, Torres has each member build their own
[[Experience Map]] alone before the three are merged. Doing the individual pass
first prevents groupthink and stops the decision from being made by the loudest
voice or the most senior person in the room
([[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_07-chapter-four-visualizing-what-you-know]]).

### Building a trio where none exists

Torres's advice for organisations with no formal trio structure is to start small
rather than wait for permission: if a designer or engineer is not interested in a
full discovery partnership, ask them to weigh in on one small decision, then
iterate from there
([[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_17-chapter-fourteen-start-small-and-iterate]]).

### Why one product owner is not enough (Patton)

- Patton challenges the Agile assumption that a single product owner writes all the
  stories and attends all the conversations. Two reasons: there are too many
  conversations for one person, and one person cannot hold the business acumen,
  user experience expertise and technical knowledge needed to find the
  valuable-usable-feasible sweet spot
  ([[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_17-12-rock-breakers]]).
- His answer is the small cross-functional discovery team, which he says moves
  faster and finds more innovative solutions than a lone product owner. The product
  owner keeps the team focused on business vision and strategy, makes the call when
  trade-offs are needed, and remains accountable for a cohesive product: "Design by
  community is not design by committee…design is never democratic" (same chapter).
- Patton warns against the client-vendor anti-pattern, where conversation is
  replaced by a requirements specification and a handoff. He proposes reframing the
  relationship as doctor-patient (an expert listening deeply) or producer-artist, a
  metaphor he attributes to a casual conversation with his friend David Hussman
  (same chapter).
- Beyond the triad, Patton observes that a story attracts several parallel
  conversations because each role cares about something different: market
  hypotheses, business rules, failure modes, usability, dependencies, schedule.
  That chapter does not use the word triad, but the diversity of roles it lists
  (product manager, designer, tester, developer, analyst, writer) is his argument
  for why product decisions need cross-functional input
  ([[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_13-8-its-not-all-on-the-card]]). See
  [[Team Collaboration]].

### Where the two works differ

Both books reject the lone decision-maker, but they do not prescribe the same
group. Torres fixes the composition (product manager, designer, engineer) and
distributes ownership across the three, expanding the group only when
decision-making speed permits
([[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_04-chapter-one-the-what-and-why-of-continuous-discovery]]).
Patton fixes a size range (two to four) and a leader, insisting the product owner
decides after incorporating the diverse expertise
([[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_17-12-rock-breakers]]). And in Patton's
vocabulary the delivery-stage three amigos are a separate grouping again, adding a
tester alongside the designer or analyst and limiting the scope to the story
workshop.
