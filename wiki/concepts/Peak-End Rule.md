# Peak-End Rule

## Definition

The peak-end rule holds that people judge an experience largely on how they
felt at its most emotionally intense moment and at its end, rather than on the
total sum or average of every moment
([[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_08-6-peakend-rule]]). Memory is not an accurate record
of events: the mind averages the moments that stand out, so peaks and endings
are weighted far beyond their share of the elapsed time
([[2018-12-30_peak-end-rule]]).

Both sources trace the rule to Daniel Kahneman's research, first explored in
1993 and documented with Barbara Frederickson
([[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_08-6-peakend-rule]], [[2018-12-30_peak-end-rule]]). In
the hand-immersion experiment, participants preferred a second trial of 60
seconds in 14°C water followed by 30 seconds as the water warmed to 15°C over a
first trial of 60 seconds at 14°C alone, and 80% chose to repeat the longer,
more painful trial because its final moments were slightly warmer
([[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_08-6-peakend-rule]], [[2018-12-30_peak-end-rule]]). A
randomized colonoscopy study showed the same pattern: patients whose procedure
was extended by three extra minutes with the scope tip left in experienced the
final moments as less painful, rated the whole experience as less unpleasant,
and were more likely to return, compared with patients who underwent the
standard, shorter version ([[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_08-6-peakend-rule]]). For
design, the consequence is that a small improvement near the end of an
experience can radically shift how the whole event is remembered
([[2018-12-30_peak-end-rule]]).

## Practice

### Design deliberate positive peaks

- Elements that highlight convenience, comfort and delight turn a merely
  pleasant experience into a memorable one; Duolingo reinforces positive
  moments with playful feedback ([[2018-12-30_peak-end-rule]]).
- Spotify Wrapped, Duolingo's gamified achievement celebrations and its
  Legendary Level rewards are given as examples of peak moments engineered to
  leave a lasting positive impression
  ([[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_08-6-peakend-rule]]). This is the domain of
  [[Emotional Design]].

### Treat the ending as disproportionate

- Users judge an experience heavily on how it concludes. Progress indicators
  that accelerate toward completion feel faster, and a celebratory endpoint,
  such as TurboTax's tax-filing completion screen, leaves a positive lasting
  impression ([[2018-12-30_peak-end-rule]]).
- Attention to detail around the important moments of the journey, with
  particular emphasis on the last step, is what makes a product worth
  remembering ([[2018-12-30_peak-end-rule]]).

### Prevent negative peaks, and defuse the ones you cannot remove

- Humans register and dwell on negative events more readily than positive ones,
  a negativity bias that makes bad peaks especially costly
  ([[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_08-6-peakend-rule]]); see [[Cognitive Bias]].
- Confusion and frustration are themselves emotionally charged peaks: a
  difficult navigation menu encountered early in a site visit shaped a user's
  overall assessment and willingness to recommend the company
  ([[2018-12-30_peak-end-rule]]).
- Prevent them with proactive guidance, such as Mailchimp's real-time password
  validation, and humanize unavoidable friction with brand personality
  ([[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_08-6-peakend-rule]]).
- Handle errors and service disruptions gracefully, particularly at endpoints:
  unclear messages that blame the user or offer no path to resolution deepen
  the negative moment and become the lasting impression
  ([[2018-12-30_peak-end-rule]]).
- Uber's approach to waiting combines idleness aversion (entertaining
  animations), operational transparency (estimated times and clarity about how
  they are calculated) and the goal gradient effect (progressive updates),
  which reduced post-request cancellation rates
  ([[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_08-6-peakend-rule]]).

### Locate the peaks before designing for them

- Identifying where the emotional peaks fall requires [[Journey Mapping]]:
  visualizing the user's perspective (persona and expectations), their
  experience (actions, mindset, emotions) and the actionable insights that
  follow. The emotional layer of the map is what shows where the peaks are
  ([[2024-01-23_laws-of-ux_08-6-peakend-rule]]).
