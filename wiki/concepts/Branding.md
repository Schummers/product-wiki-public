# Branding

## Definition

Branding is no longer the management of a visual mark but the management of a
perception built from everything a person experiences with a product or a
company. [[2016-07-03_brand-experience-ux]] defines brand as a subjective
perception of value based on the sum of a person's experiences with a product or
company, which ultimately influences that person's sentiment and decisions in
the marketplace, and holds that it is expressed through three equally important
components: visuals (logos, typefaces, graphic elements), tone (communication
style across channels), and behavior (how the company and its systems act).
Because nearly every brand now has a digital presence, customers meet the brand
mostly through interactive interfaces, which makes user experience itself a
brand differentiator: most people cannot separate how they feel about a brand
from how they feel about their experiences with it.

That widened definition puts brand-building inside the interface. Interaction
qualities such as responsiveness, clear labels and consistency map onto brand
attributes like sincerity, competence and sophistication
[[2016-07-17_interaction-branding]]; the tone of small feedback moments carries
brand personality [[2018-10-21_microinteractions]]; the visual treatment of
content signals whether a site is trustworthy or advertising-driven
[[2018-04-22_banner-blindness-old-and-new-findings]]. At the same time, the
classic visual carriers still matter and are measurable: logo placement and
legibility have quantified effects on whether users remember the brand at all
[[2016-02-21_logo-placement-brand-recall]]. Branding also applies inside the
organisation, where an intranet needs its own name, logo and tone
[[2018-12-23_branding-intranet]].

The newest source approaches brand from a different starting point: a company
that does not yet have a product, an interface, or users to measure.
[[2026-03-05_10x-method_07-brand-and-comms]] holds that a startup already has a
brand whether it designed one or not, because customers, investors and partners
form impressions from the landing page, the pitch deck, the founder's LinkedIn
bio and the job posts; the only question is whether those signals are
intentional and consistent or scattered and generic. It also makes brand the
durable differentiator, but for a different reason than the NN/g articles do:
with AI and faster development tools making products easier to copy, functional
differences shrink, and it is the emotional response people have to the company
that drives choice. The two are not describing the same object — NN/g reads
brand off an existing product's interface, the 10x Method builds one before
there is a product to read — but both place the differentiator outside the
feature list: exceptional user experience in one case
[[2016-07-03_brand-experience-ux]], brand feeling and the people behind the
company in the other [[2026-03-05_10x-method_07-brand-and-comms]].

## Practice

### Brand as an experience system, not an asset kit

- Treat visuals, tone and behavior as equally weighted expressions of the brand,
  and check that all three are present and true to the core message across every
  interaction; consistency across channels is what produces a coherent
  experience [[2016-07-03_brand-experience-ux]].
- When customers cannot tell their feelings about the brand apart from their
  feelings about using it, exceptional UX becomes the competitive advantage —
  which makes interaction details a brand investment rather than a usability
  detail [[2016-07-03_brand-experience-ux]].

### Defining the brand before there is a product

The guidance in this section comes from a startup founder's manual and applies
to a company at the stage where none of the interface measures below can be
taken yet [[2026-03-05_10x-method_07-brand-and-comms]].

- **Write the core elements down in one place**: vision, mission, target users,
  key benefits and company values. The document exists to force clarity and to
  be referred back to, so that landing pages, hiring, positioning and strategic
  decisions all keep to a single narrative thread
  [[2026-03-05_10x-method_07-brand-and-comms]].
- **Translate those elements into a feeling and a voice.** Define the emotional
  response you want people to have (liberation, empowerment, quiet confidence)
  and choose two or three adjectives describing how the brand should sound to
  evoke it; that choice then guides everything from website copy to customer
  support responses [[2026-03-05_10x-method_07-brand-and-comms]]. This lands on
  the same requirement NN/g reaches from the other end — consistency of tone
  across every channel [[2016-07-03_brand-experience-ux]] — but derives it from
  a written brand document rather than from an audit of existing touchpoints.
- **Do most of it yourself, early.** Hexa says a founder can do 90–100% of the
  early brand: a simple logo, a basic palette built in Figma or Canva, one or
  two signature colours and a clean font, applied consistently. Under a small
  budget (even below 5,000 euros) a freelance designer or a small studio agency
  can produce a logo, a basic graphic system and a lightweight style guide;
  large agencies belong to a later stage, when scale calls for strategic work.
  A PR agency is called a poor fit before Series A
  [[2026-03-05_10x-method_07-brand-and-comms]].
- **The founder's own visibility is the most powerful early channel.** While the
  company is unknown, the founder is the entry point: build an authentic
  presence where the audience already is (LinkedIn, Twitter, podcasts, blogs),
  stay consistent, and diversify opportunistically through earned media. The
  founder story — why you started, what problem you experienced — humanises the
  company, and what matters is genuine alignment between who you are and how you
  express yourself [[2026-03-05_10x-method_07-brand-and-comms]] (see
  [[Founder Mindset]]).
- **Early PR shapes perception rather than selling.** It is not advertising but
  helping journalists tell stories that matter to their audiences: build
  relationships before you need them, position yourself as an expert in your
  space, and pitch stories grounded in broader industry trends rather than in
  the product alone [[2026-03-05_10x-method_07-brand-and-comms]].

### Interaction qualities that carry brand attributes

- [[2016-07-17_interaction-branding]] reports research on eight interactivity
  attributes: responsiveness, direct vs. indirect manipulation, precision,
  pliability, continuous vs. discrete behavior, clear labels, expected vs.
  unexpected behavior, and consistency. Clear labels that suggest the action
  triggered joy and associations with sincerity and competence; elements that
  behaved as expected or consistently produced the same associations.
- Inconsistent or unexpected behavior triggered surprise and anger. Deliberately
  unexpected interactions can produce pleasant surprise but can also make users
  feel powerless, so test them with real users
  [[2016-07-17_interaction-branding]].
- The recommended exercise is to inventory the interaction-design attributes of
  a product and ask deliberately whether each supports or contradicts the stated
  brand values [[2016-07-17_interaction-branding]].
- Microinteractions are a concrete lever for the same thing: the tone and style
  of feedback communicate brand values, with celebratory animation fitting
  playful brands and subtle, efficient feedback fitting professional ones
  [[2018-10-21_microinteractions]]. The emotional payoff of well-designed
  feedback, such as celebrating a completed task, builds positive brand
  perception [[2018-10-21_microinteractions]].

### Logo placement and brand recall

- Put the logo in the top left. In a study of 128 users, an average of 39%
  recalled the brand name with left-aligned logos against 21% with right-aligned
  logos, an 89% lift and among the largest effects seen in UX metrics research
  [[2016-02-21_logo-placement-brand-recall]].
- Breaking the convention buys nothing in perception: users rated right-aligned
  and left-aligned versions almost identically on uniqueness, stylishness,
  welcoming nature and purchase intent [[2016-02-21_logo-placement-brand-recall]].
- The two logo studies do not report the same effect for every off-convention
  position. Right-aligned logos measurably damaged recall
  [[2016-02-21_logo-placement-brand-recall]], while centered logos showed no
  statistically significant impact on aided recall across hotel websites, with
  contrast, padding and visual design mattering more for recall
  [[2016-07-10_centered-logos]]. Centered logos nevertheless carry a heavy
  navigation cost: users were six times as likely to fail to reach the homepage
  in one click [[2016-07-10_centered-logos]].
- Legibility outranks position: a hard-to-decipher logo script dramatically
  reduces recall wherever it sits [[2016-02-21_logo-placement-brand-recall]].
- The left-placement finding rests on left-to-right language readers; users of
  right-to-left scripts may benefit from mirrored layouts, though this was not
  tested [[2016-02-21_logo-placement-brand-recall]].
- Beyond recall, the top-left logo communicates current location, reinforces the
  brand through repeated exposure and offers a one-click path home
  [[2016-07-10_centered-logos]].

### Not looking like an advertisement

- Legitimate content styled like an ad — animation, fancy formatting, coloured
  backgrounds, embedded text, or placement in a top banner or right rail — gets
  ignored, and the hot-potato effect can spread that avoidance to an entire
  section [[2018-04-22_banner-blindness-old-and-new-findings]].
- The brand-side conclusion is to keep brand trust by refusing deceptive,
  ad-like treatments for legitimate content, even when that treatment would make
  the content more salient [[2018-04-22_banner-blindness-old-and-new-findings]].

### Branding for internal audiences

- Weak or absent intranet branding costs credibility, adoption and acceptance;
  intranets are often built by IT, HR or Communications without branding
  expertise [[2018-12-23_branding-intranet]].
- Name it memorably and pronounceably. Clever acronyms, uncommon words and
  wordplay backfire by creating doubt about pronunciation and spelling, while
  simple names (Portal, Center, Inside [Company], [Company] Hub) work reliably;
  crafty names such as JetNet can succeed if tested
  [[2018-12-23_branding-intranet]].
- Choose a name the organisation can live with for years: employees keep using
  the old name after a redesign, although renaming during a significant redesign
  can signal transformation [[2018-12-23_branding-intranet]].
- Effective intranet logos often show just the intranet name. Pairing the
  company logo with the intranet name keeps the brand connection while
  differentiating from the public site; the company logo alone leaves users
  unsure which site they are on [[2018-12-23_branding-intranet]].
- Colours and fonts can follow corporate guidelines while the tone of voice
  differs: employee-facing communication need not match external messaging
  [[2018-12-23_branding-intranet]].
- Avoid overbranding the interface. Do not give features catchy names ("Upload
  Wizard" instead of "Upload a Document") and do not use third-party software
  brand names as labels ("Job Postings", not "SAS"); both obscure what the
  feature does [[2018-12-23_branding-intranet]].
