# Artificial Intelligence

## Definition

Across these sources, artificial intelligence is treated as "intelligence" demonstrated by machines, implemented and exhibited differently from human intelligence [[2024-07-12_artificial-intelligence-glossary]]. In practice the sources describe a family of technologies rather than one thing: algorithms that infer preferences from behavioural data and produce recommendations [[2018-12-16_machine-learning-ux]], natural-language understanding behind assistants and chatbots [[2018-07-22_intelligent-assistant-usability]] [[2018-11-25_chatbots]], and generative models that construct new text or imagery. The generative layer, dominated by large language models, is the one most sources address after 2024. Its defining mechanic is probabilistic: an LLM does not retrieve indexed content the way a search engine does, it generates an answer word by word from statistical patterns learned in training, which is precisely why it can hallucinate and state incorrect information confidently [[2025-05-02_ai-model-training]]. Those patterns are laid down in distinct phases (unsupervised pretraining on vast internet text, supervised finetuning on curated prompt-response pairs, reinforcement learning from human feedback), each contributing different capabilities and each introducing its own bias [[2025-05-02_ai-model-training]].

On top of that base layer the sources distinguish degrees of autonomy. An AI agent is defined as a system that pursues a goal by iteratively taking actions, evaluating progress and deciding its own next steps, mapped against automation and simple tools on two axes, capability to act and self-direction; an LLM that answers once is the reasoning engine, not the agent [[2026-04-03_definition-ai-agent]]. Digital twins push the same generative base in another direction, using an LLM primed with personal data as a proxy for a specific individual [[2025-08-01_digital-twins]]. For designers, the recurring argument is that AI must not be treated as a magic black box: understanding how the models actually work is what allows practitioners to reason from facts rather than from hype [[2024-12-31_358_Top_3_des_livres_pour_Designers_en_2025]], and AI literacy is now foundational to contemporary UX work [[2025-12-12_top-articles-2025]].

## Practice

### Understand the mechanism before judging the capability

Several sources make technical understanding a prerequisite for good design decisions. Knowing how models are trained is what produces an accurate mental model of what an LLM can and cannot do, including why hallucination is structural rather than accidental [[2025-05-02_ai-model-training]]. A shared vocabulary matters too: the glossary exists because AI is a fast-moving technology loaded with jargon, and discussing capability or limitation requires the basics (algorithms, datasets, models, prompting strategies such as chain-of-thought, zero-shot and few-shot) [[2024-07-12_artificial-intelligence-glossary]]. The same point is made from the practitioner side: designers should read on the technical workings of AI to stop perceiving it as a magic black box [[2024-12-31_358_Top_3_des_livres_pour_Designers_en_2025]], and should get their hands into the technology, including AI APIs and LLMs, because staying abstract and far from the technical layer limits the chance of discovering high-value new uses [[2025-09-16_395_L_UX_Design_est_mort_L_impact_de_l_IA_&_du_business_sur_le_métier_de_designer]].

Definitional discipline is part of this. Because "agent" is applied to everything, the useful test is self-direction plus capability to act, and usefulness should be judged on whether the agent reliably understands goals, adapts when things fail, and minimises required human intervention, evaluated through user research and task-completion metrics rather than technical claims [[2026-04-03_definition-ai-agent]].

### Natural language remains the weak link

The earliest sources here document how far natural-language understanding falls short of what the interface promises. Intelligent assistants misunderstand multiclause sentences, return different results for equivalent formulations of the same query, and handle pronoun references poorly, forcing users to decompose questions into simple ones and to plan the phrasing before speaking [[2018-07-22_intelligent-assistant-usability]]. The diary study frames the same gap as a requirement: assistants must understand varied, complex utterances without demanding simplified or keyword-based language, and 84% of users' ideal needs assumed free-form spoken commands [[2018-10-21_intelligent-assistant-user-needs]]. Chatbots show the same failure at smaller scale, struggling with typos, ambiguous phrasing and context, working smoothly only while the user follows the expected script and breaking as soon as the input deviates [[2018-11-25_chatbots]]. One consequence is behavioural: when users know they are talking to a bot they adapt, becoming more direct and keyword-focused and dropping politeness markers the bot cannot parse [[2018-11-25_chatbots]].

Not every AI interaction has to be a text or voice conversation. Arc's Call Arc feature is presented as evidence that AI can leave the classic chat format entirely, reusing an anchored gesture (raising the phone to the ear as for a call) so that the interaction becomes immediately legible to a non-technical audience [[2024-05-28_330_Call_Arc,_l_interface_ChatGPT_parfaite_-_Analyse_Product_Design]].

### Working with AI inside the design process

The sources converge on a division of labour: AI accelerates execution, humans keep the judgement.

- **Narrow-scope beats broad-scope.** Focused features for repetitive tasks (renaming layers, rewriting copy, finding assets, generating colour palettes, producing placeholder images) are genuinely adopted and save real time, while tools claiming to generate whole designs produce generic layouts with poor information hierarchy and only minor variations rather than meaningful alternatives [[2025-05-09_ai-design-tools-update-2]]. The same split appears on the Parlons Design side, where the four tools in daily use cover image upscaling, slide illustration, brainstorming and research synthesis, and none of them replaces the designer's work [[2024-06-25_334_Les_4_outils_IA_pour_designers_product,_UX_&_UI_en_2024]].
- **Prompt specificity is the main quality lever.** Longer, more detailed prompts with clear requirements yield markedly better output, and design artefacts (Figma links, high-fidelity mockups) beat words because the model can inspect existing patterns and spatial relationships [[2025-10-24_ai-prototyping]]. Vague prompts produce "Frankenstein layouts" that feel randomly pieced together, with redundant information, prominent containers holding almost nothing, and visual hierarchy contradicting task priority [[2025-12-05_vague-prototyping]]. The fix is precision rather than verbosity: established design vocabulary ("neobrutalism" instead of "modern"), lightweight visual references such as moodboards or design-system screenshots, using a chatbot to extract design characteristics from a reference first, generating mock data to guide layout, and attaching code snippets for structural context [[2025-12-05_vague-prototyping]].
- **Prompting has a ceiling.** Prompt-length limits (around 500 characters in the tools reviewed) cannot carry enough context for contextual design decisions, and some interfaces actively discourage long prompts through the size of the input box [[2025-05-09_ai-design-tools-update-2]]. From the practitioner's side, the effort has to pay for itself: spending 45 minutes writing a prompt means the work would have been faster done by hand [[2024-06-25_334_Les_4_outils_IA_pour_designers_product,_UX_&_UI_en_2024]].
- **Ambiguous UX language must be disambiguated.** Terms like "profile page" mean different things, so designers have to communicate context precisely to prevent misinterpretation [[2025-10-24_ai-prototyping]].
- **Design-system integration is the missing piece.** Most designers work within an established system, and AI prototypes assembled from random elements are worth little because current tools cannot pull from the design system to produce cohesive results across contexts [[2025-05-09_ai-design-tools-update-2]].
- **AI reproduces its training data.** Outputs skew toward mainstream conventions and generic visual styles, sans-serif typefaces and minimalist styling, because the model learned the common patterns [[2025-10-24_ai-prototyping]].
- **Skill is a prerequisite, not a substitute.** AI promises to close skill gaps but works best for people who already understand layout, typography and component naming well enough to direct it [[2025-10-24_ai-prototyping]]; thinking through requirements, weighing tradeoffs and making informed decisions remain the designer's work [[2025-12-05_vague-prototyping]]; and no current tool replicates a fraction of what a human UX designer produces, despite vendor claims [[2025-05-09_ai-design-tools-update-2]].

The same boundary shows up outside interface work. For product documentation, AI is explicitly discouraged for building the outline, because it loses context and edge cases, but recommended for turning bullet points into written paragraphs, simplifying text, or challenging a finished draft [[2025-06-24_383_Guide_Rédiger_la_documentation_produit]]. In advertising, the recommended use is internal assistance (early ideation, copy refinement, microcopy testing) while human ownership of creative direction is retained [[2025-12-19_ai-ad]].

Where the sources differ is in tone about the ceiling. The NN/g evaluations are consistently sober, framing AI output as "good from afar, but far from good" and stressing what the tools still cannot do [[2025-10-24_ai-prototyping]] [[2025-05-09_ai-design-tools-update-2]], while the Parlons Design interview with Romain Dao emphasises expanded possibility, arguing that AI removes cost and skill barriers to instant experimentation to the point that the only limit is the designer's imagination [[2024-10-08_349_L_IA_dans_le_design_usages_concrets_et_avenir_avec_Romain_Dao]]. The two are not symmetrical claims, one is about the ceiling of finished quality and the other about the floor of what is now cheap to try, but the difference in emphasis is real.

### Designing AI-powered experiences for users

- **Make the inputs visible.** Users treat machine-learning systems as black boxes and try to infer input-output relations by experimentation; when the inputs are unclear or delayed, accurate mental models become impossible and people assume they are tracked far more invasively than they are [[2018-12-16_machine-learning-ux]]. Netflix's "Because you watched X" labels are cited as the best practice for revealing which action drove a recommendation, though even Netflix failed when outputs contradicted expectations [[2018-12-16_machine-learning-ux]].
- **Give control over the outputs.** Beyond opaque inputs, the second structural problem is that users cannot predict or influence what the algorithm returns; imperfect relevance (missed items, unpredictable ordering, low-interest recommendations) makes them inspect and skip irrelevant content, and session-specific layouts or duplicated items can make personalisation cost more effort than it saves [[2018-12-16_machine-learning-ux]].
- **Earn the creepiness tradeoff.** AI-powered personalisation and prediction are often perceived as creepy, and users run an explicit cost-benefit analysis; the benefit must be genuine and perceived, since vague promises of a "better experience" rarely convince, and explaining what is collected and why helps cautious users cross the threshold [[2019-06-02_creepiness]].
- **Explanations are necessary and unreliable at once.** Users increasingly act on AI chat output, but modern models are complex enough that even engineers cannot always trace how an output was produced. Source citations are frequently hallucinated, pointing to nonexistent URLs or unrelated articles, and their mere presence creates false confidence because users rarely click through; step-by-step reasoning often reads as transparent while being an after-the-fact rationalisation rather than a faithful trace; disclaimers fail because people skim fine print [[2025-12-12_explainable-ai]]. UX teams cannot fix the model, but they can style citations prominently and place them adjacent to the claim they support, write disclaimers in clear action-oriented language ("Please double-check responses") near the input box and in onboarding, avoid first-person anthropomorphic phrasing that inflates trust, and be honest about limitations instead of manufacturing an impression of certainty [[2025-12-12_explainable-ai]].
- **Set expectations about capability.** Chatbots should indicate what they can do, offer both predetermined buttons and free-text input rather than forcing one modality, and provide an escape hatch to a human that preserves context [[2018-11-25_chatbots]]. Users also expect proactivity that current systems rarely deliver: 12% of logged ideal needs involved the assistant acting from context without an explicit command [[2018-10-21_intelligent-assistant-user-needs]].
- **Don't lead with the technology.** "AI-powered" is meaningless to users; the question is whether AI solves a real user problem better than the existing approach, and audiences notice when the narrative is bent to accommodate the technology rather than driven by creative intent [[2025-12-19_ai-ad]]. Adopting AI is itself read as a signal of organisational priorities, efficiency over craft or cost-cutting over care, which in emotional domains can be received as a betrayal of brand heritage [[2025-12-19_ai-ad]].

### Adopting AI in a team or organisation

Telling employees to use AI without saying how, why, or which problem it solves creates chaos: performative usage where people cite AI in status updates to look current, individual "grimoires" of prompts trapped in single heads, context lost while manually copying between tools at different workflow stages, security violations when customer data or participant transcripts are uploaded to public tools, and collaboration breakdown when teams cannot build on each other's work [[2025-10-31_ai-research-ops]]. The prescription is to treat AI like any significant organisational change: analyse current workflows to find the real problems, design targeted interventions, invest in integrated tools with security controls, write explicit policies, build a shared prompt repository, pilot before scaling, and provide training and change management [[2025-10-31_ai-research-ops]]. Adoption is being measured before value is [[2025-10-31_ai-research-ops]], and the advertising case makes the parallel warning that metrics rewarding AI adoption for its own sake produce the wrong strategy [[2025-12-19_ai-ad]].

### Ethics, cost and research use

Ethical and environmental limits recur. Deploying AI tools raises real questions about artistic inspiration and the appropriation of styles, and the multiplication of cheap tests has a direct environmental cost through energy consumption [[2024-10-08_349_L_IA_dans_le_design_usages_concrets_et_avenir_avec_Romain_Dao]]. Training itself carries both an environmental and a labour cost: unsupervised learning demands enormous computation over weeks or months, and RLHF depends on thousands of human labellers, often low-paid and exposed to sensitive content [[2025-05-02_ai-model-training]]. The advertising backlash added labour displacement to that list [[2025-12-19_ai-ad]].

Using AI as a research subject rather than a tool is the sharpest case. Digital twins can fill missing survey data, shorten surveys by inferring skipped answers, stand in for hard-to-reach participants and anticipate reactions to touchpoints, and can be built by prompt augmentation, retrieval-augmented generation, or finetuning; but synthetic users tend to fail at capturing the messy, nuanced nature of real human behaviour, and consent, misrepresentation, bias amplification and fairness remain unresolved [[2025-08-01_digital-twins]]. The year-in-review makes the same point as guidance: use AI strategically in research rather than replacing real users with synthetic ones [[2025-12-12_top-articles-2025]].

### Effect on the design role

The AI shift is described as reshaping the job rather than ending it. Advances in AI are reversing the decade-long move toward specialisation and making generalists who span research, design, strategy and emerging technologies more valuable [[2025-12-12_top-articles-2025]]. On the Parlons Design side the same movement is named the "product builder": a hybrid profile that goes past the visual interface to understand application logic and databases and build a product end to end, with technical culture as the enabling skill [[2024-10-08_349_L_IA_dans_le_design_usages_concrets_et_avenir_avec_Romain_Dao]]. UX is not disappearing but must become less procedural, faster, closer to the field, and must balance business constraints alongside the end user; in that frame AI is simultaneously an accelerator of prototyping, research and data analysis, and a commoditised technology available to every company that the designer has to shape into genuine differentiation [[2025-09-16_395_L_UX_Design_est_mort_L_impact_de_l_IA_&_du_business_sur_le_métier_de_designer]]. Foundational craft does not go away: solid pattern knowledge and visual hierarchy principles are described as non-negotiable precisely because of AI [[2025-12-12_top-articles-2025]].
