# Case Study

## Definition

A case study is the detailed narrative of a design project — problem, the
designer's role, the approach taken, iterations, challenges, impact, and
learnings — used to demonstrate design thinking and process rather than final
designs [[2019-08-04_ux-design-portfolios]]. The sources treat it as the
building block of a portfolio and as the central artefact of hiring: a portfolio
is a curated set of three to five such narratives
[[2019-08-04_ux-design-portfolios]], and in French-language Product Design
hiring it also names the live interview exercise where a candidate presents a
project, or a time-boxed fictional brief designed to evaluate reasoning rather
than the finished deliverable [[2024-12-03_354_Les_6_tips_de_présentation_d_un_case_study_design_-_Guide_Product_Design]], [[2025-09-02_393_Case_Study_Design_Mes_3_pires_erreurs_à_ne_pas_reproduire...]].

The recurring claim across sources is that the polished screen is the least
interesting part. Hiring managers want the messy process and the research behind
the shiny result, not the final screenshots
[[2019-08-04_ux-design-portfolios]]; in a case-study interview every step of the
reasoning must be shared because that is what is being assessed
[[2025-09-02_393_Case_Study_Design_Mes_3_pires_erreurs_à_ne_pas_reproduire...]]. What follows from that is a warning about form: what counts is the
content, not the visual style of the site that carries it
[[2024-08-06_340_Portfolio_L_outil_parfait_pour_les_designers,_PO,_PM_et_User_Researchers]], and it is the final value that matters, not the document
presenting it [[2024-12-03_354_Les_6_tips_de_présentation_d_un_case_study_design_-_Guide_Product_Design]].

## Practice

### What a case study contains

The structural checklist [[2019-08-04_ux-design-portfolios]]: the problem or
hypothesis; the designer's specific role and who they collaborated with; the
solution approach; how the solution actually solved the problem; the challenges
and iterations along the way; the business and user impact; and the learnings.
Visuals — sketches, wireframes, photos — carry the story. Research, ideation,
prototyping, and testing iterations belong in it; final screenshots alone tell an
incomplete story.

The French sources agree on the ingredients but push against treating the
checklist as a template. A case study should reflect the reality and the unique
specifics of the project rather than apply a rigid format [[2024-08-06_340_Portfolio_L_outil_parfait_pour_les_designers,_PO,_PM_et_User_Researchers]], and a
standardised design process presented "by the book" is precisely what to avoid:
better to highlight the specific methods used to adapt to that project's context
[[2024-12-03_354_Les_6_tips_de_présentation_d_un_case_study_design_-_Guide_Product_Design]]. The same source treats the Double Diamond as a model whose strict
presentation should be avoided in favour of the adaptations actually made, and
another notes that in real projects the method never follows a perfect path
[[2024-08-06_340_Portfolio_L_outil_parfait_pour_les_designers,_PO,_PM_et_User_Researchers]].

### Telling it as a story

Frame the project as a narrative with its reversals, failures, and successes —
that is what makes the presentation unique and holds the interviewer's attention
[[2024-12-03_354_Les_6_tips_de_présentation_d_un_case_study_design_-_Guide_Product_Design]]. The source's own formulation is that a case study is like a good
story: it leans on a shared frame, but the process has its own reversals in a
universe of its own. Concretely, this means showing iterations — the successive
stages and versions of the design, briefly — to explain the reasoning and show
how testing led to the final solution [[2024-12-03_354_Les_6_tips_de_présentation_d_un_case_study_design_-_Guide_Product_Design]].

### Connecting design decisions to business

Rather than justifying a choice by aesthetic preference, tie it to concrete,
quantified problems such as raising a conversion rate [[2024-12-03_354_Les_6_tips_de_présentation_d_un_case_study_design_-_Guide_Product_Design]]. The
presentation should not stop at the mockup: share the real-world results,
measured through data, NPS, or qualitative feedback [[2024-12-03_354_Les_6_tips_de_présentation_d_un_case_study_design_-_Guide_Product_Design]]. The
portfolio-level equivalent is stating business and user impact explicitly
[[2019-08-04_ux-design-portfolios]].

This is also where an open brief goes wrong. Given a free subject at BeReal, the
author spent his time on craft and the animation of a philosophically
interesting interaction instead of a topic serving the company's growth
objectives; the lesson drawn is that even a free subject must serve the
company's strategy and be defensible in presentation [[2025-09-02_393_Case_Study_Design_Mes_3_pires_erreurs_à_ne_pas_reproduire...]].

### Research: select, don't dump

Rather than presenting every deliverable produced — personas, user journeys — it
is better to surface the key findings that directly shaped the thinking and the
solution [[2024-12-03_354_Les_6_tips_de_présentation_d_un_case_study_design_-_Guide_Product_Design]]. The complementary failure is omitting context altogether:
in a case study for Penny Lane, personas and the wider context had genuinely
informed the work but were never stated out loud, and the lesson is to always
restate the company context and the end users to make the approach visible
[[2025-09-02_393_Case_Study_Design_Mes_3_pires_erreurs_à_ne_pas_reproduire...]].

### Curating a portfolio of case studies

Choose three to five detailed case studies over many shallow ones, prioritising
projects that show varied UX skills and match the responsibilities of the jobs
being targeted; start by taking inventory of projects aligned with the career
direction wanted [[2019-08-04_ux-design-portfolios]]. Portfolios must serve
several audiences at once — hiring managers, recruiters, fellow professionals —
and be scannable, since hiring managers rarely read word for word. Format is a
choice between web-based (most common and most findable), PDF or slide decks
(easier to tailor to a specific job), and physical artefacts (useful for
in-interview discussion). Then get feedback, iterate, and keep process documents
for future case studies [[2019-08-04_ux-design-portfolios]]. Documented
roadblocks: NDAs (show the process, redact, or remake designs generically), lack
of time (favour projects covering varied skills), and student status (internship
projects with realistic constraints count).

On tooling, the French corpus is more opinionated: the criteria are format
flexibility, easy updates, one-click sharing, and confidentiality management,
which leads to a recommendation of Notion — quick to produce structured content,
highly maintainable, and visually plain in a way that puts content and visuals
first [[2024-08-06_340_Portfolio_L_outil_parfait_pour_les_designers,_PO,_PM_et_User_Researchers]]. The underlying argument is maintenance risk: an overly
aesthetic portfolio is hard to keep up and falls into disuse, and the real danger
is spending six months rebuilding a portfolio every year [[2024-08-06_340_Portfolio_L_outil_parfait_pour_les_designers,_PO,_PM_et_User_Researchers]].

### Archiving material before it is needed

Attempting an exhaustive case study for every project is called the direct route
to failure in 95% of cases; the higher the bar set, the less likely it is to be
sustained over time [[2024-08-20_342_Votre_archive_design_grâce_au_brag_document_-_Tuto_étape_par_étape]]. The alternative offered is a Brag Document — a
personal archive of projects, successes, and failures — built on four rules:
pick a format (graphic, textual, audio) matching existing habits and tools, since
that is what makes the practice stick; use an extremely minimal template
(context, actions, links to mockups) to reduce friction; update it on a regular
rhythm, end of day or end of week via a reminder or to-do item, so details are
captured while memory is fresh; and keep it private, which removes the pressure
to make it good and allows it to merely be useful, with clean formatting deferred
until there is a real need to present [[2024-08-20_342_Votre_archive_design_grâce_au_brag_document_-_Tuto_étape_par_étape]]. It pays off for interviews,
conference proposals, and performance reviews. This sits alongside the
portfolio advice to save process documents for future case studies
[[2019-08-04_ux-design-portfolios]], though the two differ in emphasis: one
describes the artefact to produce, the other insists the archive must stay
deliberately cheap or it will not exist at all.

### The interview exercise itself

The case-study exercise in recruitment is fictional and time-constrained, and
aims to evaluate a designer's reasoning and process rather than the finished
output [[2025-09-02_393_Case_Study_Design_Mes_3_pires_erreurs_à_ne_pas_reproduire...]]. The goal in presenting is to show how one reacts to each
problem in a personalised way, not that one can apply a process by the book
without understanding it [[2024-12-03_354_Les_6_tips_de_présentation_d_un_case_study_design_-_Guide_Product_Design]]. One further lesson concerns the
conditions rather than the content: accepting an interview at 22:00 across time
zones, tired and less fluent in English, out of haste, is a mistake — there is
never a rush of two or three days, and it is better to be demanding about the
slot and present in good conditions [[2025-09-02_393_Case_Study_Design_Mes_3_pires_erreurs_à_ne_pas_reproduire...]].
