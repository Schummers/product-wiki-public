# User Interviews

## Definition

A user interview is a generative research method: a structured conversation that
produces knowledge about users' thoughts, beliefs, mental models, and
experiences [[2019-06-09_why-user-interviews-fail]]. Its focus is
nonjudgmentally and objectively gathering information about user needs,
expectations, and interactions with a product or a class of products
[[2021-06-06_interview-facilitation-mistakes]]. What it cannot do is establish
behaviour: the research question dictates the method, and questions about whether
users can use a design call for watching them, not asking them
[[2019-06-09_why-user-interviews-fail]].

*Continuous Discovery Habits* gives the interview a specific job: it is how a
product team discovers opportunities, the customer needs, pain points, and
desires that its discovery work will target, and it is meant to run every week
rather than as a study [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_08-chapter-five-continuous-interviewing]]. The book meets the same limit as the sources
above from the side of cognitive psychology: direct questions about behaviour
yield unreliable data because people rationalize their actions after the fact,
so the reliable form is story-based, asking for a specific recent experience
rather than a generalization [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_08-chapter-five-continuous-interviewing]].

The sources gathered here use "interview" in three related senses. The dominant
one is the research interview with end users. A second is the stakeholder
interview, a structured conversation with people who have a vested interest in
the project, aimed at business goals, constraints, and organizational history
rather than end-user behaviour [[2022-10-23_stakeholder-interviews]],
[[2022-10-02_stakeholders-study-guide]]. A third is the job interview, where the
designer is the one being questioned
[[2022-12-04_answer-ux-job-interview-questions]],
[[2025-01-24_ux-job-seeker-resources]]. The craft overlaps — open questions,
concrete stories, structured preparation — but the goals differ, and the practice
below is grouped accordingly.

## Practice

### Match the method to the question, before anything else

The most consequential failure is using an interview for a question it cannot
answer, such as colour preference or purchase intent; those need observational
methods or usability testing [[2019-06-09_why-user-interviews-fail]]. The same
caution appears in the Critical Incident Technique write-up, which states plainly
that observational research such as contextual inquiry or usability testing will
always teach you more about how people use existing interfaces and where their
pain points are [[2020-01-26_critical-incident-technique]]. Interviews earn their
place when the goal is thoughts, impressions, and perceptions of an experience
[[2019-06-09_why-user-interviews-fail]].

### Plan, and bring stakeholders in early

Unplanned interviews meander and yield superficial findings. A guide should hold
a few well-constructed open-ended questions, sessions should be piloted, and they
should be spaced across the week rather than stacked, to keep quality up
[[2019-06-09_why-user-interviews-fail]]. Stakeholder skepticism is treated as a
research-design problem rather than a communication one: stakeholders often do
not understand the differences between methods and will dismiss interview
findings, so educating them and involving them in prioritising the research
creates ownership and makes the findings likelier to be accepted
[[2019-06-09_why-user-interviews-fail]], [[2022-10-02_stakeholders-study-guide]].

### Structure the guide as a funnel

Start broad and narrow gradually: open-ended main questions first, then
open-ended probes, then closed questions, repeating the funnel for each topic,
with roughly five to eight broad main questions in a guide
[[2022-07-24_the-funnel-technique-in-qualitative-user-research]]. The reason is
bias: specific questions asked too early prime participants and cost you the
chance to see what they volunteer on their own. The same shape applies to
follow-ups, which should move from general reactions to specific UI elements so
that honest, unsolicited feedback comes before invented answers
[[2022-07-24_the-funnel-technique-in-qualitative-user-research]].

### Craft the individual questions

Six recurring mistakes are named [[2022-03-06_interview-questions-mistakes]]:
opening with closed questions that belong in a screener survey, which blocks
stories and prevents you from learning what you did not think to ask; asking
about "typical" behaviour, since people's sense of typical diverges from what
they actually do, so ask for specific examples instead; hypothetical questions,
because people predict their own future behaviour badly while answering
confidently; leading questions built on "because", replaced by neutral prompts
such as "What made you decide...?"; compound questions that exceed working
memory and should be split; and ambiguous, over-broad questions that participants
reinterpret. The article's framing is that questions fit for a questionnaire are
often unfit for an interview.

*UX Research* frames the same craft as a structural discipline. A good
question has a setup (what, why, how, when, or where), an area of inquiry,
laddering — a follow-up probe that asks a participant to explain the
rationale behind a response, reaching deeper and potentially more impactful
stories — and a segue into the next question, all built to guard against four
pitfalls: leading questions that inadvertently suggest an answer, shallow
yes/no questions that give participants an easy out, personal bias that lets
the researcher's own beliefs slip into a question, and unconscious bias
reflecting social norms the researcher is not aware of holding
[[2016-11-04_ux-research_03-chapter-2-good-research-starts-with-good-questions]].
Once a researcher is practised, the book allows deliberately breaking these
rules — a leading question to build trust or validate a comment, a shallow
question to ease a participant into comfort, personal bias to spark
discussion through devil's advocacy — while still holding the same check as
the sources above: every question should tie back to the research goals, and
should be validated through dry runs with coworkers before it reaches an
actual participant
[[2016-11-04_ux-research_03-chapter-2-good-research-starts-with-good-questions]].

The book reaches the same two prohibitions on "typical" and hypothetical
questions by a different route. People are poor at answering factual questions
about their own behaviour: they cannot fully recall why they acted and construct
a coherent but unreliable narrative instead, which the book attributes to the
"left brain interpreter" [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_08-chapter-five-continuous-interviewing]]. Asking why someone bought jeans therefore
returns an idealized story, not the actual decision drivers; the replacement
is a request for one instance, "Tell me about the last time you purchased a
pair of jeans", rather than "What criteria do you use when purchasing a pair
of jeans?" [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_08-chapter-five-continuous-interviewing]].

### Story-based interviewing

Where the Critical Incident Technique asks for an impactful incident, the book
asks for the most recent one, and then excavates it. A story is treated as
having a beginning, a middle, and an end, with supporting characters and
obstacles, and the interviewer walks the customer through it with temporal
prompts such as "What happened first?", "What happened next?", and "Who was
with you?" [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_08-chapter-five-continuous-interviewing]]. The team's experience map (its drawn picture of how customers
go through the process) serves as the guide during the interview: it tracks
the customer's journey, identifies the nodes and steps in their story, and lets
the team compare that individual story against the generalized map [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_08-chapter-five-continuous-interviewing]]. What
the interview is looking for is opportunities, needs, pain points and desires,
which the book keeps distinct from feature requests or solutions [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_08-chapter-five-continuous-interviewing]].

The Critical Incident Technique offers a scripted alternative to the generic
example question: ask participants to recall a specific time when a behaviour,
action, or occurrence had a positive or negative impact on a given outcome
[[2020-01-26_critical-incident-technique]]. Positive and negative incidents are
usually asked separately, typically starting with the positive to begin
constructively. CIT surfaces significant and rare events that observation would
miss and can reach back years, but it depends on fallible memory, can be
stressful to recall, and skews toward extreme rather than typical usage, so small
everyday usability issues rarely come up
[[2020-01-26_critical-incident-technique]].

### Facilitate without contaminating the data

Rapport first, but bounded: introduce yourself, ask about the participant's day,
speak slowly, call it a chat rather than an interview, and do not share your own
experiences, since dissimilar experiences make participants withdraw and skew
their answers [[2021-06-06_interview-facilitation-mistakes]]. Participants mirror
the researcher, so a slow pace calms them and gives them time to think; the
target is roughly 80% of the talking done by the participant.

Probe deliberately. "How does that make you feel?", "Why?", "Tell me more?" are
what uncover motivation and rationale; without them the data stays shallow, so
prepare optional follow-ups and keep a bank of generic probes
[[2021-06-06_interview-facilitation-mistakes]]. Probes should still avoid
leading, using forms like "Can you expand on that?" or "Why do you think that?"
[[2022-07-24_the-funnel-technique-in-qualitative-user-research]].

Use silence as a tool rather than a gap to fill. English speakers grow
uncomfortable after about four seconds, but the counsel is to count to seven
before speaking: the first four seconds feel awkward and the next three are where
genuine thinking happens, and it is in periods of silence that participants offer
the most poignant information [[2019-05-05_intentional-silence-ux]]. Do not
finish participants' sentences or jump to the next question, because a first
response is often just thinking aloud. Tolerance for silence is cultural — the
source contrasts about four seconds for English speakers with 8.2 for Japanese
speakers, and notes Finnish speakers also value silence — so facilitators should
adapt [[2019-05-05_intentional-silence-ux]].

Stop taking notes during the session. Recording and transcribing preserves the
data and gives the whole team access to it; live note-taking diverts attention and
signals selective interest, which is itself a priming cue. If recording is
impossible, assign a dedicated notetaker who is not the facilitator
[[2021-06-06_interview-facilitation-mistakes]].

Control the room. Observers turn a conversation into an interrogation, so record
for later viewing instead, make clear only the facilitator asks questions, and
cap the room at three people [[2021-06-06_interview-facilitation-mistakes]].
Finally, keep the study purpose vague, start with broad open-ended questions, and
hold neutral body language: revealing the purpose, phrasing like "How important
is price?", and vigorous nodding all prime responses
[[2021-06-06_interview-facilitation-mistakes]].

### Session roles and structure

*UX Research* adds mechanics that sit upstream of any single question or
probe. Run sessions with a two-person minimum — a moderator guiding the
conversation and a note taker capturing observations — because actively
listening, formulating a response, and taking notes at the same time is very
difficult for one person; roles can rotate between sessions, and observers
should watch remotely or from behind glass, holding their questions until
afterward rather than interrupting
[[2016-11-04_ux-research_09-chapter-8-making-research-happen]]. Frame the
relationship as the participant teaching the researcher, rather than as
interviewer and interviewee, so the participant leads the conversation and
chooses what to share
[[2016-11-04_ux-research_09-chapter-8-making-research-happen]]. Structure
sessions from simpler, established tasks toward more challenging ones;
moderated sessions typically run 60–90 minutes and unmoderated ones 15–30.
Treat incomplete tasks as normal and say so at the outset — the product is
being evaluated, not the participant — and leave room after the defined tasks
for participants and observers to raise questions or share insights outside
the formal research goals
[[2016-11-04_ux-research_09-chapter-8-making-research-happen]]. As with
question design, dry runs with colleagues rather than participants are where
technology, flow, and resource timing get tested and the guide gets revised
before real sessions begin
[[2016-11-04_ux-research_09-chapter-8-making-research-happen]].

### Analyse the data, don't remember it

Interviews that are never analysed lose their insights to bias. Record,
transcribe, and analyse systematically as a team using highlighted transcripts
and thematic clustering; working from transcripts rather than patchy notes or
memory keeps you closer to the data for longer and makes unsupported leaps less
likely [[2019-06-09_why-user-interviews-fail]]. For stakeholder studies the
threshold is explicit: with five or fewer stakeholders informal analysis is
enough, beyond that use systematic thematic analysis
[[2022-10-23_stakeholder-interviews]].

The book adds a per-interview unit of synthesis, made necessary by cadence:
when interviewing never stops, there is no clear point at which to stop and
synthesize, so synthesis happens as you go [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_08-chapter-five-continuous-interviewing]]. Its tool is the interview
snapshot, a one-pager with a memorable quote, a photo of the customer, quick
facts, the opportunities identified, and a drawn experience map, which turns
copious notes into actionable insights, helps the team remember a specific
customer's story weeks or months later, and surfaces patterns across
interviews [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_08-chapter-five-continuous-interviewing]]. The two approaches are complementary rather than opposed:
the NN/g guidance describes the analysis of a bounded study from transcripts
[[2019-06-09_why-user-interviews-fail]], the book the running index of an
open-ended one [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_08-chapter-five-continuous-interviewing]].

### Interview weekly, and as a trio

The book's cadence is one interview a week, on the argument that a weekly habit
is easier to sustain than starting and stopping [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_08-chapter-five-continuous-interviewing]]. That only works if
recruiting costs no ongoing effort, so it is automated: in-product prompts,
customer-facing colleagues who pass customers along, or advisory boards [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_08-chapter-five-continuous-interviewing]].
The interviewing team is the product trio, product manager, designer, and
engineer together, because each role hears different salient details in the
same conversation and because concentrating the "voice of the customer" in a
single person is a power the book wants to avoid [[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_08-chapter-five-continuous-interviewing]]. Three people in the
room is within the cap the facilitation guidance sets
[[2021-06-06_interview-facilitation-mistakes]]; the book does not say which of
the three asks the questions.

### Improve as an interviewer

Interviewing is a skill built by practice: review your own recordings and get
feedback from a mentor to spot ineffective questions and habits
[[2019-06-09_why-user-interviews-fail]]. Tooling can automate part of that
self-review — Gong joins calls from the calendar, announces the recording itself
(removing the friction of asking permission), transcribes at good quality, and,
in the source's view its standout feature, scores the interviewer on metrics such
as talk time, monologues, and a patience score, which exposes biases you cannot
see from inside the conversation
[[2025-04-08_372_Gong_l’allié_IA_parfait_pour_vos_user_interviews]]. That source
is also explicit about where the value is not: producing a written summary is not
the point, ingesting the information and feeding it into the product is; and its
keyword search would be more useful for research if it became semantic
[[2025-04-08_372_Gong_l’allié_IA_parfait_pour_vos_user_interviews]].

### Stakeholder interviews

Run them early, at project kickoff or when a new stakeholder arrives, to surface
organizational history, prior solutions, business objectives, constraints, and
politics before they become obstacles
[[2022-10-23_stakeholder-interviews]], [[2022-10-02_stakeholders-study-guide]].
Set a clear goal for each interview — knowledge, buy-in, concerns, or success
metrics — rather than holding an unstructured conversation. Use a discussion
guide rather than a script, covering success metrics, priorities, history and
expertise, and process preferences, so the conversation can flow while coverage
is guaranteed. Conduct them one-on-one with active listening: neutral posture,
eye contact, note-taking, and acknowledgments such as "I see" and "That's
helpful", opening with a reassurance that there are no right or wrong answers and
that nothing will be attributed back
[[2022-10-23_stakeholder-interviews]]. Note that note-taking is recommended here
as a signal of listening, where the user-interview guidance treats live
note-taking as a bias risk to be replaced by recording
[[2021-06-06_interview-facilitation-mistakes]]. Beyond the interview, engagement
continues by having stakeholders observe usability sessions to build empathy, and
by translating findings into business impact and ROI rather than UX vocabulary
[[2022-10-02_stakeholders-study-guide]].

### Being interviewed: UX job interviews

Behavioural questions (used by 80% of UX teams) and situational questions (63%)
dominate, resting on behavioural consistency, the idea that past behaviour
predicts future performance [[2022-12-04_answer-ux-job-interview-questions]].
Structure behavioural answers with STAR — Situation, Tasks, Actions, Results —
keeping each part brief, specific, and quantifiable. Answer situational questions
with METEOR: Musing, Enquiring, Theorizing, Endeavoring, Outcomes, Recap. Tell
stories rather than stating facts, since story-based answers earn better hiring
recommendations while candidates use them only 23% of the time; and choose
episodes where you stumbled and learned, because self-awareness and progress read
better than unrealistic perfection. Preparation means mock interviews with
trusted colleagues, recorded and transcribed, then restructured into the
frameworks [[2022-12-04_answer-ux-job-interview-questions]]. Around the
interview itself, practise responses with the NN/g UX Job-Interview Coach, send
thank-you notes as both a signal of attention to detail and a second chance to
restate your qualifications, and request feedback after a rejection even though it
is rarely granted [[2025-01-24_ux-job-seeker-resources]]. Templates should stay
authentic: use generative AI only as a proofreader or light editor of a message
you drafted, since machine-generated outreach has become ubiquitous and is
perceived less favourably [[2025-01-24_ux-job-seeker-resources]].
