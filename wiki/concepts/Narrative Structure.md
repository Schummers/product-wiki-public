## Sources (8)

- [[2019-12-17_storytelling-in-design_03-chapter-2-the-anatomy-of-a-great-story]] — Dahlström establishes narrative structure as fundamental to storytelling, and traces dramaturgy back to Gotthold Ephraim Lessing, who invented it in the 18th century as a practice-based discipline examining the context of a play. She presents both Aristotle's three-act model (setup, confrontation, resolution) and Freytag's five-act pyramid (exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, denouement) as proven frameworks that guide how stories unfold and how events logically follow from one another.
- [[2019-12-17_storytelling-in-design_04-chapter-3-storytelling-for-product-design]] — The chapter moves beyond the beginning, middle and end "just as Aristotle defined it" to show how on-demand, participative, and data-driven platforms are fragmenting the linear story. Product design must now handle branching narratives where users choose entry points and sequence.
- [[2019-12-17_storytelling-in-design_06-chapter-5-defining-and-structuring-experiences-with]] — Dahlström covers dramaturgy, Aristotle's three-act structure, and three major variations (Lavandier's, Field's Paradigm, Daniel's sequence paradigm); shows how story structure determines audience understanding and how these frameworks map to product life cycles and user journeys.
- [[2019-12-17_storytelling-in-design_10-chapter-9-visualizing-the-shape-of-your-product-experience]] — Dahlström argues that defining narrative structure early in the project (before wireframing or prototyping) helps teams think through emotional rhythm; she recommends five steps: brainstorm experience goals, identify patterns, formulate three to five overarching goals, define and map detailed experience statements, and review for emotional realism.
- [[2019-12-17_storytelling-in-design_11-chapter-10-applying-main-plots-and-subplots-to-user]] — Dahlström applies the main plot / subplot relation to user journeys: a subplot should have acts and carry the same narrative elements as the main plot (an inciting incident, a climax), and should impact the main plot, otherwise it does not belong in the story.
- [[2019-12-17_storytelling-in-design_12-chapter-11-theme-and-story-development-in-product-design]] — product teams should define the product story through plotting (Chapter 5) and theme, distinguishing between the idea, concept, premise, and theme, then building content strategy and information architecture to execute it coherently.
- [[2019-12-17_storytelling-in-design_13-chapter-12-choose-your-own-adventure-stories-and-modular]] — CYOA patterns (Time Cave, Gauntlet, Branch and Bottleneck, Quest, Open Map, Sorting Hat, Floating Modules, Loop and Grow) serve as templates for understanding how branching narratives work. Each pattern describes rules for how branches rejoin, how state affects progression, and what structure suits different story types.
- [[2019-12-17_storytelling-in-design_15-chapter-14-presenting-and-sharing-your-story]] — Dahlström reuses the methods the book applied to product experiences (the two-page synopsis and the index card method from Chapter 5) to structure a presentation, sketching an outline with plot points and a final act where everything comes together, and mapping a desired emotional response onto it as in Chapter 9.
