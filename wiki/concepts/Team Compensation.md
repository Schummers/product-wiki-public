# Team Compensation

## Definition

Team compensation is how a company pays the people who build it: the mix of
cash salary, equity, and the framing that surrounds both. Across the sources it
is treated less as a payroll question than as an alignment mechanism, because
the organisations described here cannot win on cash. A venture studio and an
early-stage startup both compete for talent against better-funded employers, so
the sources converge on the same structure: a salary high enough to make the job
liveable, plus an equity stake large enough to make the person behave like an
owner rather than an employee, plus explicit and transparent criteria so nobody
has to guess what they are worth.

The two works use the term for partly different objects, and the difference is
worth keeping. *Startup Studio Playbook* ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_11-ahead-of-time]],
[[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_18-core-team]]) is about a **studio**
compensating a core team that works across several ventures at once, which
raises questions a single startup never faces: what does a person own when they
contributed to five companies, and how do you pay a shared team from a studio
balance sheet with no product revenue. *The 10x Method*
([[2026-03-05_10x-method_03-choosing-your-cofounder]],
[[2026-03-05_10x-method_05-hiring-early-talent]]) is about a **single
early-stage startup** compensating its cofounders and its first ten hires: the
equity split between founders, and the ESOP that the first hires draw from.
Two of the Szigeti records folded into this concept
([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_19-a-preview-on-financial-modeling]],
[[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_20-fundraising-and-investor-relations]])
stretch it further still, to how the studio funds the payroll in the first
place — a financing question rather than a compensation one, though the two are
inseparable when the runway is what pays the team; see [[Studio Fundraising]]
and [[Studio Economics]].

## Practice

### Salary is the base, and it has to be good

- **Pay the core team well.** [[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_18-core-team]]
  states plainly that the base compensation of a studio core team is salary, and
  that it must be competitive enough to attract and retain people building
  multiple ventures at once.
- **Pay above the startups you compete with, if you are capital-poor.** Laicos
  set base salary above competing startups precisely to overcome its capital
  disadvantage in a secondary market
  ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_11-ahead-of-time]]). Salary is what
  attracts and retains people month to month; equity is what works over years.
- **The two works disagree on the level.** Szigeti's advice is to pay well;
  [[2026-03-05_10x-method_05-hiring-early-talent]] states the opposite as a
  fact of early-stage life — salaries rarely compete with scale-ups, so equity
  and genuine ownership have to carry the offer. The disagreement is partly one
  of object: a studio pays a core team from its own runway, while a pre-seed
  startup pays from a round it has barely raised.

### Equity is what turns employees into owners

- **Options change the psychology, not just the arithmetic.** At Laicos, the
  team receiving options in a symbolic "piece of the pie" ceremony shifted them
  from salary-only employment to co-ownership of shared upside
  ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_11-ahead-of-time]]). Without equity,
  competing with VC-backed rivals for top developers was impossible. See
  [[Equity Ownership]].
- **A studio can share the whole portfolio.**
  [[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_18-core-team]] suggests an equity pool
  giving every core team member a symbolic share in all portfolio companies, so
  the success of any single venture benefits everyone — the structural answer to
  a team that works across ventures rather than on one.
- **Ownership is not only equity.** The same record insists the studio's culture
  must generate ownership in team members *beyond* equity: safe experimentation,
  trust, and room to act. See [[Studio Culture]] and [[Studio Team]].
- **Story beats share count.**
  [[2026-03-05_10x-method_05-hiring-early-talent]] argues the story, the vision
  and genuine empowerment matter more to an early hire than the number of shares,
  while still requiring the grant to be meaningful.

### Concrete early-stage structure

[[2026-03-05_10x-method_05-hiring-early-talent]] is the only source that gives
numbers for a startup's plan:

- Allocate **5–10 % of the post-seed cap table** to the ESOP.
- Give the **first 5–10 hires outsized grants**, tied to performance rather than
  to salary alone. These hires are treated as cofounders without the title.
- Standard vesting: **4 years with a 1-year cliff**.
- Use a **simple salary grid** rather than à la carte negotiation, so pay is
  fair and consistent instead of a function of who negotiates hardest.
- Invest in **ESOP communication**: a well-explained plan is what lets an early
  hire understand the upside they are being offered.

See [[Early Hiring]].

### Founder equity splits

[[2026-03-05_10x-method_03-choosing-your-cofounder]] treats the split between
cofounders as a motivation and accountability question rather than a valuation
one. Equal splits tend to work better when cofounders join simultaneously and
contribute equally: they reinforce mutual accountability and signal equal
weight. Unequal splits can introduce subtle power dynamics in which one founder
comes to feel like an early hire rather than a true partner. The chapter pairs
the split with clear role definition and regular realignment rituals rather than
letting the cap table carry the whole relationship; see [[Operating Rituals]].

### Transparency and criteria

Both sides of the studio corpus insist the *method* matters as much as the
amount. Negri worked with an attorney to set objective criteria for option
grants — time at the company, skills, perceived future potential — which removed
ambiguity, prevented any perception of favouritism, and communicated to each
person how the company valued them
([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_11-ahead-of-time]]). The same chapter
notes the legal work this required: company valuation to set a strike price,
vesting schedules, exercise terms, and tax implications. The 10x Method reaches
the same place from the other direction with its salary grid: fairness by
published rule, not by negotiation
([[2026-03-05_10x-method_05-hiring-early-talent]]).

### Paying for it: where the money comes from

The two financial-modelling chapters fold into this concept because a studio's
compensation is bounded by its runway.

- **Model the burn, then the runway.**
  [[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_19-a-preview-on-financial-modeling]]
  frames the core calculation as monthly cash burn for the core team and
  infrastructure, multiplied by the time expected before the first major exit —
  which can take years. Regional wage expectations are one of the inputs the
  model needs before it can say anything. The chapter also covers projecting
  equity dilution across rounds and typical ESOP sizes.
- **Four funding routes for the payroll.** The same chapter lays out the studio
  founder's options: self-fund from personal wealth, raise from VCs, take agency
  revenue to subsidise ventures, or require portfolio companies to raise seed
  before the studio exits.
- **Self-funding buys freedom, and is rare.**
  [[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_20-fundraising-and-investor-relations]]
  observes that most early successful studios were founded by established
  entrepreneurs with deep pockets; self-funding the first 5–10 ventures removes
  external fundraising friction and lets the studio build traction before
  raising. Its limitation is that it presupposes the wealth.
- **Agency revenue has a cultural cost.** Laicos took client work to sustain
  cash flow and the team, who had signed on to build the studio's own products,
  lost motivation — a misalignment that ended in layoffs
  ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_11-ahead-of-time]]). The bootstrapping
  route pays the salaries and erodes the reason people accepted them. See
  [[Revenue Model]].
- **Charge portfolio companies below the cost of hiring.** If a studio bills its
  startups for shared resources, the rate must stay low enough that using the
  studio is cheaper than a full-time hire, yet high enough for the studio to
  sustain itself without investors seeing overcharging
  ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_19-a-preview-on-financial-modeling]]).
- **Investors watch the team's skin in the game.**
  [[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_20-fundraising-and-investor-relations]]
  reports that investors worry a studio taking majority equity early leaves the
  CEO and team under-motivated; the answer is to show post-seed cap table
  adjustments that raise team ownership. Compensation design is therefore also a
  fundraising argument. See [[Venture Studio]].
