# Design Presentation

## Definition

Design presentation is the act of putting design work in front of the people who
decide its fate, and doing it in a way that produces a decision. Three works in
this corpus cover it. Tom Greever's *Articulating Design Decisions* treats it as the
central professional skill: the meeting where stakeholders see the work is where
support is won or lost, and without that support "our designs will never see the
light of day" ([[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_01-preface]]). Artiom
Dashinsky's *Solving Product Design Exercises* covers the same act inside a hiring
process, where the presentation phase of a [[Design Exercise]] is what the
interviewer actually scores
([[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_16-how-to-present-your-solution]]). His later
*The Path to Senior Product Designer* treats it as a competency a designer grows
into on the job, and locates the shift in audience: designers learn to present to
other designers during their studies, then join a company and are "suddenly
required to present to people without a design background and, more importantly,
with completely different goals and objectives in mind"
([[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_13-11-communicating-design-communication]]).

All three start from the same premise: what gets presented is the reasoning, not the
artefact. Greever grounds this in the subjectivity of design, since every design
has legitimate alternatives, which makes it impossible to rely on the work
speaking for itself
([[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_02-chapter-1-great-designers-are-great-communicators]]);
Dashinsky states plainly that "the presentation should include all of the thinking
that led to the solution"
([[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_16-how-to-present-your-solution]]). Greever
goes further and argues that communication about the work is not adjacent to the
job but is the job, and that the difference between a good and a great designer is
the capacity to articulate a solution convincingly rather than the capacity to
find one.

## Practice

### Why articulation is the deciding variable

[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_02-chapter-1-great-designers-are-great-communicators]]
sets out the case: design is subjective, so multiple solutions exist for every
problem; stakeholders without design expertise will influence the project
regardless; and unchecked contribution turns into design-by-committee, groupthink
and artefacts like the "CEO button" or "home page syndrome". Being articulate,
defined as using words to yield the desired response, is what keeps the designer's
expertise central. The chapter names four things articulation signals to a
stakeholder: intelligence, intentionality, confidence and respect. It also gives
three tests every design must pass, presented as equally necessary: does it solve
a problem, is it easy for users, and is it supported by everyone on the team. The
prerequisite is conscious awareness, writing down the reasoning rather than
designing intuitively and reconstructing it later (see [[Design Rationale]]).

### Know who is in the room

[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_03-chapter-2-stakeholders-are-people-too]]
makes the audience the starting point: understanding stakeholders' priorities and
constraints lets a designer present in terms that appeal to their needs rather
than the designer's own. Roles value different things, executives vision and
reporting, developers quality and feasibility, product owners innovation and
simplicity, project managers schedule and scope, and that mapping shapes how to
address each one. The chapter also treats stakeholder reactions as human and often
driven by factors outside the project, and argues that the quality of the
relationship matters more than the words spoken in the meeting (see
[[Stakeholder Engagement]]).

[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_13-11-communicating-design-communication]]
draws the same map from the other book: product managers evaluate a design against
business metrics, ease of development and measurability; developers assess
implementation complexity; stakeholders and executives arrive with varied
objectives of their own; and external parties such as investors, clients and
vendors form yet another audience. Because different people respond to different
types of evidence, that chapter makes the audience the variable that decides which
argument to lead with.

### Design the meeting itself

[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_04-chapter-3-design-the-meeting]] applies
interface thinking to the meeting: remove [[Cognitive Load]] so stakeholders can
spend their attention on the design decision.

- **Set the context first.** State the goal, summarise previous decisions, show
  where you are in the process on a timeline, specify what feedback is needed at
  this stage, and restate the goal. The source notes this need not be long, a few
  sentences or a single slide, and for informal reviews a few bullet talking points.
- **Structure for memory.** Apply primacy and recency, repetition, surprise and
  mixed media; break content into distinct chunks with visual transitions rather
  than delivering one long presentation.
- **Remove distractions.** Placeholder content such as stock images or lorem ipsum,
  and visual clutter, can derail an entire conversation; the chapter argues for
  spending the extra time on real copy, aligned layouts and appropriate imagery,
  and states that the same distraction appearing in two consecutive meetings is a
  failure on the designer's part.
- **Anticipate the reactions.** Write down the objections you expect, prepare
  alternative designs that answer them, and gather supporting data; most people
  react predictably given their role and values.
- **Build a support network.** Include people who already agree and are ready to
  advocate, aligning with them one-on-one before the meeting.
- **Rehearse.** A printed agenda, speaking the content out loud, and a short team
  huddle beforehand reduce the designer's own cognitive load during the meeting.

[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_13-11-communicating-design-communication]]
offers a four-step presentation framework along the same lines, presented as
adaptable rather than fixed: explain why you are there and state your ask clearly;
give the project context, covering the problem, timeline, scope, readiness level,
constraints, blindspots and competitive analysis; present the solution; then
suggest next steps and reiterate the ask. For the solution itself it uses the Five
Ws and How, asking why the thing is being built, who it is for, when and where it
will be used, what should be built and how it can be measured, which it presents as
a way to cover the essentials without overwhelming the audience.

[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_16-14-stakeholder-management-collaboration]]
covers the discipline around the meeting rather than inside it: as a participant,
prepare by defining clear goals; as an organiser, avoid unnecessary meetings by
using what the chapter reports as Dropbox's approach of only convening for the
"three Ds" (important decisions, debates, discussions), establish clear ownership,
share the agenda and materials in advance, and end with assigned action items,
each with an owner and a deadline.

### Choose the message

[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_08-chapter-7-choose-a-message]] argues
that justifications should be reusable rather than reinvented, and sorts them into
four categories: Business (goal, use case, branding), Design (common pattern,
attention, flow), Research (data, testing, external research) and Limitations
(resources, technology, standards). Specific guidance from the chapter:

- "Helps achieve a goal" is among the most persuasive messages; the chapter gives
  the pattern "[design] will affect [goal] because [reason]".
- Distinguish existing data, useful for informed guesses, from reflective data,
  before-and-after metrics that prove effectiveness. Data interpretation carries
  assumptions, so avoid overinterpreting and show your reasoning.
- Stories beat statistics: user testing observations and video clips are often more
  compelling than spreadsheets because they create empathy and show that users
  informed the work (see [[Storytelling]]).
- Established patterns are cheaper and easier to defend than custom solutions, and
  consistency sets user expectations.
- Name resource, technology and standards limits early rather than letting them
  surface during implementation as apparent cutbacks.

The audience determines the choice: a business stakeholder may respond better to a
financial argument while a research-oriented one wants data or testing evidence.

[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_13-11-communicating-design-communication]]
attacks the same problem at the level of the sentence. Its test is to check whether
"I think/believe that…" can be replaced with "We know that…", "Data shows that…" or
"According to research…", and it lists the kinds of reasons it considers robust:
data and analytics, research, visual design principles, business objectives,
technical feasibility, project context, and ethical or legal considerations. The
chapter's claim is not only rhetorical, it argues that this sort of reasoning is
harder to disagree with and, in most cases, leads to better solutions, because
articulating reasoning challenges your own decisions, builds trust and removes
unnecessary feedback cycles.
[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_15-13-written-and-oral-communication-communication]]
applies the same move to qualifiers: instead of "a lot of users can't find X", say
"12% of users can't find X", on the grounds that checking the exact number gives
better context, appears more credible, and may change your own opinion if the
reality differs from the assumption. That chapter also recommends the Pyramid
Principle, which it attributes to former McKinsey consultant Barbara Minto, for
structuring a complex message: start with the key idea, the answer or conclusion,
group the supporting arguments, and provide evidence for each. It presents this
top-down approach as working for presentations, written communication and emails
alike.

### Respond to feedback

[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_07-chapter-6-form-a-response]] separates
strategy, what to say, from tactics, how to deliver it. The strategic moves are to
appeal to a nobler motive by connecting the decision to an agreed business goal,
metric or problem, which keeps the discussion out of subjective preference; to
represent the user with a real observation about a specific person rather than a
generic reference to "the user"; and to demonstrate effectiveness by showing
rather than describing, using visuals, prototypes or side-by-side comparisons.

The five tactics are: show a comparison, propose an alternative, give a choice
while highlighting what will be lost, ask others to weigh in, or postpone the
decision. They can be used alone or combined, and which one applies depends on the
situation. Feedback that falls outside the current scope should be reframed as a
future backlog item so the meeting stays on its stated goal. The chapter treats
fluency as a product of practice: memorise the strategy, improvise the tactics.

### Close the loop

[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_09-chapter-8-lock-in-agreement]] gathers
the preceding chapters into the IDEAL response: Identify the problem, Define the
solution, Empathize with the user, Appeal to the business, Lock in agreement. Its
distinctive claims:

- **Support is not consensus.** You will never get everyone to agree on every
  detail, but you can get agreement to move forward; that distinction lets
  stakeholders voice concerns while still committing.
- **Ask directly.** A simple "Do you agree?" compels a position, where open-ended
  discussion lets it drift; framing the question around benefits or risks pushes
  towards commitment and stops the decision being relitigated in the next meeting.
- **Without the L it is only an IDEA.** The final step is what turns the discussion
  into something actionable.
- **It is a checklist, not a script.** The formula exists to ensure completeness;
  internalised, the response should sound conversational.

The chapter closes with worked case studies, including phone-number collection,
overbranding, a cluttered main menu and custom widgets, showing the formula applied
to common tensions.

### Presenting in a hiring context

[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_16-how-to-present-your-solution]] gives
format-specific guidance:

- **Whiteboarding: narrate the process.** State each step you are taking and write
  the steps on the board as a prompt; if you are stuck on a step, explain the step
  aloud rather than going silent. Interviewer input during the exercise is normal
  and does not automatically reduce your chances.
- **Take-home: prototype plus written explanation.** Pair a clickable prototype
  with a written presentation covering the task, the problem definition, the
  audience, the context, the selected idea summarised in one or two sentences, the
  visual decisions and the success metrics.
- **State scope explicitly.** Say what the solution addresses, what it does not,
  and what you would do with more time.
- **Name your blind spots.** For critical assumptions, propose how you would
  validate them, through quick user research, surveys or testing services.
- **Position against the landscape.** Say whether the solution differentiates or
  catches up, and with a large company, how it could connect to the wider
  ecosystem.

[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_06-chapter-2-how-to-solve-a-product-design-exercise]]
adds two upstream points: clarify the presentation format and whether presenting
is even required before starting, alongside deliverable type and fidelity; and be
ready to discuss the pros and cons of your own solution, since being aware of
trade-offs demonstrates critical thinking and prevents defensive attachment to an
idea.

From the interviewer's side,
[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_23-chapter-4-how-to-use-a-design-exercise-when-interviewing]]
confirms what is being watched during both the solution and presentation phases:
clarity of communication, the ability to handle pushback and feedback, and the
willingness to explore ideas beyond the obvious. That chapter's advice to offer a
single deliberate pushback makes handling criticism an explicit part of the
presentation score.

### Presentation as a career-level skill

[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_27-helen-tran-the-skill-most-designers-overlook]]
names communication as one of the most overlooked skills in design careers and
insists it is as learnable as any deliverable: present research findings, lead
group critiques, run retrospectives, facilitate team alignment, and practise
through company speaking opportunities, Toastmasters groups, improv groups and
settings outside the tech scene. Its stated purpose for the skill is to convince
teams to build what serves both the business and the user.

[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_15-13-written-and-oral-communication-communication]]
names the recurring design critique meeting as the most convenient place to get
that practice, since it builds presentation skill, feedback skill, confidence and
oral communication at once (see [[Design Critique]]). A designer without such a
forum is advised to find a sparring partner and set up a mutual weekly critique,
following a simplified version of the presentation structure: explain why you
designed it, state what feedback you want, then walk through the solution. For the
mechanics of speaking, the chapter recommends recording yourself and reviewing the
recording to spot filler words, body language, pace and intonation, a method it
reports as endorsed by public speaking coaches and as showing rapid improvement;
it also points to AI tools such as Speeko for real-time feedback and to hiring an
affordable speaking coach through a platform like Fiverr.

[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_23-21-advocacy-community]] extends the
same skill outside the company, treating public speaking as one channel of
[[Design Advocacy]]. It describes a progression that typically runs from internal
events to small meetups to conferences, and distinguishes being scouted, which it
calls rare and dependent on an already established public presence, from submitting
through a call for papers, which it presents as the realistic route. Preparation
and coaching are what it says make the difference once the slot is obtained.

[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_25-bobby-ghoshal-how-designers-should-change-their-mindset]]
extends the audience beyond the product team: designers need to communicate
business strategy and build diplomatic relationships with marketing, finance,
operations and support, spending a large share of their time away from their desk
doing it.

[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_01-preface]] converges on the same point
from the other work: the author's early realisation was that stakeholders care
about what a design does rather than how it looks, and that the first question is
not a technical specification but "What are we trying to communicate?". The
preface's closing claim is that articulating design decisions is a skill that can
be practised, learned and made habitual (see [[UX Career]]).
