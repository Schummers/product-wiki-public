# Venture Studio

## Definition

A venture studio (also called a startup studio, venture builder, company builder, foundry or startup factory) is an organization that produces companies. Both books in the corpus insist on the same distinction: a studio is not an investor, a mentor or a programme, it is a co-founder and a builder. Szigeti's *Startup Studio Playbook* defines studios as organizations that "produce companies", acting as founders and builders rather than financial backers ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_02-a-place-for-pioneers-and-creators]]), and gives the recipe: assemble a core team and entrepreneurs in residence, add shared infrastructure and in-house funding, generate ideas internally or co-found with external teams, build several startups in parallel, discard what fails, spin off what succeeds ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_03-startup-studios-in-a-nutshell]]). Kannan and Peterman's *Venture Studios Demystified*, a Stanford GSB research project built on 19 interviews with studio leaders, describes the same object from the outside: fully-equipped teams of builders that develop venture ideas internally, test them, build early MVPs, recruit the founding team and provide the first capital, in exchange for a founding equity stake priced at near zero at issuance ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_02-part-1-venture-studio-basics]]). What separates a studio from an accelerator, an incubator or a VC fund is the stage at which it enters (ideation, before a company exists), the duration of its support (across the whole lifecycle, not a 3-6 month programme) and the depth of its operational involvement ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_03-comparison-to-other-models]]).

The underlying bet, stated explicitly by *Demystified*, is that building successful startups can be systematized: if 90% of startups fail, a studio is the hypothesis that a repeatable process can lift the success rate above that baseline ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_01-introduction]]). The *Playbook* frames the same bet as a factory: "Startups are the product, studios are the factories" ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_14-studio-leadership]]). The two books differ in vantage point. Szigeti writes a practitioner's playbook and hands most of the chapters to guest founders (Betaworks, TechnoSpark, eFounders, Lab.Coop, Midealab, Backspace, Laicos, Innonic, Drukka), so his book records what particular studios did, often from bootstrapped positions in secondary markets. Kannan and Peterman interview 19 mostly US, independent, tech-focused studios and build a financial model, so their book records distributions, thresholds and industry-level dynamics. Where the two give different numbers, this page keeps both.

## Practice

### What a studio does that other models do not

*Demystified* positions studios against three neighbours ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_03-comparison-to-other-models]]):

- **Accelerators** (Y Combinator, Techstars) accept existing startups, run a 3-6 month programme, take 5-10% equity and play a volume game (YC's summer 2021 batch was 377 companies). Their zone is validation to customer acquisition.
- **Incubators** work with earlier-stage companies than accelerators, offer flexible, unstructured support, do not generate ideas in-house, and take 5-15%.
- **Venture capital** is the furthest away: financiers who provide capital and ad-hoc advice through their network, and never take on the role or responsibilities of a co-founder.

A studio covers the whole journey from ideation through customer acquisition, keeps supporting companies after launch, and holds substantially higher founding equity because it built the company from the ground floor. The *Playbook* draws the same line from the inside: studios are co-founders and builders, not advisors or funders; the studio explores markets, creates ideas and builds them into products and companies ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_03-startup-studios-in-a-nutshell]]). Szigeti's foreword (by Michael Jones of Science Inc.) adds a ternary mission: originate ideas and concepts, invest and advise, and acquire properties to redistribute resources and scale them ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_01-foreword]]).

Szigeti also describes hybrid uses of the model: an agency dedicating part of its business to internal startups, or an accelerator adding a studio component to solve its "demo-day problem" by supporting companies from ideation through exit rather than only during training ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_04-are-startup-studios-for-me]]).

### Why the model exists

Both books justify the studio through the failure economics of traditional startups.

- **Failure becomes recoverable.** In a traditional startup, when the company fails everything is lost: the team disperses and its collective knowledge disappears. A studio running several startups in parallel treats failure as an expected outcome; the team and its expertise are reassigned to another concept and the talent pool is retained ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_03-startup-studios-in-a-nutshell]], [[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_04-are-startup-studios-for-me]]). Szigeti describes a well-designed studio as antifragile: it reinforces its capabilities after each attempt and emerges stronger ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_02-a-place-for-pioneers-and-creators]]).
- **Validation before commitment.** Studios use Design Thinking and Lean Startup methods to let founders prove viability before committing capital and personal resources ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_04-are-startup-studios-for-me]]). *Demystified* says the same: processes are designed to reduce the probability of failure by evaluating product-market fit upfront via user research and by placing stage gates that stop bad ideas from advancing ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_02-part-1-venture-studio-basics]]).
- **Talent allocation.** Centralized teams can be moved across startups as needs evolve; a studio can afford "a big gun" that no single early startup could, and distribute that person's skills across the portfolio ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_03-startup-studios-in-a-nutshell]]).
- **Access to entrepreneurship.** Studios motivate people who would not otherwise launch a company: those with no idea they believe in enough to take the risk, or those who cannot afford to go without a salary for one or two years ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_03-startup-studios-in-a-nutshell]]). *Demystified* frames this as an ecosystem effect: upfront funding lets entrepreneurs without personal capital participate, and successful studio founders later become VCs, serial founders or mentors ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_02-part-1-venture-studio-basics]]).
- **Speed.** Faster venture creation and iteration is presented as a competitive edge in itself ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_01-foreword]], [[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_02-a-place-for-pioneers-and-creators]]).

Szigeti names three drivers of the model's growth: post-exit entrepreneurs who prefer owning a studio to pure investing, corporations that want something better than "hollow accelerator programs", and later-stage VCs who suspect that building their own deal flow beats a 90% failure rate ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_03-startup-studios-in-a-nutshell]]).

### Who founds a studio

The two books converge on founder origins, with slightly different taxonomies.

The *Playbook* identifies three founding models: successful serial entrepreneurs launching their own studio (Idealab, Betaworks, Science, eFounders), an entire startup team converting itself into a studio (Lab.Coop), and corporations or institutional investors creating one (AXA's Kamet Ventures). Most successful studio founders are entrepreneurs who already made their fortune, want hands-on impact beyond investing, and whose network and market expertise often define the studio's sector focus ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_14-studio-leadership]]).

*Demystified* finds independent studios originating from three backgrounds, each with a characteristic weakness ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_04-studio-formation]]):

| Founder background | Strength | Weakness | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Serial entrepreneurs (most common) | Ideation, founder recruiting, startup operations | Asset management, fundraising for an associated fund | Expa, High Alpha |
| VC investors | Market knowledge, pattern recognition, asset management, fundraising | Less hands-on experience with early ideation and idea funnels | Pioneer Square Labs, Sutter Hill Ventures |
| Product / engineering agencies | Technical depth, rapid MVP building, testing | Market validation, founder recruiting, scaling beyond the build | Coplex, Revelry |

The authors recommend that new studio partnerships draw from all three areas, because venture development spans ideation, market validation, technical development, recruiting, asset management and fundraising, and no single profile brings all of it. Several *Playbook* case studies are the agency path in practice: Innonic grew out of a web design agency ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_12-changing-ecommerce-one-startup-at-a-time]]), Drukka out of a software and online marketing agency ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_13-stretching-our-limits]]), and Lab.Coop out of a failed startup's team that chose to stay together ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_08-building-a-100-co-owned-venture-builder]]).

### Where the ideas come from

*Demystified* stresses that a studio's idea supply is its own constraint: it can only draw ideas from its own people, unlike a VC that sources deals globally, and it needs roughly 30-100 top-level ideas to launch one company ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_07-industry-focus]], [[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_06-process]]). This explains an unexpected finding: only about 50% of the interviewed studios specialize, the other half stay industry agnostic, because a narrow focus can exhaust opportunities. Focus follows founder expertise; among specialized studios B2B SaaS is the most common choice (4 of 19), for its recurring revenue and playbook-like scaling. The authors expect the agnostic share to grow as competition for idea sources rises ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_07-industry-focus]]).

The *Playbook* shows the mechanics of idea generation at three studios:

- **eFounders** holds monthly "Ideation Breakfasts" where team members pitch, tracks trends with Feedly and Slack rooms, and produces about 20 ideas a year, of which a two-month validation (market analysis, technical analysis, prototype testing) keeps 4 ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_07-game-plan]]).
- **Midealab** ideates from megatrends: structural shifts in behaviour or technology that create years of demand, richest where two or more collide (mobile data plus social media gives mobile video). It segments adoption into innovation, growth and saturation phases and deliberately enters in the growth phase, where demand is predictable and competition not yet entrenched. Ideas are filtered for implementability, favouring "operations-light" models that scale with capital and engineering rather than headcount. Discovery runs as weekend hackathons that generate ideas, reveal talent fit and provide early validation at once ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_09-building-startups-from-megatrends]]).
- **Innonic** collects ideas continuously from customer conversations, conferences and reading into a shared spreadsheet, then has a Validation Team evaluate them with several models, with no single "golden standard" ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_12-changing-ecommerce-one-startup-at-a-time]]).

Midealab's geography is itself an ideation tool: offices in Lviv, Helsinki and Tallinn give access to different talent pools and to megatrends at different phases, mobile apps saturating in developed markets while still growing in developing ones ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_09-building-startups-from-megatrends]]).

### Validation, gates and the funnel

*Demystified* finds that studios broadly follow the same three-step process built on design thinking: (1) brainstorming and early idea development, (2) customer validation through user research and marketing tests (click-through rates, customer LOIs), (3) building and testing early prototypes. Many ideas are killed in the earliest research stages and advanced ideas are scrutinized against short evaluation windows; stage gates with explicit criteria (LOIs, click-through targets, investment committee review) decide whether an idea advances or gets more funding. Funnel efficiency varies widely, from 30 to 107 ideas per launched company ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_06-process]]). The authors note that studios share these fundamental processes for ideation, company building and founder recruitment, and that it is unclear whether process nuances drive differentiated returns ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_23-implications-for-stakeholders]]).

The *Playbook* case studies put cost and time figures on those stages:

- **Innonic** runs a five-stage funnel: continuous ideation; research-based pre-validation (2-4 weeks, a couple of thousand dollars, customer interviews and landing-page conversion tests); validation with a dedicated CEO (3-6 months, EUR 20,000); formation with EUR 200,000 of capital (18 months); growth toward independence or VC funding ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_12-changing-ecommerce-one-startup-at-a-time]]).
- **Drukka** started with EUR 100,000 and allocated 10,000-20,000 USD per initiative, which forced it to begin with low-risk copycat sharing-economy ideas. Each incoming CEO develops 3-5 bare-minimum concept validations (an offer, no product) in parallel with investor market testing, filtering down to 1-2 viable ideas ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_13-stretching-our-limits]]).
- **eFounders** confronts an MVP with 25 pilot users daily during its Build phase and requires product-market fit before the Scale phase ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_07-game-plan]]).

Two *Playbook* studios reject the bare MVP. Midealab builds a "minimum loveable product" that works, not merely a viable one ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_09-building-startups-from-megatrends]]); Innonic builds a "Minimal Awesome Product" because users no longer tolerate buggy first versions and a product must be awesome in at least one way, either solving a painful problem uniquely or being far more user-friendly than competitors ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_12-changing-ecommerce-one-startup-at-a-time]]).

### The platform: team, processes, infrastructure

Szigeti's core claim is that the studio's value lies in its platform and people, and that this platform is the moat. eFounders is the fullest example: a core team of 11+ specialists across creative, tech, product, marketing, sales and admin; a "Game Plan" of 166 defined processes spread over 18 months; internal tools (ContactX for contacts, "The Books" as knowledge base, Trello); a 6,000-contact network for pilots, experts and investors; and communities (SaaS Club, Build Together) that serve as beta-test pools. Each asset supports several ventures at once, lowering per-company cost, and team members are asked to write down their knowledge for the next generation ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_07-game-plan]]). Betaworks similarly keeps a strong core team in data science, design, branding, distribution and product, handles back-office, fundraising coordination and administration so that a product-focused founder CEO can keep coding, and runs a Hackers-in-Residence programme where technologists explore ideas without a clear initial product vision ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_05-emerging-from-beta]]). Backspace assesses every venture, and itself, against three pillars that must all be filled from day one with no concessions: technology, design and traction ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_10-culture-is-a-verb]]).

*Demystified* records a later shift that runs against the eFounders picture. Early studio designs kept full engineering, product and design teams in-house; by 2022 many studios were outsourcing development to third-party agencies (often partner agencies at discounted rates) because personnel costs exceeded what management fees could support, and because moving engineers between the studio and its ventures proved hard in practice (studio engineers leave to join a venture, or the venture is left with a technical gap). Several studios slimmed down to core teams focused on ideation, recruiting and administration (legal, HR, finance), which is where they now locate their unique value. Outsourced work is paid from the operating budget before a venture exists, from the venture's allocated capital afterwards, and sometimes billed back to the venture once incorporated ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_09-third-party-agencies]]). The two books therefore disagree on whether the in-house build team is the platform's core or a cost to shed: the *Playbook* (2019) presents in-house multidisciplinary execution as the competitive advantage, *Demystified* (2022) documents studios concluding the opposite for early-stage concepts.

Szigeti also documents the resource conflict this platform creates: parallel startups compete for the same people, and when one venture approaches a breakthrough the leaders must make go/no-go decisions that reprioritize the portfolio ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_03-startup-studios-in-a-nutshell]], [[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_14-studio-leadership]]). Drukka's answer was batching: grouping startups by industry, running joint mentoring sessions for related CEOs and reusing code bases, which let it move from 6-7 concurrent ventures to a dozen a year ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_13-stretching-our-limits]]).

### Cadence, rituals and the road to independence

Studios in both books describe a defined path from idea to an independent company, with different clocks.

- **eFounders**: Build (months 1-9) then Scale (months 9-18), organized by function; ventures become 100% operationally independent after 18 months and enter Growth (Aircall is the given example). Momentum is kept by weekly Monday Kick-Off Meetings (30 minutes), weekly Friday Product Committees (1 hour), monthly Sunday emails from CEOs and quarterly Demo Days with public goal-setting; the rituals cost time but prevent misalignment ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_07-game-plan]]).
- **Innonic**: 18 months of formation before growth ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_12-changing-ecommerce-one-startup-at-a-time]]).
- **Drukka**: seed rounds for portfolio companies within 18-24 months, with the studio staying active post-spin-off ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_13-stretching-our-limits]]).
- **Betaworks**: a "flex-to-growth" model, funding internally at low cost until traction, then helping the team raise externally and spin off with continued support ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_05-emerging-from-beta]]).
- **TechnoSpark** (hardware): a conveyor where startups pass defined stages and exit at various maturity points, tracked through 7-9 year cycles ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_06-technospark-a-conveyor-of-hardware-startups]]).
- **Midealab**: discovery, build and growth phases, with seed funding secured before growth so the venture can focus on execution ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_09-building-startups-from-megatrends]]).

*Demystified* gives the distribution: studios typically need 6-18 months to Seed and another 12 months to Series A, in line with a GSSN average of 11 and 15 months; 73% of studios aim to launch at most 4 companies a year, 13% target 4-6 and 13% launch 6-12 ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_06-process]]). The *Playbook* cases sit at both ends of that range: eFounders keeps 4 projects a year ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_07-game-plan]]), Innonic launches 2-3 ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_12-changing-ecommerce-one-startup-at-a-time]]), while Drukka's mantra is "build startups by the dozen" ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_13-stretching-our-limits]]) and TechnoSpark launches 10-15 companies a year ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_06-technospark-a-conveyor-of-hardware-startups]]).

### Founders and equity

Equity is where the two books diverge most, partly because they measure different things and partly because of their dates.

The *Playbook* reports studio stakes that are often majority:

- Studios take larger stakes than VCs, often exceeding 50%, which produces substantially larger returns at exit; Szigeti acknowledges this raises "capitalization table skepticism" and questions about founder motivation and authenticity ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_03-startup-studios-in-a-nutshell]]).
- Betaworks typically splits 50-50 between studio and team, reflecting the resources, expertise and network provided ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_05-emerging-from-beta]]).
- CEO/EIR compensation is a competitive salary plus equity of 30-80%, averaging 50%, reflecting the studio's capital and infrastructure contribution ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_17-entrepreneurs-in-residence-and-ceos]]).
- Drukka's Studioone.hu fund holds 65-75% of each startup it seeds with EUR 20-40,000 ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_13-stretching-our-limits]]).

*Demystified* reports founding stakes of 30-45% and models the dilution that follows:

- Studio equity is priced at nearly $0 at issuance, which is the source of its financial upside ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_02-part-1-venture-studio-basics]]). A studio may start at 30-45% but end as low as 5% at exit after VCs and employees take their pieces ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_05-strategy]]).
- The book's cap-table model runs from 30% at pre-seed to 19% at Seed, 15% at Series A, 12% at Series B, 9% at Series C and 8% at Series D. A 10% increase in round sizes at every stage cuts the studio's return per exit from $22M to $19M and its year-10 cash balance by 17%; a 20% increase cuts exits to $16M and the cash balance by 31% ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_18-equity-ownership]]).
- Viability floor: a studio without a fund needs at least 10% founding equity to cover operating costs; 8% is break-even, dilutes to 1% by Series D and yields only $3.6M per exit; at 10% the studio can operate but cannot return money to investors ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_18-equity-ownership]], [[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_22-part-5-conclusion]]).
- Direction of travel: as studios multiply and compete for top EIRs and founders, and as VCs move upstream, founding equity is expected to decline toward a 10% floor where the economics stop being attractive to studios, with established studios keeping a premium ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_18-equity-ownership]], [[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_25-industrys-path-forward]]).

For the entrepreneur, *Demystified* states the trade plainly: founding equity and early autonomy are exchanged for support, de-risking and structured ideation; founders may treat the studio as a non-technical co-founder and grant co-founder-level shares, but those services are paid for at pre-seed and often stop being available later in the company's life. Whether the price is acceptable depends on the alternatives, and "if your alternative was not being able to start a company at all", it may be more than acceptable ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_23-implications-for-stakeholders]]). Szigeti's Drukka chapter records the investor side of the same worry: early investors feared cap-table dilution and CEO motivation problems, and were won over only by spin-offs achieving 13x valuation increases at seed and 6-8x during Series A ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_13-stretching-our-limits]]).

Recruiting the founders themselves is treated as the highest-leverage decision. eFounders receives 100+ applications a month for 4 slots a year, requires entrepreneurs able to scale a billion-dollar company, and has every candidate meet the entire team ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_07-game-plan]]); Drukka recruits young entrepreneurs who have already tried building a startup or lifestyle business and value not having to manage infrastructure ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_13-stretching-our-limits]]); Midealab uses hackathons to see people work before committing ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_09-building-startups-from-megatrends]]). *Demystified* counts founder recruitment among the fundamental processes every studio shares, and notes that most run similar mechanisms despite claims of a secret sauce ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_23-implications-for-stakeholders]]).

### Funding the studio itself

Both books treat capital as the studio's hardest problem. Szigeti lists capital intensity, investor wariness of first-time studios and cap-table skepticism as the model's main risks ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_03-startup-studios-in-a-nutshell]]); *Demystified* calls funding the primary challenge for studio founders, noting that established studios (High Alpha, PSL, Atomic) make it look easy while new founders face extraordinary stress raising enough to launch ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_23-implications-for-stakeholders]]).

*Demystified* on the investor side ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_13-studio-financing]]):

- Studios are a new asset class unlike VC structures, so convincing VC-accustomed investors to fund the operating entity is hard, more so in less mature ecosystems.
- Operating companies keep a small LP base of high net worth individuals and family offices who want direct early-stage exposure, accept no exit guarantee and can wait 5-7 years.
- Typical raise: $10M-20M for the operating or holding company, with a few outliers at $50M. In dual-entity studios the operating company and the investment fund draw on separate LP pools, the fund's being larger and more diverse.
- Upfront patient capital from LPs is judged the approach most aligned with the studio's mission, ahead of bill-backs and secondary sales ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_23-implications-for-stakeholders]]).

*Demystified* also documents the strategic response to dilution: 11 of 19 interviewed studios had raised an investment fund and 4 more planned to, using management fees to offset operating costs and pro-rata rights to hold a target stake (often 20%) through later rounds, which raises the absolute return per exit (potentially $22M). About half of these studios invest their fund outside their own portfolio, converging toward VC economics ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_05-strategy]]). The book finds no superior structure: open- versus closed-ended funds, dual-entity versus single-fund, structured versus ad-hoc founder programmes all succeed depending on philosophy, local environment and fundraising constraints ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_23-implications-for-stakeholders]]).

The *Playbook* shows what studios do when that capital is not available:

- **Self-funding from legacy businesses.** Innonic funds 2-3 ventures a year from the profits of ShopRenter.hu and OptiMonk, free of investor timelines ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_12-changing-ecommerce-one-startup-at-a-time]]).
- **Agency work as runway.** Lab.Coop started as a development agency (Chain.Reaction) and used the revenue to fund internal projects and co-founded ventures (Green Fox Academy, SmartWare.tech), proving the model before scaling and turning "no VC money" into lower burn and real revenue ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_08-building-a-100-co-owned-venture-builder]]). Laicos took the same route and paid for it: team members who had signed on to build products lost motivation building for clients, which led to layoffs and restructuring ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_11-ahead-of-time]]). The corpus therefore holds both outcomes of the agency-hybrid: a de-risking base in one case, an erosion of the studio's culture in the other.
- **A pre-seed fund with co-investors.** Drukka launched Studioone.hu, co-financed with outside investors: EUR 20-40,000 per startup from the fund, all other resources from Drukka; EUR 1 million could build 30-40 startups ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_13-stretching-our-limits]]).
- **Contract companies subsidizing product companies.** TechnoSpark puts 70% of its investment into contract services companies (high CapEx, immediate revenue, supplying manufacturing and components) and 30% into product companies (low OpEx, revenue reinvested) ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_06-technospark-a-conveyor-of-hardware-startups]]).

Szigeti also states the investor's case for the model as "one-to-many investing": one decision yields equity in several startups ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_04-are-startup-studios-for-me]]).

### Economics and viability

*Demystified* is the only source with a model, and it draws three conclusions ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_22-part-5-conclusion]]):

- A studio without a fund needs 10% founding equity and average exits around $60M to break even in perpetuity.
- Best-in-class studios (High Alpha, Atomic, Founders) realized about 100% IRR at the operating-company level and 26% at fund level, against an average VC IRR of 21.9% for 2010-2016 and a top quartile of 25.6%.
- Published performance is biased upward: the estimated failure rate is about 1.3% a year (18 of 700 studios), but many surviving studios may never deliver sustainable returns, and failed studios do not publish. Whether studios are an appealing asset class is answered "yes, depending on who you are".

The mechanism behind the returns, as *Demystified* puts it, is not better ideas but high founder-level ownership across several companies, secured by sufficient upfront capital and high operational involvement; studios claim a higher "batting average" with fewer zero outcomes, hence less dependence on one monster winner, though whether that holds at scale is unproven ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_23-implications-for-stakeholders]]). The one outcome statistic offered is that companies founded out of studios convert from Seed to Series A at 72% versus 42% for others ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_06-process]]).

The *Playbook* gives case-level figures rather than a model: eFounders had built over 10 companies with combined valuations of $125M+ ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_07-game-plan]]); Drukka got almost half of a dozen initiatives to seed-ready stage on most of EUR 100,000 ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_13-stretching-our-limits]]); TechnoSpark had built over 100 high-tech startups since 2012 and aims to set up hardware companies 2-3 times faster than the usual 15-20 years, treating venture building as a learnable profession divisible into specializations ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_06-technospark-a-conveyor-of-hardware-startups]]).

### Culture and leadership inside the factory

The *Playbook* devotes more attention than *Demystified* to how a studio holds its own people together while they build things they will have to let go.

- **The factory mindset.** Leaders must accept that they are running factory operations rather than chasing one unicorn, enjoy letting mature companies become independent, and kill unsuccessful initiatives to recycle resources. They must scale their own expertise across dozens of CEOs through standardized processes, delegation and an executive team capable of portfolio management, without becoming the bottleneck. They balance three constituencies at once: the studio, the portfolio companies (founders and investees simultaneously) and external investors, and the cap table and management fees must reflect all three. Retaining venture builders who repeatedly release what they build requires constant inspiration against the repetitiveness of the work ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_14-studio-leadership]]).
- **Culture as a verb.** Backspace spent one day a month deliberately engineering culture for a new 11-person team: surfacing core and aspirational values, a bottom-up mission in about eight words, and rituals (daily lunch, weekly proud moments, monthly off-sites, quarterly governance reviews). The studio counts itself as a fourth company subject to the same discipline, shares tools and templates with its ventures without imposing its culture, and copies practices back from them ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_10-culture-is-a-verb]]).
- **Co-ownership and holacracy.** Lab.Coop made every team member a co-owner with equity that grows with tenure, work complexity and peer-recognized skills (a Badge-Based Compensation Scheme), governed by holacracy with transparent, formula-based salaries. It closed the motivation gap that fixed salaries cannot close against VC-backed competitors, and scaled from 7 to 35 people without a management layer, at the cost of more effort educating newcomers than expected ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_08-building-a-100-co-owned-venture-builder]]).
- **Equity for the studio team.** Laicos set up an employee stock option plan with attorney-defined valuation, vesting and objective grant criteria (tenure, skills, future potential) so that it could compete for developers in a capital-scarce region; the "piece of the pie" ceremony shifted the team's psychology toward co-ownership ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_11-ahead-of-time]]).
- **Values as advantage.** Innonic's AWESOME values and investments in people (academy talks, management training, coaching) are positioned as a competitive advantage in retaining both the core team and venture CEOs ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_12-changing-ecommerce-one-startup-at-a-time]]).

### Geography and ecosystem maturity

Several sources tie a studio's survival to where it sits. Laicos, founded in 2012 in California and later run from Tampa, found that local investors were dated in their practices, wanted $1M+ revenue before investing, or saw the studio as a threat to their own venture role; entrepreneurs supported the model while investors did not. Investors in Las Vegas and Austin offered relocation the team could not accept, and the studio arm was shut down; Negri concludes that capital access and investor sophistication are prerequisites, not options ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_11-ahead-of-time]]). *Demystified* makes the same point statistically: the new-asset-class barrier is amplified in less mature entrepreneurial ecosystems ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_13-studio-financing]]), and its own sample is 63% US-based (16% Europe, 11% Canada, 5% LatAm, 5% Middle East), which the authors flag as a limit on international applicability ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_01-introduction]]). The Central European cases in the *Playbook* (Lab.Coop, Innonic, Drukka in Hungary) all solved the same problem with revenue rather than investors.

### Scaling limits and the industry's trajectory

Both books count studios, at different dates: about 50 in 2015 and more than 300 at the *Playbook*'s writing ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_02-a-place-for-pioneers-and-creators]]); 710 worldwide in 2022, a 625% increase since 2013, still far fewer than the 1,965 US VC firms ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_02-part-1-venture-studio-basics]]).

*Demystified* closes with structural limits and three expansion paths ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_25-industrys-path-forward]]):

- Follow-on investment in successful companies can scale indefinitely, but studio operations cannot: a bigger team slows ideation and decisions, partners resist diluting their carry and ownership to add partners, and founder-level equity has to be earned with sustained high-touch involvement.
- New studios will come from traditional VCs building their own, from serial entrepreneurs, and from established studios seeding new studio leaders once their own team hits the limit of how many ideas it can support.
- The asset class is moving from "innovation trigger" toward the "peak of inflated expectations": LP demand will exceed studio supply for a while, making fundraising feasible even for non-premier founders, after which capital will concentrate on proven studio leaders.

Szigeti's foreword expects the model to spread beyond software and commerce into other growth areas ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_01-foreword]]), and TechnoSpark's hardware conveyor is the *Playbook*'s evidence that studio economics reach capital-intensive, manufacturing-dependent ventures when the work is split into hyperspecialized companies ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_06-technospark-a-conveyor-of-hardware-startups]]).
