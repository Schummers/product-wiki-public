# Sustainable Growth

## Definition

_Partially written: what follows covers only *Product Management for UX People*; the other sources listed below have not yet been folded into this prose._

Christian Crumlish approaches sustainable growth from two sides. On the product side, durable user growth rests on retention rather than acquisition alone, and is tracked through growth metrics such as DAU/MAU and retention cohorts ([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_09-chapter-6-product-analytics-growth-engagement-retention]]). On the financial side, it is the survivability of the business model itself, read across a revenue lifecycle that runs from an establishing phase with costs and no revenue, through growth, to the optimisation and plateau of an established stream, and eventually to sunsetting when a product no longer breaks even and has no viable future ([[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_11-chapter-8-getting-the-money]]).

## Practice

### Retention is what makes growth compound (book)

[[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_09-chapter-6-product-analytics-growth-engagement-retention]] organises the growth levers with Dave McClure's AARRR mnemonic — Acquisition, Activation, Retention, Referral, Revenue, sometimes written AAARRR with Awareness on top — and treats each stage as instrumentable as its own funnel. Crumlish's ratio guidance is explicitly a rule of thumb: DAU/MAU tells you how many days of the month a typical user drops by, 40% is generally considered good and above 50% excellent, "but this will actually vary depending on industry norms". He warns that counting anyone who shows up as active overstates the case and makes a vanity metric, so a useful definition of active needs a basket of qualifying events, and notes that comparing engaged users to active users shows where "more lookie-loos can be converted to participants" — the more engaged a person is, the more likely you are to retain them. See [[User Retention]] and [[Funnel]].

### "Ramen profitable" is a milestone, not a destination (book)

[[2022-01-19_product-management-for-ux-people_11-chapter-8-getting-the-money]] tells this through 7 Cups. Reaching break-even through paid professional therapy made the company profitable "in the only way that a lean, volunteer-powered start-up in a mission-driven space could be", by keeping expenses at a bare minimum, doing everything "scrappy, not crappy", and paying below market rates — which Crumlish says is itself not real sustainability in the long run. It broke the cycle of venture-capital dependency, and it opened three directions rather than closing the question: organic growth with small bets, optimisation of the core model toward healthy sustainability, or a swing at a 100x breakthrough. The path there was layered rather than singular: donations, consumer upgrades, white-label SaaS for universities, a therapist directory, and paid therapy, on different pay cycles, compounding.

The chapter also names the long-run threat to an established stream, the "innovator's dilemma", which Crumlish credits to Clay Christensen: established revenue creates risk aversion, incumbents cannot afford to cannibalise what they already earn, and that is the opening disruptive upstarts take.

**Crumlish's own sidebar disagrees with the goal.** B. Pagels-Minor, then a product manager at Netflix, calls break-even "pretty illogical in modern tech companies", pointing out that Amazon and Netflix were deliberately unprofitable for years, and argues the goal is more likely market penetration. Crumlish accepts the objection and asks the reader to treat his story as one particular path, not the success goal. See [[Revenue Model]] and [[Business Strategy]].
