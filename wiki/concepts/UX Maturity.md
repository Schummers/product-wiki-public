# UX Maturity

## Definition

UX maturity describes an organization's capability to apply user-centered
design consistently across its products and services — its desire and its
ability to deliver on that intent [[2021-07-25_factors-ux-maturity]],
[[2024-11-15_what-is-user-experience]]. It is not a property of a single
project or team but of the organization: how UX shows up in its strategy, in
what its people believe about users, in the methods it actually runs, and in
the outcomes it chooses to measure. Nielsen Norman Group formalises it as a
six-stage model assessed across those four factors — strategy, culture,
process, outcomes — each with subfactors, so that maturity reads as a
multidimensional profile rather than a single score; two organizations at the
same stage can look very different depending on size, priorities, and politics
[[2021-07-25_factors-ux-maturity]]. Seen from the other side of the table, the
same capability reads as a set of conditions leadership grants rather than a
score a design team earns: whether design expertise is trusted as strategic
problem-solving, how fast decisions get made, and whether a designer sits at
the executive level at all
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_12-chapter-11-how-executives-can-help-designers]].

The sources insist that maturity is dynamic, not a ladder climbed once.
[[2025-08-29_informal-ux-maturity]] calls it a living system that drifts
without tending, and [[2025-09-19_signs-of-ux-maturity-regression]] treats
regression as a normal, recoverable condition rather than failure — a framing
[[2022-01-02_ux-maturity-stage-6]] shares when it says a slip from stage 6 to
stage 5 should motivate renewed effort rather than be read as total defeat. At
the level of the field rather than the firm, the same word describes the
discipline's own maturation: [[2018-08-08_nielsen-norman-group-20-years]] and
[[2017-12-24_100-years-ux]] chart UX growing from a handful of practitioners
to a global profession, while [[2020-11-01_ux-gains-shrinking]] reads the drop
in average UX improvement scores (247% in 2006–2008 down to 75% in 2020) not
as declining quality but as evidence the field has already solved its most
egregious problems and moved into refinement.

## Practice

### The four factors

The model's structure is what makes it actionable
[[2021-07-25_factors-ux-maturity]]:

- **Strategy** — vision built on user-centered objectives, planning and
  prioritization that embed UX in schedules and processes, and budget
  allocation. Low-maturity organizations barely mention UX in a schedule; high
  maturity ones prioritize UX work and track quality systematically.
- **Culture** — awareness of what UX is, appreciation and support (including
  C-suite involvement), competency (defined skills and career paths), and
  adaptability. Rigid organizations struggle to adopt or adjust UX approaches.
- **Process** — established methods across the product lifecycle,
  cross-functional collaboration, and consistency through shared frameworks
  and tools. Low maturity uses methods haphazardly or reactively.
- **Outcomes** — design impact measured by user-centered metrics rather than
  shipped features, and the discipline of actually tracking and using those
  goals. Low-maturity organizations chase metrics unrelated to user needs.

The central prescription is **synergistic growth**: grow all factors in
parallel rather than optimizing one, because they reinforce each other and
parallel growth returns better ROI than pushing a single factor far ahead
[[2021-07-25_factors-ux-maturity]].

### What the stages look like

The corpus documents four of the six stages directly.

- **Stage 2 (Limited)** — small, erratic efforts driven by a few passionate
  advocates, with no official recognition, dedicated role, or budget. Proper
  methods get substituted by shortcuts: informal checklists instead of
  research, focus groups instead of usability testing, research run by
  inexperienced people producing inaccurate results. Without shared tools,
  design inconsistency is rampant, and quantitative data collected tends to be
  vanity metrics [[2021-09-19_ux-maturity-stage-2]].
- **Stage 3 (Emergent)** — named in the quiz data as the most common score
  among respondents [[2022-01-16_state-ux-maturity-quiz]].
- **Stage 4 (Structured)** — one or more established UX teams, leadership
  support, a documented iterative human-centered process, research across the
  lifecycle, and design systems starting to gain traction. Remaining obstacles:
  a few unsupportive leaders, dividing responsibilities across specialized
  roles as teams scale, getting UX into company metrics, and over-allocating UX
  resources to a few big customers [[2021-11-21_ux-maturity-stage-4]].
  [[2022-01-16_state-ux-maturity-quiz]] adds a warning: many companies become
  complacent at stage 4 and never move past it.
- **Stage 5 (Integrated)** — UX understood and supported across departments,
  established and sometimes innovative methods, robust design systems and
  research repositories run by dedicated ops teams, and UX performance metrics
  in everyone's goals. Business goals still drive the priorities
  [[2021-12-12_ux-maturity-stage-5]].
- **Stage 6 (User-Driven)** — user research drives everything from top-level
  strategy to the smallest element of a design system; dedicated high-level UX
  roles (VP, design director, research director), a specialized design-system
  team continuously iterating, budgets consistently directed at UX growth, and
  discovery research treated as nonnegotiable before project approval
  [[2022-01-02_ux-maturity-stage-6]].

Where the sources differ is on where to aim. [[2022-01-02_ux-maturity-stage-6]]
presents stage 6 as the ultimate goal; [[2021-12-12_ux-maturity-stage-5]]
argues stage 5 is the highest level most organizations will reach and a
perfectly good place to operate long term, precisely because its systems and
infrastructure make it sustainable while stage 6 usually does not last.

### Moving up a stage

Each stage article names its own lever rather than prescribing a generic
programme:

- From stage 2, focus on **culture and process**: build organization-wide
  understanding of UX's value, invest in executing methods properly, recruit
  more advocates, and expand the toolkit [[2021-09-19_ux-maturity-stage-2]].
- From stage 4, focus on **process flexibility**: diagnose why some teams
  struggle with the methods, ask whether the overall process needs adjusting,
  and adapt it to each team's needs — small improvements, not an overhaul
  [[2021-11-21_ux-maturity-stage-4]].
- At stage 6, the lever is **culture maintenance**: nurture shared user-focused
  values through leadership messaging, accountability, and celebrating people
  who demonstrate them, because the culture deteriorates as soon as decisions
  stop being aligned with user-centered design
  [[2022-01-02_ux-maturity-stage-6]].

Structural incentives matter as much as advocacy: [[2017-12-24_100-years-ux]]
attributes UX's historical growth to the PC revolution making the user the
buyer and the web making experience the gatekeeper to revenue, not to cultural
recognition alone.

[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_12-chapter-11-how-executives-can-help-designers]]
names the levers that sit with executives rather than with designers, as four
obligations: **realize** that designers solve problems strategically and are
better at design than the stakeholders who hired them; **prioritize** their
needs by supplying clear business requirements, timelines, technical access,
stakeholder contact and a budget for usability research, so work can start
without waiting for information; **authorize** fast decisions, on the argument
that projects move at the speed of decision-making rather than the speed of
talent, which makes daily design reviews and empowered designers better than
weekly meetings and committee calls; and **recognize** designers' humanity,
since problem-focused rather than person-focused language is what lets people
take creative risks. Its structural claim is that design leadership must exist
at the highest level — without designers in positions of authority, even a
talented team works under constraints that prevent the best solution — and its
practical instrument is a **project checklist** run at kickoff: vision, goals
and metrics, user understanding, workflow cadence, technical requirements, and
who decides. Greever presents the checklist as a flexible guide rather than a
fixed list; running one is what keeps stakeholders and designers aligned from
the start, which reduces rework and speeds delivery.

### Assessing without a formal audit

[[2025-08-29_informal-ux-maturity]] gives the low-budget protocol: shift the
question from "what stage are we in?" to "what signals are we seeing?", and use
the model as a lens in retrospectives and planning sessions. Concrete low-lift
methods include quarterly UX-health snapshots and traffic-light (green/yellow/
red) tracking, plus maturity maps across teams. It also warns against
over-assessing: reassessing as often as quarterly creates pressure to show
progress and produces superficial fixes; formal assessment is worth the
investment when leadership needs objective evidence, major organizational
change is coming, a funding business case is needed, or enough time has passed
to act on prior recommendations.

Self-assessment has a known reliability problem. In the NN/g quiz data, role
strongly shaped the score: research, management and experience-design roles
rated their organizations higher (49, 47, 46 on average) than engineering,
visual design or marketing roles (all 40 or below) — so the same organization
scores differently depending on who answers, and outside assessors give a more
objective measurement [[2022-01-16_state-ux-maturity-quiz]].

### Spotting and preventing regression

[[2025-09-19_signs-of-ux-maturity-regression]] is the operational companion:
regression happens quietly, and is noticed only once problems are too big and
expensive to fix quickly. Its signals and countermeasures:

- **Rituals become hollow** — design reviews, retrospectives and research
  debriefs where nobody engages. Rotate facilitators, introduce prompts, focus
  on themes.
- **Metrics exist but are unused** — usability data and surveys that never
  shape prioritization become vanity dashboards; pair them with business
  outcomes so UX impact ties to decisions.
- **Systems available but not adopted** — a design system nobody uses.
- **Ownership concentrated in a few champions** — makes maturity fragile to
  personnel change; shared ownership and rotated responsibility are the
  structural safeguard.
- **Overconfidence** — "we have a design system, so UX is fine" creates blind
  spots until costly rework appears.

Its recommended cadence is a 30–45 minute quarterly check-in across the four
factors, plus a visible "UX health log" that makes drift legible over time.
[[2025-08-29_informal-ux-maturity]] adds what to watch between formal
assessments: whether practices have slipped, whether known strengths are still
effective, and whether any factor has become performative or plateaued.

### Markers of low maturity

The corpus supplies several concrete tells:

- **Rushing to implementation** with too little time for UX activities, and
  lack of management buy-in — the top two barriers to discovery, with only 20%
  of surveyed organizations running discovery for every project
  [[2020-05-17_discoveries-in-industry-revealed]].
- **Treating UX as expendable when budgets tighten**: many organizations still
  sit in the bottom half of the maturity scale and cut UX first, which
  [[2024-11-15_what-is-user-experience]] calls short-sighted, and which
  [[2025-01-10_ux-reset-2025]] identifies as a recurring pattern going back to
  the dot-com bust.
- **Template-following in place of thinking**: relying on pre-built frameworks
  without adapting them to context undermines the depth of the practice, and
  the value of a template lies in its thoughtful transformation through a
  team's own strategy and constraints, not in its initial form
  [[2025-05-30_template-trap]].
- **Informal research infrastructure**: panel maturity runs from ad-hoc
  spreadsheets at the low end to structured systems with automation and
  governance at the high end, and building a panel at all signals
  organizational investment in research — panels move research from isolated
  projects to steady organizational decision-making
  [[2026-01-23_user-panels-101]].

### What maturity is not determined by

[[2022-01-16_state-ux-maturity-quiz]] rules out two intuitive predictors:
geographic region has no practical significance (all regions averaged 42–46
points, stage 3), and company size does not predict maturity (small, medium and
large all averaged 44–45) — large companies have budget advantages but struggle
with cultural shifts, while smaller ones can adopt practices faster or skip
early stages entirely. High stages are genuinely rare in the data: 4% at stage
5 and 0.04% at stage 6.

Finally, [[2025-01-10_ux-reset-2025]] warns against a fake fix: renaming teams
to "insights research" or "experience design" addresses the symptom
(misperception) without solving the underlying problem of demonstrating UX's
business value — the answer is improving organizational maturity and strategy.
