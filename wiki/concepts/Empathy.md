# Empathy

## Definition

Empathy, as the sources define it, is the ability to fully understand, mirror
and then share another person's expressions, needs and motivations [[2019-04-21_sympathy-vs-empathy-ux]]. It is
deliberately opposed to sympathy, which is feeling sorry for someone's
difficulty without necessarily understanding it and without putting yourself in
their shoes or feeling their frustrations [[2019-04-21_sympathy-vs-empathy-ux]]. The two sit on a spectrum, from
pity, which requires minimal effort, to compassion, which requires understanding
and a genuine compulsion to help [[2019-04-21_sympathy-vs-empathy-ux]]. Many UX teams believe they practise
empathy while actually practising sympathy, and the gap shows in their ability
to address real needs [[2019-04-21_sympathy-vs-empathy-ux]].

Empathy is treated as an operational requirement rather than a sentiment. It
goes deeper than acknowledgement, into users' hopes, fears, reasoning and goals,
and it leads to designing solutions that improve lives and remove unnecessary
friction [[2019-04-21_sympathy-vs-empathy-ux]]; the stated goal of design there is not to be nice to users but to
empower them. It develops through engaging with real people, observing their
frustrations, and understanding how they experience the world differently, and
it is foundational to ethical design and user-centred decision making [[2021-08-01_qualitative-rigor]]. The
foreword to *UX Research* extends this beyond the design context specifically:
research is fundamentally an act of human connection, and practising it gives
researchers a different way of engaging in all their relationships, not only
their professional ones [[2016-11-04_ux-research_01-foreword]]. The
sources also give it two standard artifacts: empathy maps, which externalise
what a team knows about a user type [[2018-01-14_empathy-mapping]] [[2023-02-12_using-empathy-maps]], and personas, which turn user
segments into concrete characters [[2025-10-03_persona]].

One source stretches the word past the user, and does so deliberately. Tom
Greever observes that UX people are good at putting the user first and
garnering empathy for them, yet often fail to do the same for the people who
hold the keys to their success; true empathy for a stakeholder means seeing the
project from their perspective deeply enough to want them to succeed, rather
than treating them as an obstacle
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_03-chapter-2-stakeholders-are-people-too]].
It extends to their needs, insecurities and constraints — a manager who needs
something to show in a meeting of their own — so the designer serves stakeholder
success and not only user success
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_06-chapter-5-get-in-the-right-mindset]].
The same book turns the obligation around in its closing chapter, written for
executives: recognise designers as people with lives beyond the project
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_12-chapter-11-how-executives-can-help-designers]].
A third direction appears when a designer moves into management. Mia Blume
counts empathy among the skills designers build over years of honing their
craft — with creativity, vision, storytelling and systems thinking — and holds
that these transfer directly to business leadership, empathy being foundational
to coaching, where the first and most important move is listening to understand
someone's problem before offering an answer
[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_29-mia-blume-skills-which-future-design-managers-should-work-on]].
Empathy in this corpus therefore names one skill pointed in three directions:
outward at users, sideways at colleagues, and inward at the team a design
manager is responsible for.

## Practice

### Build empathy through direct exposure, not reports

Qualitative research is the primary vehicle: user interviews, cognitive mapping
and diary studies with open-ended questions reveal mental models, motivations
and unexpected needs [[2019-04-21_sympathy-vs-empathy-ux]]. Team members who observe real user sessions or watch
research videos develop stronger empathy than those who read summary reports
[[2019-04-21_sympathy-vs-empathy-ux]]. [[2019-04-21_sympathy-vs-empathy-ux]] adds two further levers: diverse team composition, and deliberate
design protocols that counter unconscious bias and false consensus (it notes that
it is part of human psychology to assume others think and behave as we do). For
users with disabilities, it argues empathy requires direct experience rather
than theoretical understanding of the challenges.

[[2021-08-01_qualitative-rigor]] makes the epistemological case behind this. Qualitative research with small
samples is rigorous, systematic inquiry, not anecdote: rigour comes from
evidence-based theoretical frameworks, specific research questions, careful
sampling, open-ended facilitation, systematic inductive coding and
triangulation, and is judged on credibility, transferability, dependability and
confirmability — not on sample size. Its goal is to understand *why* a problem
occurs and *how* to fix it, while quantitative work measures how much of the
population hits it; conflating the two produces false criticism. What
qualitative work yields, beyond findings, is genuine empathetic understanding of
users as humans rather than data points [[2021-08-01_qualitative-rigor]].

In session, empathy is also what tells a researcher when to ease off:
participants may feel obligated to tell you what they think you want to hear,
and recognizing when someone is uncomfortable or guarded — then adjusting your
approach so they feel you are listening and genuinely care — requires empathy
and genuine concern
for their experience beyond the data being collected
[[2016-11-04_ux-research_12-chapter-11-facilitating-research]].

### Empathy maps: what they are

An empathy map is a collaborative visualisation of what a team knows about a
particular type of user, divided into four quadrants — Says (what users
vocalise), Thinks (what occupies their thoughts), Does (physical actions) and
Feels (emotions) [[2018-01-14_empathy-mapping]] [[2023-02-12_using-empathy-maps]]. Its two purposes are creating a shared understanding
of user needs and aiding decision making [[2018-01-14_empathy-mapping]]. Unlike journey maps it is not
chronological: it captures a holistic snapshot of a user type at a moment [[2018-01-14_empathy-mapping]].
Juxtaposition between quadrants — positive actions alongside negative emotions,
for instance — is expected and is where hidden insights and opportunities appear
[[2018-01-14_empathy-mapping]]. The simple four-quadrant structure is approachable for non-researchers,
which is what makes it useful for engaging a wide set of stakeholders [[2023-02-12_using-empathy-maps]].

### Empathy maps: when to use them

[[2023-02-12_using-empathy-maps]] argues against treating them only as a post-research communication artifact
and identifies three moments:

- **Before research** — document current knowledge with confidence ratings; the
  gaps become research questions and make the study more focused [[2023-02-12_using-empathy-maps]]. [[2018-01-14_empathy-mapping]]
  makes the same point from the artifact side: a sparse map reveals where the
  team lacks understanding and signals that more research is needed before design
  decisions proceed.
- **During research** — capture observations as they happen, using colour-coded
  sticky notes [[2023-02-12_using-empathy-maps]].
- **After research** — communicate findings and build buy-in; involving the
  wider team in building the map from research data creates ownership and
  ensures stakeholders understand user needs, rather than merely being told them
  [[2023-02-12_using-empathy-maps]]. Affinity diagramming on the sticky notes helps organise and discuss what
  emerges [[2023-02-12_using-empathy-maps]].

### Empathy maps: how to build them

Start from research data — user interviews, field studies, diary studies — not
from assumptions, and involve the team in the mapping activity itself, since the
process, not only the finished artifact, is where the value lies: it builds
shared understanding and removes bias-based assumptions [[2018-01-14_empathy-mapping]]. Both individual
maps, based on one user, and aggregated maps, combining users with similar
behaviours, are useful; the aggregated map is a first step toward a persona [[2018-01-14_empathy-mapping]].
Treat maps as living documents, with version numbers and dates, so changes are
traceable and improving understanding is visible [[2018-01-14_empathy-mapping]]. [[2018-01-14_empathy-mapping]] recommends using
them throughout the UX process to establish common ground and prioritise needs.

### Personas as an empathy device

A persona is a fictional yet realistic description of a typical or target user,
synthesised from concrete research into a single character with a name, photo,
behaviours, goals and concerns [[2025-10-03_persona]]. The mechanism [[2025-10-03_persona]] invokes is that humans
are more captivated by concrete instances than by generalisations, so an
individual story sticks in memory where statistics do not; the persona feels
like a living person, which is what produces empathy and stops the team designing
for a generic "everyone". Practical constraints follow: include names and photos
because memorability is the persona's primary function, but leave out irrelevant
details that obscure what is needed for design decisions; base them on actual
user data from field studies, surveys, interviews and longitudinal studies, never
on assumptions; and create them collaboratively, since teams that participated
are more likely to believe, use and promote them [[2025-10-03_persona]]. The persona's name then
acts as shorthand for the full set of attributes, desires and behaviours,
replacing each team member's private mental model of "the user" [[2025-10-03_persona]]. Their use
extends past design, into screening usability-study participants, segmenting
analytics and guiding expert reviews [[2025-10-03_persona]].

### Empathy for stakeholders, not only users

[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_03-chapter-2-stakeholders-are-people-too]]
treats stakeholder empathy as a set of learnable moves rather than a
disposition: see stakeholders as human, since their reaction to design work is
often shaped by external factors rather than by the work itself; create shared
experiences to find common ground; develop empathy proper, understanding their
viewpoint until you are driven to help them succeed; and ask good questions,
the same open-ended questioning UX practitioners use with users, to learn what
matters to them. Knowing what each role values is part of the same skill:
executives care about vision and reporting, developers about quality and
feasibility, product owners about innovation and simplicity, project managers
about schedule and scope. The payoff that source states is reciprocal — "Our job
is to learn how to make our stakeholders successful, because if we can help them
be successful, that will help us be successful as a byproduct."

Listening is what makes it operational
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_05-chapter-4-listen-to-understand]].
Implicitly: let stakeholders talk without interruption, since they clarify and
rephrase as they go and gain confidence they were heard; hear what is not being
said, reading subtext and context clues (a stakeholder who says "That's
interesting" may actually disagree; enthusiasm that drops after a project update
signals something else going on); uncover the real problem behind a suggested
solution by asking "What problem are you trying to solve?", since a stakeholder
proposing a visual change is usually describing a symptom; and master the pause,
waiting two or three seconds before responding. Explicitly: take detailed notes,
ask clarifying questions, and repeat feedback back in design vocabulary —
converting "I like" into "Does this work?" — so both sides end up sharing a
language
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_05-chapter-4-listen-to-understand]].

Empathy also shapes the designer's posture in the room. Rather than defending
the work or pitching it, orient toward stakeholders' needs instead of your own
interests; giving up ultimate control removes the defensiveness that blocks
understanding, and confidence without arrogance, authenticity and humour make
the exchange safe enough to be honest
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_06-chapter-5-get-in-the-right-mindset]].
The mirror image closes the loop: executives who show genuine interest in
designers beyond the project and use kind, problem-focused language instead of
attacking the person create the psychological safety in which designers take
creative risks and propose innovative solutions
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_12-chapter-11-how-executives-can-help-designers]].

### Empathy as the ground of coaching

[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_29-mia-blume-skills-which-future-design-managers-should-work-on]]
extends the same skill to managing designers. The shift from individual
contributor to manager asks a designer to let go of control and become a coach:
the instinct is to direct a solution from one's own expertise, and the skill to
develop is coaching teammates to solve their own problems instead. Listening
comes first — when someone approaches you seeking an answer, the job is to
understand their problem before offering direction — which puts this source and
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_05-chapter-4-listen-to-understand]]
on the same ground, one facing a report and the other a stakeholder. From there
the moves are: redirect the problem back to the teammate with empowering
questions such as "What's the goal of this exercise?" or "If you had two more
weeks, how would you prioritize the work?"; avoid making decisions yourself
unless it is genuinely necessary, on high-risk projects or against tight
deadlines; and say explicitly that you trust the person's ability, offering
support and perspective rather than direction. The consequence that source
draws for training is that designers already hold the foundational leadership
skills, so management development should add coaching and delegation rather
than rebuild the rest
[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_29-mia-blume-skills-which-future-design-managers-should-work-on]].
