# Ideation

## Definition

Ideation is the process of generating a broad set of ideas on a given topic
with no attempt to judge or evaluate them
([[2017-01-15_ux-ideation]]). That source treats it as an umbrella term rather
than a single method: brainstorming is one formalised type of ideation,
following Osborn's guidelines, and many other approaches sit under the same
heading. Its defining constraints are quantity over quality, deferred judgment,
and tangible capture of every idea, because generating many ideas raises the
chance that one seeds a good solution while evaluating during generation stifles
the flow.

Ideation is positioned in a specific slot in the process: in the Explore phase
of design thinking, after user research has defined the problem and before
prototyping ([[2017-01-15_ux-ideation]]). Its purpose is parallel design,
exploring several solutions rather than committing to the first, which raises
the odds of finding an optimal path rather than merely an acceptable one. Its
prerequisite is user research: ideas ungrounded in real user needs are worthless,
because they may miss the target and, worse, leave the team without valid
criteria for choosing among them. The corpus consistently frames it as
collaborative work: [[2016-01-03_ux-deliverables-collaboration]] finds 82% of UX
professionals collaborate on deliverables, and divergent generation of multiple
alternatives before converging is one of the main reasons they give.

One source works at a different scale. [[2026-03-05_10x-method_02-finding-and-validating-the-idea]]
treats ideation as the search for a startup idea rather than for solutions to an
already-framed design problem, and what it asks you to generate is not a
one-liner but a framework combining a problem, a solution and a go-to-market
strategy, which then evolves through research, feedback and testing. It shares
the corpus's insistence on grounding, and inverts its sequencing: there is no
session, and no defined problem handed to you at the start.

[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_12-step-4-list-ideas-what]] sits in a
third context again: ideation as one numbered step of a solo design exercise,
run after the audience and the context of use have been defined and before any
evaluation. Separating generation from judgment survives the change of scale,
though the source states it structurally rather than as a rule: the criteria and
the impact/effort matrix live in the next step, not this one. The quantity norm
does not survive it. What the step asks for is three to four possible products,
not a wide sweep.

## Practice

### Framing the problem before generating ideas

[[2021-01-17_how-might-we-questions]] describes the How Might We format,
introduced at Procter & Gamble in the 1970s and adopted by IDEO, used at the end
of discovery to frame the challenge and stop teams defaulting to a pet solution.
Its quality rules: ground the question in actual research findings rather than a
generic challenge; embed no solution, since a question containing "tell users"
will only produce communication ideas; keep it broad enough for several
approaches without losing the core problem; target the desired outcome rather
than the symptom (user confidence rather than stopping calls); use positive verbs
(increase, create, enhance) instead of negative ones (reduce, remove, prevent);
and write and select the questions as a team against a checklist.

[[2021-03-28_scenario-mapping-personas]] frames the challenge as a story
instead. A scenario is a brief narrative of a persona completing one key task,
built from five elements: an actor, a motivator, an intention, an action, and a
resolution. It makes abstract requirements tangible and easier to empathise
with. That source is explicit about the level of detail: keep the scenario
high-level, inject no UI details or solution concepts, and include only a few
high-priority needs, because excessive specificity about location, devices, or
preferences forces the team to solve for those details rather than produce a
general solution. [[2021-09-26_ux-mapping-methods-study-guide]] groups scenario
mapping with cognitive mapping as the mapping methods that support early
ideation by externalising and exploring user perspectives.

### Diverge, then converge

The structural principle running through most of the corpus is separating
generation from evaluation, and individual work from group work.

[[2024-05-30_diverge-converge]] states it as a two-stage method: team members
work alone to produce ideas, insights, or designs without group influence, then
gather to discuss and synthesise. Its rationale is that many workshop failures
come precisely from denying people time to sit with their ideas before exposing
them to others' opinions, and that this ordering prevents groupthink, the HiPPO
effect, and personal bias while letting introverts contribute equally. Its
ground rules for the diverge stage: enforce quiet time, ban "no" and "but",
consider anonymity for sensitive topics, and pick low-distraction tools for
diverging against timeboxed synchronous tools for converging. Diverging can
happen before the workshop at each person's own pace or as protected solo time
inside it.

[[2017-07-02_facilitating-design-studio-workshop]] applies the same principle in
a condensed format. A design studio runs four segments in a few hours: Sketch
(individual ideation, with talking discouraged, since individual concentration
before group collaboration yields more ideas and more creativity), Present and
Critique, Converge, and Prioritize. It names a technique per segment: Crazy 8s
for volume sketching, Six Thinking Hats to structure critique through separate
lenses (logic, emotion, strengths, weaknesses, creativity, process), SCAMPER for
convergence, and the $100 Test for prioritisation, on the grounds that a
monetary framing pulls more real investment out of the discussion than arbitrary
points. Its instruction for the converge stage is notable: converge by combining
and evolving the strong elements of separate ideas, not by seeking consensus.

[[2024-04-26_affinity-diagram]] carries the same shape into synthesis: generate
independently on sticky notes for 5-10 minutes to avoid bias from dominant
participants, cluster collaboratively while staying open to other people's
interpretations and without forcing ideas together, label each cluster, then
prioritise by dot voting and record action items and a point person. Its stated
payoff is the discussion rather than the artifact: the journey matters more than
the destination, and small clusters keep their value even when they hold few
items.

### Making group ideation actually work

[[2017-08-13_group-ideation]] reports that 66% of UX professionals think group
work produces higher-quality design ideas, and argues that group ideation's bad
reputation comes from group dynamics rather than from any flaw in the method.
Its fixes: counter groupthink and fixation on the first idea by explicitly
setting initial suggestions aside and pushing for wider diversity first; counter
unequal contribution by emphasising quantity over quality, warming participants
up, and lowering the pressure so quieter people speak; counter hierarchy
intimidation by excluding high-ranking members from the session or running a
pre-session where junior staff can speak safely first; and use parallel design,
parking lots for off-topic ideas, and facilitators trained in group dynamics.
Its position on solo work is that individual ideation has value but doing 100%
of ideation alone means missing big opportunities.

[[2017-10-29_ideation-in-practice]] supplies the survey evidence, from 257 UX
professionals: only 15% consider their ideation process very effective and 59%
call it merely somewhat effective. What separates the effective teams is not
time (77% of them spend under three hours) but structure. They collaborate more
and rely less on individual ideation; they ideate after research and before
prototyping (71% versus 48% of the merely-somewhat-effective); they use written
techniques such as brainwriting far more, which cuts groupthink and hierarchy
bias; and they use formal methods rather than open discussion, where 95% of
ineffective teams rely on unstructured conversation. It also names managerial
support as a barrier: 15% cite its absence, since without clout you cannot pull
people off other teams or reserve the time.

Note a tension worth holding: [[2024-05-30_diverge-converge]] and
[[2017-07-02_facilitating-design-studio-workshop]] both insist on solo
generation first, while [[2017-10-29_ideation-in-practice]] and
[[2017-08-13_group-ideation]] find that effective teams rely less on individual
ideation. The corpus resolves this as sequencing rather than opposition: solo
generation inside a collaborative session, not solo ideation instead of one.

### Workshop formats and facilitation

[[2018-07-22_ux-workshop-exercises]] gives a general three-step model,
Explain-Execute-Examine. Explain introduces steps, roles, and context neutrally
and never states what will be learned, which would bias the output. Execute
means stepping back: the facilitator observes rather than participates, because
holding the facilitator role gives any contribution disproportionate weight.
Examine has three parts, presenting outcomes, reflecting on the experience, and
connecting outputs to previous and future work. It adds that silence is a tool
(people need it to formulate thoughts) and that facilitation itself should be
iterated on, by documenting what worked and watching other facilitators.

[[2018-09-23_collaborative-agile-activities]] adapts ideation to Agile
constraints with three lightweight activities. 6-up 1-up: five minutes of
individual sketching for six rapid ideas, group discussion of themes, five
minutes refining one chosen solution, then a group review that picks a hybrid,
suited to larger teams and features far down the backlog. Whiteboard sessions:
informal real-time work with clear goals and acceptance criteria, suited to
small teams and near-term sprints needing immediate alignment. Telephone:
passing a sketch around, each person adding for one or two minutes, which
generates diversity and builds comfort with building on others' work. It ties
format choice to urgency (focused one-on-one work for the current sprint, larger
brainstorms during lulls for distant backlog items) and advises introducing the
activities incrementally by carving time out of existing ceremonies.

[[2019-12-22_how-to-get-stakeholders-to-sketch]] targets the specific obstacle
of non-designers who say they cannot draw, with a four-variable formula: fat
markers (thick tips block detail and keep output rough enough to invite
conversation), tiny spaces (index cards, sticky notes, 8-up sheets, so shrinking
the space shrinks the anxiety), time limits (called the most important variable,
with visible countdown timers, since people expand to fill available time with
no gain in quality or quantity), and ugly examples (deliberately crude
reference sketches, because polished examples signal that sketching belongs to
creative roles). It adds a vocabulary note: calling the activity "sketching"
rather than "designing" lowers the barrier. The benefits it claims are buy-in,
idea diversity, shared ownership, faster process, and exposing non-designers to
design work.

[[2016-01-03_ux-deliverables-collaboration]] adds who to invite and why:
developers for technical feasibility, product and business stakeholders for
requirements and goals, and other UX professionals for fresh perspective, with
in-person whiteboard sessions and sketching charrettes named as the effective
formats. It also cites the evaluator effect, that different evaluators find
different problems, as a reason to put more eyes on the work, and the four-eyes
principle of showing unfinished deliverables to colleagues before stakeholders,
as a cheaper way to catch blind spots than spending user testing on them.
[[2017-07-02_facilitating-design-studio-workshop]] and
[[2017-01-15_ux-ideation]] agree on the composition rule: diverse backgrounds
and expertise widen the solution space and expose blind spots a single
discipline would miss, and [[2017-10-29_ideation-in-practice]] adds that this
does not require a crowd.

### Breaking fixation

[[2017-07-30_functional-fixedness]] treats fixation as the enemy of divergence.
Functional fixedness is a cognitive bias that drives people to use objects only
in their traditional ways; it strengthens with practice and age, so experience
that should broaden perspective narrows it instead (five-year-olds solve the
classic candle-mounting puzzle faster than older children). Its remedies:
abstract the problem by stripping surface details and domain vocabulary, which
reveals structurally similar but distant fields (a power-outlet spacing problem
restated abstractly becomes a space-packing challenge, answerable from
contortionism, landscaping, or Japanese aesthetics); involve multiple
disciplines, since hearing other perspectives jostles you off a single solution;
and delay judgment, generating many options including unlikely ones before
converging.

[[2017-01-15_ux-ideation]] adds two practical habits in the same direction:
record every idea on a tangible artifact, both to keep it from evaporating and
to seed further ideas in the session, and step away between generation and
evaluation, which avoids deciding while fatigued and marks the phase change. Its
argument for tolerating extreme ideas is that scaling back a crazy idea that
addresses a true user need is easier than making a mundane idea desirable.

[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_12-step-4-list-ideas-what]] attacks the
same reflex from the solution side: its worked example names the obvious answer
first — for bike navigation on Waze, another mode inside the existing mobile
app — precisely so the designer can step past it by varying the solution's
platform and interface.

### Remote and distributed ideation

[[2019-07-28_synchronous-asynchronous-ideation]] states that remote ideation can
be just as effective as in-person work and opens more structural options.
Synchronous (video, calls, screen-sharing) gives fast back-and-forth, focused
attention inside a time limit, and team bonding, and suits quick decisions,
complex problems needing discussion, and tight deadlines; its costs are
scheduling across time zones and workloads, remote-meeting awkwardness
(simultaneous talking, long silences), and the difficulty of finding tools that
support several people sketching at once. Asynchronous (shared document, thread,
or tool) removes the scheduling problem and lets ideas incubate, and suits
longer timelines and dispersed teams; its costs are less team building, fading
attention over days or weeks, and time spent re-reading prior contributions.
That source declines to rank them and recommends choosing on deadline, timezone
spread, and complexity, or blending: a synchronous kickoff to frame the problem,
an open asynchronous period for contributions, then synchronous convergence.

### Fidelity and attachment

[[2026-05-22_design-disposables]] argues against polishing early exploration.
Designers fall into a deliverable trap, treating every artifact as something to
be seen and judged, which slows the process and deepens attachment to unproven
ideas. It distinguishes disposables (made to help you think, allowed to be
wrong) from deliverables (made to communicate, deserving care), and insists the
difference lies in the intention rather than in the artifact. Its mechanism is
the sunk-cost fallacy: once significant time is invested, designers defend flawed
ideas instead of discarding them, so keeping explorations rough is what keeps
them questionable. Its practical test is to ask, before making anything, whether
this is for me to think with or for someone else to understand. It also warns
that AI compounds the problem in a specific way: generating the artifact removes
the friction that produces learning, so you arrive at an output without having
gone through the making. The formula in
[[2019-12-22_how-to-get-stakeholders-to-sketch]] works on the same logic from
the other end, using physical constraints to make polish impossible.

### Generating solutions in a design exercise

[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_12-step-4-list-ideas-what]] gives a
generation method rather than a session format, aimed at a designer working
alone through a product design exercise once the audience and the context of
use are settled. The method is to vary the properties of the solution instead of
elaborating the first one that comes to mind: its type (digital or physical),
its platform (smartwatch, smartphone, desktop, VR), and its interface type
(graphic, audio or voice, VR, AR). Bike navigation for Waze is the worked
example: property variation turns one obvious mobile-app mode into a physical
steering-wheel device with LEDs, a smartwatch app using haptic feedback, and a
smartphone app giving voice guidance from the rider's pocket. Platforms and
interfaces are meant to be combined rather than chosen separately, since the
platform is what makes a given interface possible. That source also supplies a
template for getting unstuck, `Build X for <Who / Step 2>, that <When and Where
/ Step 3> to <Why / Step 1>`, which composes the audience, context, and
motivation established in the earlier steps into a candidate product.

Its quantity target is much lower than the rest of the corpus's. Three to four
possible products are enough, against the quantity-over-quality rule of
[[2017-01-15_ux-ideation]] and the 15-20 diverse ideas that
[[2021-05-18_continuous-discovery-habits_11-chapter-eight-supercharged-ideation]]
has a team reach, through alternating solo and group cycles, before it filters
and dot-votes down to three finalists. The contexts are not the same — a solo
exercise under interview conditions against a team's discovery cycle — but the
two sources are not saying the same thing about how many ideas are enough.

[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_21-3-4-improving-primary-health-care]]
shows the method producing structurally different candidates rather than
variants of one: a family-doctor marketplace, on-demand chat, group
appointments, and improved appointment scheduling, each attacking a different
part of the same problem. Convergence there runs on an impact/effort matrix,
and the winner is picked for novelty and leverage — group appointments let one
GP educate several patients at once — over the two ideas that already exist on
the market and the one that only improves administrative efficiency.

[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_24-chapter-5-tasks-list]] is the practice
material for all of this: a collated library of 30+ open-ended prompts spanning
fintech, transportation, healthcare, e-commerce, social, real estate, and media,
at scales from a single-screen flow to a platform, an ecosystem, or a
policy-adjacent system, with a recurring emphasis on underserved audiences.
Many prompts carry explicit constraints — every technology suggested has to be
available on the market today, the solution has to be mobile-friendly, six-month
market viability, a named metric target such as retention or a new revenue
driver — though the chapter states them without arguing what they are for.

### Finding a startup idea

[[2026-03-05_10x-method_02-finding-and-validating-the-idea]] argues against the
"1% idea, 99% execution" view: the idea is the map, and execution in the wrong
direction is still the wrong direction. Where it differs from the rest of this
page is that it never describes a session. Ideas come from staying close to real
problems and noticing pain points others overlook, and from action, building,
talking to users, testing tools, rather than from sitting and waiting for
inspiration. It calls the capacity an "idea muscle", a habit built over years of
engagement with the world, not an output a workshop produces on demand. It also
detaches the idea from its author: ideas belong to their time, emerging at the
intersection of cultural shifts, new technologies and evolving user behaviours,
so the advantage lies in recognising when an idea's moment has arrived and
moving fast, rather than in being first to think of it.

It sorts startup ideas into three categories, each demanding a different search
and a different kind of evidence: problem-solution fit, drawn from personal
frustration or industry knowledge; a market or technology shift, where a new
capability allows a dramatically better solution to a known problem; and
category creation, where a change in behaviour opens an entirely new way of
doing things. Its filter on what is worth pursuing is narrow but generous:
innovation in at least one of three dimensions, problem discovery, solution
creation, or go-to-market, and excelling in just one of them can already create
substantial value. What follows generation, in that source, is the validation
sequence held in [[Idea Validation]].

### AI in ideation

Three sources cover AI as an ideation partner, and they agree on its shape while
diverging on its weight.

[[2024-06-25_334_Les_4_outils_IA_pour_designers_product,_UX_&_UI_en_2024]]
treats it as an assistant rather than a replacement: a conversational model
(Mistral le chat, presented as a French alternative to ChatGPT) works as a
creative rebound partner, generating many variations from one initial idea to
unblock creativity, without needing elaborate prompts. That record is explicit
that none of the tools it covers replaces the designer's work, and adds a cost
heuristic: if writing the prompt takes 45 minutes, doing the work yourself would
have been faster.

[[2025-05-23_ai-creative-teammate]] reports a 2025 study of 776 Procter & Gamble
employees working individually or in pairs, with or without AI. Teams with AI
produced both the highest average quality and a disproportionate share of the
top 10% of ideas; individuals with AI beat both individuals and teams without AI
on average and matched AI teams on quantity and detail. Its conclusion is that
AI does not make teams obsolete, that organisations chasing breakthrough
solutions should prioritise team-based AI ideation, and that AI should be
treated as a teammate rather than a tool, which demands facilitation different
from traditional workshops. It also bounds the use case: AI suits ideation
precisely because truth is not the primary criterion there, unlike data analysis
where hallucination is unacceptable.

[[2025-06-06_facilitating-ai-workshops]] supplies that facilitation. Its core
observation is that AI generates hundreds of ideas in minutes, which makes the
standard diverge-converge structure unmanageable, and that human and AI ideation
differ in kind: humans fixate on early ideas and drift toward consensus while AI
produces diverse options fast. Its modifications: insert an intermediate step
where AI ranks its own output against criteria before group discussion; prepare
those evaluation criteria with the group in advance (feasibility, desirability,
viability, or custom); allow more time rather than less, 8-10 minutes per prompt
against 5 without AI, for writing, reading, and iterating; have small groups
co-write prompts, since the discussion while drafting is often as valuable as the
output; and document digitally, because AI's long outputs do not fit sticky
notes. It explicitly rejects the obvious shortcut of capping how many ideas the
AI produces, on the grounds that this undermines its main strength and violates
the ideation mindset.

Set against this, [[2026-05-22_design-disposables]] cautions that AI accelerates
artifact production without accelerating the thinking that should accompany it.
The corpus does not reconcile the two: the enthusiasm is about idea volume and
quality, the caution is about what the designer learns while making.
