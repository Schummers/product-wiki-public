# Storytelling

## Definition

Storytelling in UX is the use of narrative to communicate what users
experience, why a design decision was made, and why anyone else should care.
A UX story is an account of events from the user's perspective showing how an
experience evolves, and it differs from a use case or a scenario in that it
emphasises character, motivation and emotion rather than a sequence of actions
[[2017-01-15_ux-stories]]. Its justification is cognitive and political at
once: people remember facts better when laced together in a coherent story than
presented in isolation, and raw data — figures, quotes, charts — does not by
itself convince anyone [[2017-01-15_ux-stories]],
[[2019-04-28_persuasive-storytelling]], [[2022-04-03_engaging-reports-presentations]].

The corpus treats it as a working skill rather than a flourish. Stories run
across the whole design process — summarising research, generating ideas
through "what-if" exploration, conveying abstract concepts, persuading
stakeholders, and evaluating a design against a baseline narrative
[[2017-01-15_ux-stories]] — down to the level of justifying a single design
decision in a stakeholder meeting, where a user-testing story is treated as a
stronger argument than a spreadsheet
[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_08-chapter-7-choose-a-message]].
They also extend to the practitioner's own career,
in job interviews and portfolio case studies
[[2022-12-04_answer-ux-job-interview-questions]],
[[2024-12-03_354_Les_6_tips_de_présentation_d_un_case_study_design_-_Guide_Product_Design]].
[[2025-06-06_future-proof-designer]] goes further, naming storytelling one of
the uniquely human capabilities that keeps designers relevant as AI absorbs
tactical execution. The recurring caveat, and the corpus's sharpest internal
tension, is that the same narrative power that makes stories persuasive makes
them dangerous when they are not grounded in research
[[2017-02-19_narrative-biases]].

## Practice

### What a UX story is made of

[[2017-01-15_ux-stories]] names six components: character (the user),
goal or motivation, context (time and place), plot (the series of events),
insight (why it matters, the pain points), and spectacle (how it is presented).
Its quality bar is simplicity — a good story is understandable across levels of
expertise and age, and complexity obscures rather than clarifies.

[[2022-05-15_two-tips-better-ux-storytelling]] adds two structural tools:

- **The story triangle** — story, storyteller and audience. Storytelling is a
  conversation, not a broadcast: the same story lands differently depending on
  each listener's prior experience, so invite feedback and expect
  misinterpretation. The audience actively fills in what is missing, which is
  both the emotional opportunity and the risk.
- **The story mountain** (adapted from Freytag's pyramid) — exposition
  establishing user and setting, rising action presenting the problem, climax
  as the turning point, falling action showing the resolution steps, and
  resolution showing the outcome. Structure is what makes recommendations
  memorable and actionable.

The same source insists on calibrating detail: too few details leave room for
distraction, too many feel rigid and limit emotional involvement, and the right
amount depends on the goal. [[2019-04-28_persuasive-storytelling]] makes the
same point from the other side — rather than describing everything users want,
leave the audience some freedom to envision the solution.

### Rules for persuading with a story

[[2019-04-28_persuasive-storytelling]] gives six:

1. **Adapt vocabulary to the audience** — learn their terminology, processes
   and concerns; unfamiliar language loses attention and costs credibility.
2. **Appeal to their needs** — budget, timeline, user satisfaction, whatever
   they actually care about, addressed early; frame around their priorities,
   not your design preferences.
3. **Back up points with real data** — qualitative or quantitative insights
   with real usability-test quotes, so there is an answer when the
   recommendation is questioned.
4. **Focus on the entire experience** — before, during and after the
   interaction, including the responsibilities and distractions users face.
5. **Pair the story with artifacts** — storyboards, personas, journey maps,
   research reports give people something tangible to recall later.
6. **Follow up with a summary** — reinforcing decisions and rationale creates
   a reference for future discussions.

[[2017-12-10_bad-design-suggestions]] covers the adversarial case, when the
communication is a refusal. Its stance is that even a bad suggestion may point
at a real problem, so separate the proposed solution from the underlying
problem, acknowledge the idea's benefits alongside its costs, and buy time by
offering to research it rather than saying no on the spot. Rejection should
explain the reasoning from UX research and principles, turning it into a
teaching moment — the moment someone is engaged enough to make a suggestion is
the best moment to spread UX knowledge. For powerful stakeholders it recommends
an "aikido" move: agree to explore the suggestion, then present alternatives
that reach the same goal without the problematic design. It also recommends
soliciting stakeholder input early and on a schedule, so late-stage surprise
demands become rarer.

### Stories as justification for a single design decision

[[2020-08-03_articulating-design-decisions_08-chapter-7-choose-a-message]]
comes at storytelling from inside a stakeholder meeting rather than from a
research readout. The chapter catalogues reusable messages that justify a
design decision, sorted into four categories — Business (goal, use case,
branding), Design (common pattern, attention, flow), Research (data, testing,
external research) and Limitations (resources, technology, standards) — so a
designer is not reinventing a justification on the spot. Storytelling is its
answer inside the Research category: user-testing stories built on real
observations, anecdotes and video clips are often more compelling than
spreadsheets, because they ground abstract design principles in human
experience, create empathy, and show that users actually informed the work.
Its stated reason is the same as [[2017-01-15_ux-stories]]'s — stories are
remembered and retold better than statistics.

That chapter is also where the corpus's confidence in raw data is highest. It
calls data "the golden ticket to getting support", on the grounds that it is
the most scientific way to demonstrate a design has the intended effect, while
warning that interpretation involves assumptions, so show your reasoning rather
than overinterpret. [[2022-04-03_engaging-reports-presentations]] starts from
the opposite premise, explicitly attacking the belief that data speaks for
itself. In practice the two prescribe the same thing — pair the evidence with
the narrative — but they disagree about which half does the convincing.

### Turning research data into a narrative

[[2020-07-19_complex-data-compelling-stories]] gives a five-step process:

1. Ground the research in solid questions, and frame the story around what you
   set out to discover rather than around what happened in the study.
2. List every finding as a bullet.
3. Cluster findings into themes — explicitly resisting the study's own
   chronology (task 1, task 2, survey), which turns an account of what
   participants did into a story about user needs.
4. Decide what to include or leave out.
5. Translate each theme into a descriptive, user-focused headline with
   supporting statements, tested by a simple check: reading the headlines in
   sequence should still tell the whole story.

Two integrity rules come with it: include contradictory findings rather than
omitting them, examining why they contradict (methodology, context) and saying
plainly when something is unclear; and keep tangential-but-interesting findings
in a separate pile, reintroduced under next steps or future research rather
than discarded.

### Formats and delivery

- **Asynchronous reports and decks.** [[2022-04-03_engaging-reports-presentations]]
  targets the case where you cannot narrate live. Structure the report for
  three reading levels — headlines for executives, themes for stakeholders,
  detailed data for specialists. Use anecdotes to create emotional connection
  (supporting, not replacing, quantitative evidence, since people depersonalise
  large numbers), find relatable analogies that make abstract statistics
  tangible, break up text with checklists, charts, quotes and images, and
  consider nonlinear "choose-your-own-adventure" formats serving several
  audiences. It explicitly attacks the belief that data speaks for itself:
  laying out all the facts does not guarantee agreement.
- **Remote presentations.** [[2017-09-24_presenting-remotely]] notes that 80%
  of corporate presentations are delivered remotely, which removes the physical
  presence normally carrying energy. Its fifth strategy is to turn the content
  into a story built around user research, journey and conflict, because
  stories are what audiences remember after sign-off. The surrounding
  mechanics: stand while presenting and use a decent microphone, put presenter
  photos on slides so voices get faces, keep one major idea per slide because
  remote viewers multitask and need to regain context, and control the
  environment against distractions.
- **Storyboards.** [[2018-07-15_storyboards-visualize-ideas]] defines a
  storyboard as a story told through sequential panels, using images to make it
  understandable at a glance and easy to remember. Three elements: a specific
  scenario, visuals, captions. Start low-fidelity — sketches or sticky notes,
  easy to iterate, no polish unless presenting to a client. Unlike journey
  maps, storyboards favour imagery over text and usually depict a fragment
  rather than the whole journey, and the two complement each other. Its
  six-step process: gather data, choose fidelity, define persona and scenario,
  plan the steps with emotional states, create visuals and captions, then
  distribute and iterate — with one path per storyboard, splitting complex
  scenarios into separate boards.
- **Wireframes.** [[2021-06-20_draw-wireframe-even-if-you-cant-draw]] treats
  low-fidelity wireframes as a communication device whose messiness is a
  feature: rough sketches invite feedback on the concept rather than on
  aesthetics, and thick pens, time-boxing and limited space stop you
  over-polishing. Anyone can produce useful ones by learning component
  conventions (thick lines for headers, thin for body text, rectangle-with-an-X
  for images, caret for dropdowns) rather than by learning to draw, working
  from the device aspect ratio, to navigation and search, to the largest
  elements, to the details. Add text labels to primary CTAs so reviewers get
  the key information.

### The grounding problem

[[2017-01-15_ux-stories]] states the condition directly: stories must stay
grounded in real user research, because fabricated ones risk satisfying
imaginary needs, and they need regular validation against actual users.

[[2017-02-19_narrative-biases]] is the corpus's counterweight to everything
above — the one source that argues storytelling can actively harm UX.
Narrative bias is the tendency to read information as part of a larger story
whether or not the facts support it, and the two elements that make a story
persuasive are exactly the two that mislead: **specific details** (which
override base rates — 41% of surveyed UX practitioners fell for this) and
**cause-and-effect explanations** (which feel more plausible than randomness,
so a high exit rate on a low-traffic page reads as a design flaw when the
sample is far too small to support causation). It notes that simply knowing
about the bias does not immunise you. Its remedies: derive memorable details
from actual research across multiple data sources, even if only a quick survey
checks a basic assumption; and for implicit stories the team is telling itself
— the "digital natives figure out any interface" narrative, for example —
force them into the open through formal problem definition so the assumption
can be challenged.

The two positions are reconcilable but genuinely in tension: the persuasion
sources treat narrative as the way to make findings land, while
[[2017-02-19_narrative-biases]] treats it as the mechanism by which a team can
convince itself of something untrue. Both agree the discriminator is whether
the story traces back to data.

### Storytelling as the alternative to written requirements

[[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_11-6-the-real-story-about-stories]] and
[[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_12-7-telling-better-stories]] use "story" in a
different but related sense from the rest of this page: not a narrative told
about research or a design decision, but the conversational practice at the
root of the Agile "user story." The two chapters trace that practice back to
Kent Beck, whose insight was that document-driven requirements fail because
different readers imagine different solutions from the same document; telling
stories together, in conversation, generates the shared understanding that a
written spec cannot. Patton frames stories by how they are *used* rather than
how they are written down: Ron Jeffries's "3 Cs" (Card, Conversation,
Confirmation) structure a story around a minimal written card that starts the
conversation, the conversation itself where understanding is actually built,
and a confirmation step that agrees on acceptance criteria. The Connextra "as
a... I want... so that..." template is, in this framing, only a conversation
starter — Patton warns against becoming a "template zombie," a term he takes
from Tom DeMarco et al.'s *Adrenaline Junkies and Template Zombies*, forcing every
idea into the template or refusing to treat something as a real story unless
it is written that way, which reverts to the same document-driven thinking
the practice was meant to replace.

[[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_12-7-telling-better-stories]] gives the
practical content of a good story conversation: talk specifically about who
(user types, customers, other stakeholders), what (tasks, but also backend
services and edge cases), why (pushed with a "why stick" through several
layers), context (where, when, who else is present), failure modes,
assumptions, and whether a better solution exists. Patton recommends recording
these conversations as sketches and whiteboard photos — "vacation photos" —
rather than relying on the written card, since the real value of a story
conversation is what the team learns together, not what ends up written down.
This is a narrower, agile-process meaning of storytelling than the
communication and persuasion practice described above, but it shares the same
underlying claim as [[2017-01-15_ux-stories]] and
[[2019-04-28_persuasive-storytelling]]: a narrative told and discussed together
sticks and aligns people in a way that an isolated written artifact does not.

### Storytelling about your own work

- **Job interviews.** [[2022-12-04_answer-ux-job-interview-questions]] cites
  research that candidates who answer with stories get better hiring
  recommendations, while most candidates use stories only 23% of the time. Its
  structures: **STAR** (Situation, Tasks, Actions, Results) for behavioural
  questions, kept brief, specific and quantifiable; **METEOR** (Musing,
  Enquiring, Theorizing, Endeavoring, Outcomes, Recap) for situational ones.
  It recommends sharing experiences where you stumbled and learned, since
  self-awareness reads better than unrealistic perfection, and building the
  skill through recorded mock interviews restructured to fit the frameworks.
- **Case studies.**
  [[2024-12-03_354_Les_6_tips_de_présentation_d_un_case_study_design_-_Guide_Product_Design]]
  applies the same logic to design case-study presentations: tell the project
  as a story including its twists, failures and successes, which is what makes
  it unique and holds attention. Its other tips: tie design choices to
  quantified business stakes rather than to aesthetic preference, present the
  research learnings that actually shaped the solution instead of every
  deliverable (personas, user journeys), avoid parading a standardised process
  in favour of the methods you adapted to that context, show iterations
  briefly to expose your reasoning, and finish on real-world impact measured by
  data, NPS or qualitative feedback rather than stopping at the mockup.
- **Career positioning.** [[2025-06-06_future-proof-designer]] frames
  storytelling as irreplaceable: narratives connecting solutions to business
  outcomes and user needs cut through noise and position designers as strategic
  leaders, alongside emotional intelligence and audience awareness —
  understanding stakeholder motivations and speaking their language — as human
  strengths AI lacks. Its instruction is to be able to tell the story of why
  your design matters to customers and to the business.
