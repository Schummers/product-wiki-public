# Design Hiring

## Definition

Design hiring is how organisations find, assess and select product designers,
and how designers position themselves to be selected. Three works in this corpus
describe it from different ends of the table. Artiom Dashinsky writes as a design
leader who interviewed dozens of candidates and found that many could not connect
design to business strategy, so his account maps the process stage by stage and
gives candidates and hiring managers a shared vocabulary for it
([[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_02-why-i-wrote-this-book]],
[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_03-why-should-you-read-this-book]]). The
Nielsen Norman Group interview with recruiter Hang Xu describes the market
conditions around that process and what candidates misunderstand about it
([[2026-01-16_ux-hiring-insights]]). Dashinsky's later *The Path to Senior Product
Designer* takes a third position: hiring as a competency an individual contributor
can practise, not only a thing managers do
([[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_21-19-hiring-leadership]]).

Across both, hiring is treated as an assessment problem rather than a filtering
formality: the question is which signals actually predict a designer's value, and
which artefacts (portfolio, exercise, resume) carry those signals. Dashinsky
frames the recurring failure as an education gap made visible at interview time,
since design schools and employers do not teach the business-facing skills the
interview tests ([[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_02-why-i-wrote-this-book]]).

## Practice

### The shape of the process

[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_04-chapter-1-introducing-the-design-exercise]]
describes a seven-stage process typical of tech companies: a recruiter phone
screen of 10 to 20 minutes to assess basic fit; an interview covering process and
team dynamics; a portfolio review; an optional product critique; the
[[Design Exercise]], named as the key decision point; an optional informal
follow-up interview; and the offer. Only steps three to five are specific to
designers. Portfolio review and design exercise test different things: the first
walks through completed work with its context, objectives, contributions and
reasoning, the second tests problem-solving in real time.

That structure is a general shape, not a standard.
[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_05-what-is-a-design-exercise]] notes that
processes vary significantly across companies and within the same company over
time, citing one designer who interviewed at Google, Palantir, Facebook, Apple
and Kayak and met five completely different processes.

[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_21-19-hiring-leadership]] maps the
same terrain with the ends attached: sourcing, portfolio screening, a screening
call, a professional interview, a professional evaluation, a senior management
interview, the offer, and onboarding. Its addition is that the process does not
stop at the signature, since a new hire draws a salary before they can generate
value and onboarding exists to close that gap.

### What the process is trying to measure

- **Business-connected problem solving, not execution.** The motivating
  observation in [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_02-why-i-wrote-this-book]]
  is that candidates failed because they could not connect design to business
  strategy. The chapter attributes the wider confusion to the "Dribbblisation of
  Design": communities that celebrate visual work create the belief that product
  design equals visual design. It uses Julie Zhuo's three career stages, craft and
  execution, then [[Product Thinking]], then influencing skills, to locate what is
  missing.
- **Behaviour under interview conditions.**
  [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_05-what-is-a-design-exercise]] lists
  communication effectiveness, critical thinking and questioning, handling
  feedback, performance under pressure, and whether the candidate is someone the
  team wants to collaborate with daily.
- **Explicit evaluation criteria.**
  [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_23-chapter-4-how-to-use-a-design-exercise-when-interviewing]]
  gives the interviewer a checklist: does the candidate ask questions, take
  feedback well, communicate clearly, follow a process, make valid assumptions,
  connect the solution to the company mission, think about audience and
  [[Context of Use]], explore non-obvious solutions, and define success metrics.
- **Craft, which is not one thing.** [[2026-01-16_ux-hiring-insights]] reports
  that "craft" means different things to different reviewers: invisible details in
  the Steve Jobs sense, visual impressiveness for newer designers, or holistic
  attention to detail. The evaluation criterion is therefore reviewer-dependent.

### Running the process as a hiring manager

[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_23-chapter-4-how-to-use-a-design-exercise-when-interviewing]]
is the operational chapter for the employer side:

- **Decide what you are testing before choosing an exercise.** The recommended
  arrangement is two exercises for two skill sets: a take-home (4 to 8 hours of
  work, a 2 to 7 day deadline) for visual and deliverable skills, and an on-site
  exercise, whiteboarding or quiet thinking, for product thinking and
  problem-solving under pressure.
- **Make the candidate comfortable without softening the test.** Clear logistics,
  a friendly and empathetic demeanour, and one deliberate pushback to see how
  criticism is handled. The chapter warns against leaving a stuck candidate
  feeling trapped.
- **Do not use your own product as the exercise.** Three reasons are given:
  familiarity gives the interviewer a biased advantage, the candidate is pulled
  towards the interviewer's existing perspective, and it can feel like unpaid work.
- **Source tasks deliberately.** Ideas come from market opportunities identified
  through industry research, real problems found in news, Product Hunt or idea
  communities, or established companies' business needs and underserved user
  segments.

[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_30-additional-resources]] points hiring
managers to external material on hiring designers, writing job descriptions and
evaluating UX skills, and notes a trend towards transparency: companies such as
BuzzFeed publish their hiring process and designer levelling, with frameworks from
Khosla Ventures and Paul Adams for evaluating design skill.

### Market conditions on the candidate side

[[2026-01-16_ux-hiring-insights]] describes a market where power has returned to
employers after the 2021 to 2022 hiring boom: processes are long, take-home
assignments are back, and candidates spend 10 to 30 times more time on hiring than
hiring managers do. Its practical corrections to candidate folklore:

- **Your first reviewer is usually not a designer.** Recruiters, product managers
  or founders read the resume, looking for solutions to their problems rather than
  a lesson in what design is.
- **Applicant tracking systems are not the filter.** They track data but do not
  decide interviews; humans review resumes, often in real time as they arrive.
  Fast rejections come from knockout questions, real-time review, or a role already
  filled. The source treats "ATS-friendly" as a red flag and argues for legibility
  instead, including using a standard tool such as Google Docs rather than Figma so
  the document parses and applying stays cheap.
- **Portfolios address the hiring manager, not other designers.** Legibility beats
  visual complexity (see [[Portfolio]]).

The two works are eight years apart and their accounts of the take-home reflect
that. [[2026-01-16_ux-hiring-insights]] reports that take-home design exercises
disappeared almost overnight during the 2022 spree, because candidates would not
progress through a process that included one, and have since returned;
[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_23-chapter-4-how-to-use-a-design-exercise-when-interviewing]]
treats the take-home as a standard instrument. Read together they describe an
instrument whose availability follows the balance of power, not a settled practice.

### How much to tailor: the sources disagree

[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_28-joel-califa-two-tips-for-getting-a-job]]
argues for treating the job search as a design process, with the candidate as the
product and the hiring manager as the user: research each hiring manager's
background, the team's values, previous hires and the job posting's language, and
customise the application accordingly, because "spray and pray" does not work for
competitive roles. It also stresses polishing CV, portfolio and cover letter,
since a hiring manager reviewing dozens or hundreds of applications a week
develops pattern recognition and pet peeves.

[[2026-01-16_ux-hiring-insights]] agrees on the "understand your audience" premise
but rejects the workload: tailoring every resume is unsustainable, so the
candidate should assess their market position, identify their primary audience,
and concentrate effort where returns are highest. The disagreement is about
coverage, not about method.

On the longer horizon,
[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_28-joel-califa-two-tips-for-getting-a-job]]
recommends authentic networking over transactional networking: connect with people
genuinely, help without expecting a return, and maintain a reputation, an
investment measured in years that makes both getting in the door and getting the
job easier. [[2026-01-16_ux-hiring-insights]] adds that a candidate must judge
when to talk about personal design beliefs and when to set them aside.

### Behavioural questions

[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_30-additional-resources]] records that many
designers report being intimidated by behavioural questions such as "What is your
greatest strength?" or a request to describe a stressful situation. Its own
observation is that these questions are not specific to design interviews, which
is why general preparation material applies.

### Hiring is itself a designer skill

Both the framing chapters and the guest chapters treat interviewing as something
designers learn, not only something done to them.
[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_03-why-should-you-read-this-book]] addresses
mid-level and senior designers who need to learn to interview others, and business
leaders who need to define what they expect from a designer and how to evaluate
candidates and role scope.
[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_26-justin-maxwell-advice-for-designers-who-want-to-become]]
places hiring and interviewing among the people skills a designer should build
while still an employee, alongside active listening and critical inquiry, and adds
that going through rejection and hiring failures builds the resilience founder
life demands.
[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_28-joel-califa-two-tips-for-getting-a-job]]
supplies the counterpart view from inside: what a design leader notices when
reviewing applications at volume (see also [[UX Career]]).

[[2023-09-05_path-to-senior-product-designer_21-19-hiring-leadership]] turns that
framing into a route an individual contributor can walk, and is blunt about why
designers have an opening: it holds that an average recruiter's job is to browse
LinkedIn and message people until someone answers, whereas a designer already sits
inside the design community, through professional networks, meetups and former
colleagues. Its concrete moves:

- **Source from where you already are.** Keep a constantly updated list of
  portfolios of designers you would like to work with, notice when people become
  reachable (layoffs, unhappiness in a current role), and introduce strong
  candidates to HR or the hiring manager proactively.
- **Take the beginning of the pipeline first.** Offer to do the initial portfolio
  evaluation, research candidates beyond the portfolio on Dribbble, Behance and
  their case studies, and give thoughtful feedback; one approach it suggests is a
  weekly email of sourced candidates, growing into the curator role. Credibility
  earned there is what opens the later steps.
- **Structure the professional interview.** Explain the meeting's structure to the
  candidate, explain the company and team, answer their questions, listen to them
  describe their background and approach, and summarise next steps. The chapter
  reads two signals from this: their questions reveal what they prioritise, and
  the way they talk about past employers and teams reveals attitude and cultural
  fit (see [[Organizational Culture]]).
- **Get into the evaluation gradually.** It reports that a designer's professional
  level is normally assessed through a whiteboard challenge, a take-home exercise
  or a portfolio review, sometimes combined, and suggests entering by proposing
  exercise ideas, answering candidate questions during a take-home, or giving
  candidates feedback, before owning evaluation decisions.
- **Fix onboarding, which nobody owns.** Interview hires from the last six months
  about what confused or frustrated them and what they wish they had known, then
  document team structure and goals, design processes, design systems, handoff
  standards, product and competition knowledge and customer insights, connect new
  hires with domain experts, and include practical details such as lunch
  recommendations.

The chapter's argument for spending the time is career-shaped rather than
altruistic: hiring is "normally something managers are heavily involved in, so
it's also a great way to get a taste of the management role", and it presents
participation as one of the best available ways to build organizational impact
(see [[Design Leadership]]). It acknowledges in the same breath that the time
commitment is substantial.
