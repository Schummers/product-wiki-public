# Studio Team

## Definition

The studio team is the permanent group of people a [[Venture Studio]] employs to build several startups at once, as distinct from the founders and entrepreneurs in residence who lead a single venture. Szigeti treats assembling it as a harder problem than building a startup's founding team, because its members work on multiple companies simultaneously and must therefore cover every competency a startup needs (development, design, marketing, sales, legal, finance, HR) while accepting that projects run in parallel, some fail, and people move between them ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_18-core-team]]). Betaworks illustrates the same idea in practice: a core team with data science, design, branding, distribution and product expertise that founders draw on for iteration, fundraising and business development ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_05-emerging-from-beta]]).

Kannan and Peterman approach the team from the top and from its consequences. At formation, they recommend partnerships that combine serial entrepreneurs, investors and product-development professionals, because no single founder type brings every capability venture building requires ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_04-studio-formation]]). Downstream, the size of the core team and its internal ideation capacity determine how a studio can recruit external founders ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_08-working-with-founders]]). Both books agree that the team's competency spread is what lets a studio absorb work that founders would otherwise do themselves; they differ on scale, with Szigeti describing full in-house coverage and Kannan and Peterman describing studios that vary from robust research and business-design teams to much lighter cores.

## Practice

### Which competencies to hold in-house

Szigeti's rule is that a studio covers every competency a startup needs internally, and that this is a cost-optimisation strategy rather than a luxury: spreading specialised roles across several ventures shrinks the per-startup payroll compared with independent startups each hiring a full team ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_18-core-team]]). Betaworks' core team, as described in its case study, spans data science, design, branding, distribution and product, and it handles back-office operations, fundraising coordination and administration so that product-focused founder CEOs can keep coding and iterating ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_05-emerging-from-beta]]).

Kannan and Peterman's interviews describe studios with different depths of internal team. Some run robust internal ideation with research and business-design teams and can assign a founder to a pre-validated concept; those with lighter internal processes rely instead on structured founder programs or a limited number of EIR slots and on founder-led ideation ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_08-working-with-founders]]). The choice of recruitment model therefore follows from core-team size, ideation capacity and network depth, not the other way round. See [[Founder Recruitment]] and [[Entrepreneur in Residence]].

### How to structure the team across ventures

Szigeti reports three organisational patterns, chosen according to studio strategy and portfolio size ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_18-core-team]]):

- Fully dedicated teams per venture (the Betaworks model).
- Centralised competencies with rotating leadership (Quasar Ventures).
- Almost entirely shared resources with only the CEO dedicated to a venture (Drukka).

Whatever the structure, he advises tracking allocation across projects with simple time-management tools so that effort stays transparent, and adopting standard tooling (Slack, Dropbox, GSuite, Jira) rather than building studio-specific infrastructure. Resource allocation is described as psychological as much as logistical: team members and CEOs must accept that several projects run in parallel, that some fail, and that people are moved between initiatives, and early studios struggle most with that mindset shift. See [[Resource Allocation]].

### Composition at the founding level

Kannan and Peterman identify three backgrounds from which independent studios originate, each with a characteristic gap ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_04-studio-formation]]):

- **Serial entrepreneurs** (the most common; Expa, High Alpha) are strong at ideation, founder recruiting and startup operations, but often lack asset-management and fundraising experience, which hinders raising an associated fund.
- **Investors from venture capital** (Pioneer Square Labs, Sutter Hill Ventures) bring market knowledge, pattern recognition, asset management and fundraising ability, but less hands-on experience with early-stage ideation and building idea funnels.
- **Product development and engineering agencies** (Coplex, Revelry) bring technical expertise, rapid MVP development and testing proficiency, but limited experience with market validation, founder recruiting and scaling beyond the build.

Because venture development spans ideation, market validation, technical development, recruiting, asset management and fundraising, the authors recommend that new studio partnerships draw from all three areas. See [[Studio Leadership]].

### Attracting and retaining talent

Szigeti's levers are compensation, trust and flexibility ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_18-core-team]]):

- **Salary first.** Base compensation is salary, paid well enough to attract and retain people who will build multiple ventures.
- **Portfolio equity.** Consider an equity pool that gives every team member a symbolic share in all portfolio companies, so that any venture's success benefits everyone. See [[Team Compensation]].
- **Trust and room to act.** To produce more than a dozen ventures, leadership has to give the team room; micromanagement dilutes responsibility and wastes the founder's time on execution instead of strategy. The culture must generate ownership beyond equity, allow safe experimentation, and let startups become independent of the studio. See [[Studio Culture]].

The corporate chapter adds the retention conditions specific to a corporate venture builder ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_21-corporate-venture-builders]]): the studio draws internal talent frustrated by corporate constraints together with external entrepreneurs; compensation must enable failure, meaning good salaries plus equity in successes; and a studio charged only on revenue drifts into an agency mindset rather than a venture mindset. Placing experiments in a separate entity also protects the parent's reputation, which is what allows bolder experimentation. See [[Corporate Venture Building]].

Kannan and Peterman describe the founder side of compensation rather than the core team's: full-time EIRs earn $150,000 or more in major markets from the studio's operating budget, term-based contractors receive stipends over 6 to 12 months, and founders retained to run a venture may be paid either from studio operating funds or from that venture's invested capital ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_08-working-with-founders]]). They also note that studios compete with VC for founders by offering administrative support, lower distraction and shared infrastructure, an offer that depends on the core team actually delivering it.

### Bringing founders into the team

Both books treat the boundary between the core team and venture leadership as a design choice. Betaworks' Hackers-in-Residence program lets experienced technologists experiment with ideas inside the studio in a low-risk, high-support environment without a clear initial product vision; ventures are funded internally at low cost until traction, then helped to raise external funds and spun off with continued studio support, and the equity split described there is 50-50 between studio and team ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_05-emerging-from-beta]]). The case study's founder describes the relief of not having to build a business plan and pitch deck or chase initial investment for months, which is the core team's work.

Kannan and Peterman observed stronger outcomes when founding teams or CEOs joined ventures earlier, even at the cost of higher founder equity; a leader assigned late to a developed concept finds it harder to put a stamp on the business. Most studios in their sample prioritise second-time founders for their networks and experience, while also targeting first-time founders with domain expertise, technical skills and entrepreneurial spirit ([[2022-02-08_venture-studios-demystified_08-working-with-founders]]). Szigeti's corporate chapter adds that a studio set up in a low-cost, underserved region gives local talent an opportunity and proves that startups can be built locally, which in turn attracts follow-on corporate investment ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_21-corporate-venture-builders]]).

### Where the books diverge

- **Scope of the in-house team.** Szigeti prescribes full internal coverage of startup competencies; Kannan and Peterman's sample includes studios with much lighter cores that delegate ideation to founders. Neither declares one size correct, and Kannan and Peterman tie the choice to recruitment strategy rather than cost alone.
- **Unit of analysis.** Szigeti writes about the employed core team and its culture; Kannan and Peterman write about founding-partner composition and about EIRs and founders. The two overlap on the principle that competency spread is what the studio sells, and on paying people from operating budgets during the build.
