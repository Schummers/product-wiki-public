# Team Scaling

## Definition

Team scaling, as the sources describe it, is the problem of growing what a team delivers faster than the team itself grows. Two Nielsen Norman Group articles treat it at the scale of a design or research function inside an organisation. [[2020-08-16_research-ops-101]] presents ResearchOps as the discipline that lets research demand rise tenfold while the budget rises by roughly eight or nine times rather than eleven, through economies of scale, reuse and dedicated operational roles. [[2026-05-15_lean-design-system-teams]] observes that design-system teams stay at two to five people even in companies with thousands of employees, and argues that extending a system's reach means shifting from producer to enabler, through champions and contributors, rather than adding headcount.

Three chapters of Attila Szigeti's *Startup Studio Playbook* use the term for a venture studio: an organisation that must grow from a founding team into one that builds dozens of startups in parallel. Here scaling means distributing authority so that founders stop being the bottleneck ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_08-building-a-100-co-owned-venture-builder]], [[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_14-studio-leadership]]) and building a repeatable system for recruiting and developing the CEOs who will run each venture ([[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_17-entrepreneurs-in-residence-and-ceos]]). The scales differ, but the sources converge on the same mechanism: scale comes from structure, shared knowledge and enabling others, not from proportional growth in people.

## Practice

### Two scales, one mechanism

The NN/g sources scale a *function* (research, a design system) that serves many product teams. The studio chapters scale an *organisation* that spawns many companies. The units differ, so the guidance is grouped by scale below. What both share is the claim that headcount is the wrong lever: ReOps reuses assets and standardises methods, a lean design-system team enables contributors, Lab.Coop distributes authority through holacracy, and a studio codifies leadership knowledge into processes and training so it can be spread across dozens of CEOs.

### Function scale: scaling research through operations

[[2020-08-16_research-ops-101]] defines ResearchOps as the orchestration and optimisation of people, processes and craft to amplify the value and impact of research at scale, born of the exponential growth of the UX profession and the need to meet rising demand without proportional cost.

- **Scaling economics.** When an organisation conducts ten times more research, costs should not rise tenfold; through ReOps practices, cost scaling of eight to nine times the original budget is achievable instead of eleven, via economies of scale and reuse.
- **Six interconnected components.** Participants (recruiting, screening, scheduling, compensating); governance (consent, privacy, information storage); knowledge (collecting, synthesising, sharing insights); tools (consistent toolsets); competency (enabling others to perform research); advocacy (defining and sharing research value). Each affects and is affected by the others.
- **Reuse is the lever.** Standardised methods and supporting documentation (scripts, templates, consent forms) allow consistent application across teams and save planning time. A shared insights database helps teams avoid repeating studies and educates people outside the research team (see [[Research Operations]], [[Research Methods]]).
- **Enable others.** The competency component explicitly covers enabling non-researchers to do research, a form of scaling through distribution rather than through more researchers. ReOps is broader than participant recruitment: it includes educating partners and socialising impact.
- **Getting started.** Identify current pain points for researchers and their partners through internal research, then focus on the most pressing area while keeping balance across all six.

### Function scale: scaling a design system without scaling the team

[[2026-05-15_lean-design-system-teams]] reports that design-system teams remain small (typically 2-5 people) across organisations of all sizes, and reads this as a deliberate operating model rather than mere resource constraint.

- **Why small works.** Shared context eliminates handoff delays and formal status meetings; conflicts resolve quickly with fewer opinions to reconcile. Blurred roles (designers contributing to API design, developers critiquing designs, product managers doing hands-on work) develop cross-disciplinary understanding and shorten feedback loops. Constrained capacity forces explicit prioritisation, which product teams accept more readily when limits are transparent.
- **Small by design versus small by default.** Smallness is only an advantage when paired with executive buy-in, defined scope and realistic expectations. Without them, small is underinvestment disguised as scrappiness, and the team suffers burnout and maintenance debt. The article stresses that this is not an excuse to understaff.
- **Little slack.** Small teams have no redundancy: when one person is out, a significant share of capacity goes with them.
- **Scale through contribution and championship.** Extending impact requires the team to shift from producer to enabler. Champion programmes and contributor models distribute work across the organisation without adding headcount (see [[Design System]], [[Organizational Culture]]).

The two NN/g sources agree on the mechanism, enabling others, but differ on whether the team itself should grow: ReOps introduces dedicated operational roles, whereas the design-system article argues for keeping the core team small and growing the periphery.

### Studio scale: scaling authority without a management layer

[[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_08-building-a-100-co-owned-venture-builder]] documents Lab.Coop, which grew from 7 to 35 people (including portfolio companies) after its founders chose to keep the Brickflow team together rather than disperse.

- **An organisational operating system.** Lab.Coop built its structure on holacracy: authority is distributed across role-based domains rather than concentrated in a hierarchy. Domains are split among team members, anyone can propose rule changes, and only the domain owner implements the changes they judge beneficial. Peer-to-peer tension resolution replaces direct supervision, which reduced founder overhead while keeping accountability clear.
- **Co-ownership as the retention lever.** When cash is scarce, fixed salaries cannot compete with VC-backed employers. Every team member became a co-owner, with equity growing on tenure, work complexity and peer-recognised skill, and with a voice in high-level decisions. Compensation follows transparent formulas and a Badge-Based Compensation Scheme where peers propose badges for emerging competencies (see [[Equity Ownership]], [[Studio Structure]]).
- **Culture holds it together.** Trust and radical transparency made co-ownership and self-management workable rather than chaotic; self-selection and peer pressure maintained the culture as the team grew. The chapter notes that Lab.Coop must put considerable effort, more than expected, into educating newcomers, and that this effort pays off (see [[Studio Culture]]).
- **Runway first.** Lab.Coop started as an agency (Chain.Reaction) to fund internal projects and co-founded ventures, proving the model before scaling it (see [[Venture Studio]]).

### Studio scale: self-scaling leadership

[[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_14-studio-leadership]] names the central problem "self-scaling": studio leaders must spread their expertise across dozens of CEOs through structured knowledge sharing and hands-on mentorship without becoming the bottleneck.

- **Structure that thrives without the founder.** A studio that wants to stay meaningfully involved in dozens of startups needs standardised processes, delegated decision-making and an executive team capable of managing portfolios. The chapter's phrasing: "You don't want to end up being the bottleneck."
- **Core teams that work across ventures.** Leaders must build teams that work on multiple products simultaneously and keep them motivated on projects they will eventually release. Continuous recruitment of talented venture builders requires maintaining inspiration and addressing the eventual repetitiveness of the work.
- **Portfolio management becomes critical at scale.** When several startups need the same person and one approaches breakthrough, leaders must make go/no-go decisions that reprioritise the portfolio; this demands portfolio management expertise beyond single-startup experience (see [[Portfolio Strategy]]).
- **Three constituencies.** Leaders balance the studio organisation, the portfolio startups (founders and investees at once) and external investors; cap tables and management fees must reflect these competing interests.
- **The factory mindset.** Studios are the factories and startups are the product; leaders must enjoy letting mature companies become independent and killing unsuccessful initiatives to recycle resources (see [[Studio Leadership]]).

### Studio scale: a CEO development system

[[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_17-entrepreneurs-in-residence-and-ceos]] addresses the supply side: a studio cannot scale on handpicked founders, so it must build a sustainable system for finding and developing entrepreneurs-in-residence and CEOs.

- **Recruitment beyond the personal network.** Personal networks are limited and non-scalable. Studios must build brand presence, speak at meetups, build community and experiment with programmes such as Hackers-in-Residence to create a candidate pipeline. Ready-made EIRs are hard to find: candidates are either inexperienced in some field or busy with their own projects (see [[Founder Recruitment]]).
- **Assess on five factors.** Track record, leadership style, network in the target market, coachability, and willingness to work across multiple ventures and release mature companies. Juniors require training investment; veterans demand compelling compensation.
- **A comprehensive training programme.** CEO development must cover generic startup know-how (idea to exit), ideation and validation, revenue generation (sales and growth hacking), investor relations, project and people management, and legal, regulatory and financial company management. High-quality online resources and streamlined onboarding are foundational.
- **Regular management and knowledge sharing.** One-on-ones check progress and calibrate challenge so EIRs are neither overloaded nor bored; leaders join seed fundraising meetings; structured programmes share knowledge across the EIR cohort (see [[Entrepreneur in Residence]]).
- **Equity that survives scaling.** Studios typically take 30-80% equity (average 50%); CEO equity can be rebalanced at Series A to keep CEOs motivated and reassure new investors.

### Where the sources converge and diverge

- **Convergence: codify, then distribute.** ReOps standardises methods and templates; the design-system team enables contributors; Lab.Coop writes transparent rules that anyone can propose to change; the studio chapters turn leadership knowledge into processes, training and onboarding. In every case the scarce resource (researcher time, system expertise, founder authority, entrepreneurial know-how) is made reusable so it can reach more people.
- **Divergence: how much the core grows.** [[2026-05-15_lean-design-system-teams]] argues for holding the core at 2-5 people; [[2020-08-16_research-ops-101]] accepts sub-linear growth with dedicated ops roles; Lab.Coop grew fivefold while flattening its structure; [[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_14-studio-leadership]] and [[2019-02-17_startup-studio-playbook_17-entrepreneurs-in-residence-and-ceos]] assume the studio will keep adding core team and CEOs, and focus on how leadership and recruitment keep pace. The corpus does not offer a single answer on team size; it offers a shared answer on what growth must not do, which is recreate a bottleneck or a bloated management layer.
- **Divergence: motivation.** Only the studio chapters treat motivation as a scaling problem, whether through co-ownership (Lab.Coop) or through keeping venture builders and EIRs engaged on projects they will release. The NN/g sources address burnout in small teams but do not tie scaling to compensation or ownership.
