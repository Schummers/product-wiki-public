# Design Thinking

## Definition

Design thinking is an ideology and an accompanying process asserting that a
hands-on, user-centric approach to problem solving leads to innovation, and that
innovation leads to differentiation and competitive advantage
[[2016-07-31_design-thinking]] [[2021-08-22_design-thinking-study-guide]]. It is
usually described as six phases — empathize, define, ideate, prototype, test,
implement — grouped into three larger flows: understand, explore, materialize
[[2016-07-31_design-thinking]]. The phases are iterative and cyclical rather than
linear: testing a prototype routinely sends a team back to the empathize or
define phase [[2016-07-31_design-thinking]]
[[2021-08-22_design-thinking-study-guide]]. The term was formalized in the 1990s
by David Kelley and Tim Brown of IDEO [[2016-07-31_design-thinking]].

There is no single agreed definition. NN/g research with 87 practitioners found
that 62% associate design thinking with characteristic attributes ("iterative",
"user-centered", "collaborative") rather than with a fixed method, and that
practitioners variously define it as a problem-solving process, a mindset shift,
or a flexible toolkit — a progression the authors hypothesise tracks expertise,
novices seeing a prescribed process and experts a dynamic toolkit
[[2018-12-02_design-thinking-practitioners-say]]. A separate survey of 1,067
practitioners describes the same trajectory as a four-stage learner's journey
from newcomer to grandmaster, where the grandmaster treats design thinking as
flexible scaffolding [[2021-01-10_design-thinking-learners-journey]]. The
frameworks themselves — the double diamond, the design-thinking cycle — are best
read as simplified representations of stages problem solving naturally passes
through, not as rigid checklists [[2026-03-13_design-process-isnt-dead]].

## Practice

### Understand: empathize before defining

The first phase is research and observation of real users, and it grounds every
decision that follows [[2016-07-31_design-thinking]]. Empathy mapping is the
canonical tool for this phase: a four-quadrant visualization (Says, Thinks, Does,
Feels) that externalizes what the team knows about a user type, is deliberately
non-chronological, and exposes knowledge gaps — a sparse map is itself the signal
that more research is needed before designing
[[2018-01-14_empathy-mapping]]. Mapping methods form a toolkit rather than a
single technique: empathy mapping for mindset, customer journey mapping for one
person's interaction with a product, experience mapping for generic human
behavior across products, and service blueprinting for the organizational
frontstage/backstage view; each mapping requires three explicit choices — current
vs. future state, hypothesis vs. research-based, low vs. high fidelity
[[2017-11-05_ux-mapping-cheat-sheet]].

In complex, specialized domains (healthcare, finance, logistics), the understand
phase has to widen: study the domain itself — its goals, terminology, roles and
constraints — not only user behavior; observe work in its real environment, where
interruptions, shared equipment and cross-team coordination shape actual use; and
triangulate across frontline users, supervisors and adjacent teams, since no
single person holds the full picture [[2025-07-25_strategies-complex-application-design]].

### Define: frame the problem before solving it

The define phase turns research into an actionable problem statement. A user need
statement combines three components — a specific user, a genuine research-rooted
need, and an insight about the outcome sought — and must express needs as verbs
(goals, end states) rather than nouns (dropdowns, dashboards), which would
prematurely constrain the solution space
[[2019-03-24_user-need-statements]]. Need statements come from qualitative
research (interviews, field studies, diary studies), not from team assumptions,
and act as a north star and success metric for the rest of the project
[[2019-03-24_user-need-statements]].

"How Might We" questions, borrowed from Procter & Gamble in the 1970s and adopted
by IDEO, bridge discovery and ideation. Good HMW questions are grounded in actual
research findings, avoid embedding a solution, stay broad enough to admit
multiple approaches without losing the core problem, target the root problem
rather than its symptom, and use positive action verbs (increase, create,
enhance) rather than negative ones; teams should write and select them together
against a checklist [[2021-01-17_how-might-we-questions]].

### Explore: ideate on the right problem

Ideation is the generation of a broad set of ideas with no attempt to judge them,
and it sits in the Explore phase, after research has defined the problem and
before prototyping [[2017-01-15_ux-ideation]]. Practice guidance converges on:
quantity over quality; no evaluation during generation, since judgment stifles
contribution; recording every idea as a tangible artifact so it can seed others;
group work for diversity of ideas; and a deliberate break between generating and
evaluating [[2017-01-15_ux-ideation]]. Ideation must stay grounded in research —
ideas generated without understanding users are worthless, because the team also
lacks the criteria to choose among them [[2017-01-15_ux-ideation]]. The study
guide frames the phase as divergence followed by collaborative convergence, with
HMW questions, brainstorming, sketching and scenario mapping with personas as
techniques [[2021-08-22_design-thinking-study-guide]]. In complex domains,
regulatory, workflow and risk constraints should be brought into ideation as
generative boundaries rather than treated as obstacles
[[2025-07-25_strategies-complex-application-design]].

### Materialize: prototype, test, and actually implement

Prototypes range from low-fidelity sketches to high-fidelity interactive
representations, and testing with users creates the feedback loop that drives
refinement [[2021-08-22_design-thinking-study-guide]]. In complex applications,
high fidelity is often impractical early: sketches, storyboards and wireframes
let domain experts evaluate the logic without overcommitting, and evaluation
should be adapted to surface how experts reason through decisions rather than
only whether a task was completed
[[2025-07-25_strategies-complex-application-design]].

Implementation is the sixth phase and, per NN/g, the most important and the most
often forgotten: design thinking does not exempt anyone from the design doing,
and it is not magic [[2016-07-31_design-thinking]]. A related failure mode is
"breakage" — the chain from data to findings to insights to recommendations to
adoption breaks somewhere between the readout and the release, through
cherry-picking, reinterpretation into familiar patterns, deprioritization during
crunch, or fuzzy ownership. Every link must connect, and adoption should be
measured explicitly rather than hoped for
[[2025-11-07_research-recommendation-breakage]].

### Design thinking as a team practice

Beyond problem solving, the process builds common ground. Shared vocabulary
emerges organically as teams work through exercises together (a specific "Sam"
becomes shorthand for a persona; metaphors align teams without prescribing
features); tangible artifacts — empathy maps, journey maps, wireframes,
storyboards, mockups — act as organizational memory and a visual baseline that
transcends language; and democratic evaluation (anonymous idea cards, sticky
notes, dot-voting heat maps) keeps hierarchy out of decisions and builds
trust-based culture [[2016-09-18_design-thinking-team-building]]. Mappings are
described in the same terms: collaborative tools that break silos, where the act
of creating the map forces the conversation and an aligned mental model
[[2017-11-05_ux-mapping-cheat-sheet]]. The empathy-mapping process itself, not
the finished artifact, is where much of the value sits, and maps should be
treated as living documents with versions and dates
[[2018-01-14_empathy-mapping]].

Workshops are the format for this. Research-alignment workshops build shared
understanding of findings (gallery walks, affinity diagramming); empathy
workshops create emotional connection (persona walkthroughs, journey mapping);
research-application workshops translate insights into design directions
(opportunity mapping, ideation). The best sessions blend the three according to
audience readiness — understanding activities for teams new to the research,
empathy activities for teams that know the findings but have not internalized
them, application activities for teams ready to act
[[2025-11-21_ux-research-workshops]]. Design-thinking workshops enable hands-on,
inclusive problem solving in ways standard meetings cannot
[[2021-08-22_design-thinking-study-guide]].

### Learning it, and adapting it

Mastery is the alignment of competence (how good you are) with confidence (how
good you think you are); either one without the other limits growth. Progress
across the four stages is nonlinear and turbulent, especially in the adopter
phase, where practitioners practice "by the book", jump to conclusions, use the
wrong activity at the wrong moment, or lack organizational buy-in — and where
most learning happens through failure. Educators should target the learner's
current stage rather than the ones ahead
[[2021-01-10_design-thinking-learners-journey]].

Because definitions differ, organizations are advised to write an explicit
working definition for their own context — what design thinking means, what it
does not mean, what outcomes are expected — since multiple definitions produce
disagreement and misaligned outcomes, and since measuring effectiveness depends
on which definition was adopted
[[2018-12-02_design-thinking-practitioners-say]].

### Where design thinking fails in practice

Jeff Patton frames the five-step process (empathize, define, ideate, prototype,
test) as structure for product discovery, but names predictable ways it breaks
down: starting without framing the business need first, spending too much time
researching without stopping, assuming that doing design at all guarantees
success, producing beautiful prototypes that don't actually work, or blaming
people rather than the process when outcomes don't match expectations
[[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_20-15-using-discovery-for-validated-learning]].
Patton's own remedy is time-boxing and a tight focus on specific problems,
which he folds into a validated-learning loop borrowed from Eric Ries's Lean
Startup — name the risky assumption, build the smallest test, observe real
customers, and rethink — rather than treating the five steps as a guarantee of
a good outcome
[[2014-09-05_user-story-mapping_20-15-using-discovery-for-validated-learning]].
This lands close to the corpus's own critique that the framework is
scaffolding, not a recipe, but Patton's angle is narrower: not that experts
compress the stages, but that the process itself has known, nameable failure
modes worth watching for.

### Framework vs. judgment: where the sources push back

The sources are consistent that the framework is scaffolding, not a recipe: adapt
it to the team, the expertise and the problem [[2016-07-31_design-thinking]], and
recognise the double diamond and the design-thinking cycle as simplifications of
natural stages rather than checklists [[2026-03-13_design-process-isnt-dead]].
The 2025 industry review sharpens this into a critique: influencer culture and
template proliferation have spread shallow UX, and critical thinking and context
matter more than shortcuts — "UX is messy. It can't be placed neatly in boxes or
approached in the same exact way every time" [[2025-01-10_ux-reset-2025]].

There is a real tension in the corpus over how much process can be dropped. The
argument that intuition and skipping discovery are strengths is challenged
directly: what looks like skipping is experienced designers *compressing*
process — running the stages faster and nonlinearly on internalized expertise
that junior designers do not yet have. Solution-first design works only where the
problem space is mature, users are sophisticated and organizational maturity is
high; in regulated domains process is a safeguard against harm, not bureaucratic
ritual. The skill is process literacy — matching the right process to the problem
— not abandonment [[2026-03-13_design-process-isnt-dead]]. This sits alongside
the expertise progression in
[[2018-12-02_design-thinking-practitioners-say]] and
[[2021-01-10_design-thinking-learners-journey]], where the most advanced
practitioners are precisely those who stop following the process literally.
Applied to specialized domains, the same conclusion holds: working on complex
applications does not require inventing a new UX process, only refining the
existing one [[2025-07-25_strategies-complex-application-design]].
