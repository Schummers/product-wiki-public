# Change Management

## Definition

Change management, across these sources, is the work of making a change actually
take hold — in an organization or in a customer base — rather than merely
announcing it. Its recurring premise is that the artefact is never enough: a
journey map that no one uses is just a pretty map
[[2016-10-16_journey-mapping-ux-practitioners]], and there is no point launching
new tools if you have no capacity to get people to appropriate and adopt them
[[2026-03-17_405_Comment_adopter_le_DesignOps_-_Les_réponses_d_Amine_Ambari]].
Deliverables, metrics, design systems and rituals all decay into shelf-ware unless
someone owns their adoption [[2025-09-19_signs-of-ux-maturity-regression]],
[[2026-02-20_recommendation-adoption-score]].

The sources address two audiences with the same logic. **Internally**, change
means moving an organization from one way of working to another — up the UX
maturity stages [[2021-09-19_ux-maturity-stage-2]], from product-centric to
journey-centric [[2025-01-24_journey-centric-design-lessons]], from siloed
channel teams to cross-functional structures
[[2019-09-15_cx-organizational-fluidity]], through a merger
[[2018-11-04_intranet-merger-or-acquisition]], or into DesignOps practices
[[2026-03-17_405_Comment_adopter_le_DesignOps_-_Les_réponses_d_Amine_Ambari]].
**Externally**, it means preparing customers for operational changes so
expectations hold and trust survives [[2020-06-14_emergency-covid]]. In both
directions the mechanism is the same: explain what changed and why, before people
discover it themselves.

## Practice

### Mindset change comes before operational change

A top-down mandate alone is insufficient. Organizations must deliberately build
shared understanding — through training, resource libraries and change-management
support — before teams can adopt a new way of thinking, and the transformation
takes time and patience as staff adapt to new mental models. It also does not
happen uniformly: pilot efforts come first, and benefits compound over time
[[2025-01-24_journey-centric-design-lessons]]. The same point is made about
tooling: communication, listening and training are what guarantee adoption of new
tools without mentally overloading the designers
[[2026-03-17_405_Comment_adopter_le_DesignOps_-_Les_réponses_d_Amine_Ambari]].

Start from observed pain, not from ambition. The recommended entry into DesignOps
is an observation phase identifying the team's actual friction points, then
distributing the corrective work among designers — through guilds, for example —
before considering a dedicated role. Do not go in head-first: do it in a reasoned,
evidenced way
[[2026-03-17_405_Comment_adopter_le_DesignOps_-_Les_réponses_d_Amine_Ambari]].

### Structure follows the scope of the experience you want to change

Organizational dependencies grow with the scope of the UX ambition.
Interaction-level changes require minimal coordination; journey-level and
relationship-level changes demand cross-department alignment of processes,
technology and people that are typically siloed. When marketing, product and
support each work on their own channel, that internal fragmentation surfaces in
the customer experience — and users do not follow single-channel happy paths, they
start, stop and switch. The prescription is to design horizontally around customer
journeys rather than vertically around channel solutions, and to treat root causes
structurally: fixing individual pain points without restructuring teams and
processes simply reproduces the same problems. Digitally native companies have this
integration by design; longstanding companies must intentionally redesign legacy
structures and technologies [[2019-09-15_cx-organizational-fluidity]].

### Governance: playbooks, owners, and dedicated operations

Codify the methodology in a playbook and assign a dedicated owner to maintain and
improve it — this saves time and ensures consistency, while remaining flexible as
the program grows. Dedicated journey-operations teams can absorb onboarding,
document teams' unique needs and adjust processes situationally, so delivery-focused
managers are not burdened with it. Priorities should be set from shared business
objectives rather than departmental budgets, which converts competition for
resources into cooperation. And balance vision against execution: use ideal-state
journey maps as reference points, but commit to firm plans only for the coming
quarter, with declining certainty afterwards since market conditions shift. Accept
that the work is never finished — it requires continuous experimentation,
iteration and learning [[2025-01-24_journey-centric-design-lessons]].

### Make deliverables actionable, or they will not drive change

The survey of journey-mapping practice makes the failure mode concrete. Most maps
include the Lens zone (personas, scenarios) and the Experience zone (actions,
thoughts, emotions) but omit the Insights zone — opportunities, ownership,
metrics — which is precisely what turns a narrative into an action plan: without
ownership there is no accountability, without metrics no way to show improvement.
Maps fail from unclear focus (36%), not being grounded in research data (25%), not
being shared or used (21%), and lacking organizational trust and buy-in (11%). They
succeed when created collaboratively and shared broadly (37% of success factors),
when they lead to action and implemented recommendations (27%), when tightly
focused, and when they produce measurable results. Cross-functional co-creation is
itself the buy-in mechanism, and the most-cited benefit is a shared vision across
departments rather than departmental silos
[[2016-10-16_journey-mapping-ux-practitioners]].

### Measure adoption to change behaviour

The Recommendation-Adoption Score applies the same logic to research output: each
recommendation is inventory with a description, an owner, a definition of done and
tied-back evidence, carrying a status (Adopted, Committed, Communicated, Canceled)
and a user-value rating; RAS is actual user value over total possible value, on a
0–100 scale, read as Poor (0–29, research ignored), Fair (30–54), Good (55–79,
majority adopted) or Great (80–100, rare). Trends over a rolling 12-month window
matter more than any single score. The change-management claim is that the metric
itself moves the culture: simply tracking adoption started conversations that had
never happened before, giving product teams their own adoption rates, executives a
visible trend, and researchers evidence to push for change
[[2026-02-20_recommendation-adoption-score]]. This cuts the other way too: metrics
disconnected from decision-making degrade into vanity dashboards, so metrics should
be paired with business outcomes [[2025-09-19_signs-of-ux-maturity-regression]],
and immature organizations characteristically collect quantitative data and then
misuse it as vanity metrics [[2021-09-19_ux-maturity-stage-2]].

### Advancing maturity: culture and process

At stage 2 of UX maturity, effort is limited and erratic, driven by a few
passionate advocates rather than systematic practice; proper methods are replaced
by shortcuts (informal checklists instead of research, focus groups instead of
usability testing), research is run by inexperienced people and yields inaccurate
results, and design inconsistency is rampant for lack of shared tools or design
systems. Moving up requires focusing on two of the four maturity factors —
culture and process: building organization-wide understanding of UX's value,
investing in proper method execution, recruiting more advocates and expanding the
toolkit [[2021-09-19_ux-maturity-stage-2]].

### Maturity regresses quietly; build safeguards

Progress is not permanent. Leadership shifts, external pressure and business crises
push UX into the background, and regression happens quietly in the background,
noticed only once problems become too big and expensive to quickly rectify. The
early signals are rituals turning into hollow checkboxes, metrics collected but
unused, design systems available but not adopted, and ownership concentrating in
too few champions. Countermeasures are structural and lightweight: rotate
facilitators and ownership, introduce prompts and theme-focused framing to keep
rituals meaningful, tie metrics to business outcomes, and run a 30–45 minute
quarterly check-in on strategy, culture, process and outcomes, logged in a visible
"UX health log" so drift becomes observable. Regression should be treated as normal
and recoverable rather than as failure — and the specific danger is overconfidence:
teams assuming good UX is automatic because a design system exists or some teams do
research create blind spots that surface as costly rework
[[2025-09-19_signs-of-ux-maturity-regression]].

### Organizational upheaval: mergers and acquisitions

A merger creates unusually favourable conditions — several experienced teams
collaborating, existing designs to learn from, clear management direction, and
autonomous intranet teams — but the human side dominates. Design for three employee
emotional states: anxiety about job security, confusion about what has changed, and
excitement about new opportunities. The central rule is not to take things away
without explaining why: hiding evidence of the old organization confuses and
demotivates, reads as highhanded, and creates a sense of loss. The opposite errors
are leaving everything unchanged or implicitly running multiple intranets, which
produces duplication and lost productivity and signals disorganization, and hastily
picking one intranet over another, which signals that one group was devalued. The
recommended shape is a two-stage strategy: an interim day-one solution for urgent
needs, then research-led long-term design, which relieves the pressure to rush.
Internal and external branding rules should be separated — employees need the
freedom to see deprecated brand names in parentheses to keep their bearings. It is
difficult to motivate employees and enormously easy to demotivate them
[[2018-11-04_intranet-merger-or-acquisition]].

### Communicating change to customers

When operations change, communicate early and redundantly across the journey. Tell
customers about shipping delays before checkout, not after they have invested time
shopping, with prominent messaging on homepages and product pages so they can
decide with the facts. Use high-contrast banners with bold headlines and iconography
so critical announcements are not lost to banner blindness or mistaken for ads.
Repeat the message on the cart and checkout pages as a fallback for users who were
too task-focused to see the homepage. Layer it by scope: sitewide messaging plus
targeted banners on affected categories and products when only some items are hit.
Update automated transactional emails — confirmations, shipment and pickup notices —
because these are often the reference customers keep, and stale copy contradicts the
new reality [[2020-06-14_emergency-covid]].

Genuinely new services need explanation, not just announcement. Contactless curbside
pickup was unfamiliar to most users, so retailers advertised and explained the basics
on the homepage and at checkout, then followed with detailed step-by-step
instructions by email and in-app notification to prepare customers for the physical
interaction [[2020-06-14_emergency-covid]]. The parallel with internal change is
exact: transparency about what changed and why is what lets people adapt
[[2018-11-04_intranet-merger-or-acquisition]].
