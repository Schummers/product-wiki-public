# Portfolio

## Definition

A portfolio is a curated presentation of a practitioner's work, built to get
them hired. For designers it showcases specialties, strengths, process and style
[[2019-08-04_ux-design-portfolios]]; for researchers it has to communicate
methodology, findings and business impact rather than visual output
[[2019-03-10_ux-researcher-portfolio]]; for product roles generally it is a
shop-window for representative projects and for the person's personality, whose
main purpose is to attract recruiters by showing concretely the value delivered
[[2024-08-06_340_Portfolio_L_outil_parfait_pour_les_designers,_PO,_PM_et_User_Researchers]].

The recurring correction across the sources is that a portfolio is not a gallery
of finished screens. Hiring managers want the messy process behind the polished
design, and the final screenshots only tell part of the story
[[2019-08-04_ux-design-portfolios]]. The second recurring theme is that a
portfolio is an asset with a maintenance cost: it degrades as memory fades and
access to materials disappears, so it is built continuously rather than in a
panic [[2023-01-15_maintain-ux-portfolio]] [[2025-03-21_ux-layoff-guide]].

Seen from inside a hiring process, the portfolio is also a bounded step rather
than the whole assessment. [[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_04-chapter-1-introducing-the-design-exercise]]
places portfolio review as one of seven stages, where the candidate walks
through completed projects and explains their objectives, contributions,
decisions and reasoning, and distinguishes it from the design exercise, which
tests real-time problem-solving rather than finished work.

## Practice

### Curate rather than accumulate

Both NN/g portfolio articles converge on three to five projects
[[2019-03-10_ux-researcher-portfolio]] [[2019-08-04_ux-design-portfolios]].
Select for high impact, robust methodology and recency; do not overwhelm readers
with everything you have ever done [[2019-03-10_ux-researcher-portfolio]]. The
design-side version frames it as quality over quantity: start by taking inventory
of projects aligned with the career direction you want, then pick the case
studies that demonstrate varied skills matching the job duties you are targeting
[[2019-08-04_ux-design-portfolios]].

### Structure of a case study

[[2019-08-04_ux-design-portfolios]] proposes a cohesive template per project:
problem or hypothesis, your specific role and collaborators, the solution
approach, how the solution solved the problem, challenges and iterations,
business and user impact, and learnings — supported by visuals such as sketches,
wireframes and photos. [[2019-03-10_ux-researcher-portfolio]] asks for the
research equivalent: project goals, duration, participants and findings told as
an engaging story, with your direct responsibilities and the team named
explicitly, and both methodology artifacts (study plans, tasks, concepts) and
communication deliverables (affinity diagrams, storyboards, reports).

On the value of a fixed template the sources differ.
[[2019-08-04_ux-design-portfolios]] recommends a common structure across case
studies, while
[[2024-08-06_340_Portfolio_L_outil_parfait_pour_les_designers,_PO,_PM_et_User_Researchers]]
argues a case study should reflect the reality and the specifics of the project
rather than be forced into rigid formatting — the same episode noting that the
double diamond is often cited but never actually followed cleanly in real
projects.

[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_16-how-to-present-your-solution]] gives
the equivalent list for a take-home design exercise, whose deliverable it treats
as a portfolio piece: a clickable prototype paired with a written presentation
covering the task, the problem definition, the audience, the context, the chosen
idea summarised in one or two sentences, the visual decisions and the success
metrics. It asks for three things the other case-study templates do not mention.
State explicitly what the solution addresses, what it does not, and what you
would do with more time. Name the critical assumptions and propose how you would
validate them, through quick user research, surveys or testing services. Position
the solution against competitors, showing whether it differentiates or catches
up, and against the company's ecosystem when the employer is a large tech firm.

### The portfolio inside the hiring process

[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_04-chapter-1-introducing-the-design-exercise]]
maps the process the portfolio sits in: phone screening by a recruiter (10-20
minutes on basic fit), an on-site or phone interview covering process and team
dynamics, portfolio review, an optional product critique, the design exercise,
an optional informal follow-up interview, then the offer. Portfolio review is
one of the three steps unique to designers, alongside the critique and the
exercise, and the book's problem-solving framework is meant to apply to all
three — so the portfolio walk-through is judged on the same reasoning the
exercise tests, only on finished work.

What that walk-through has to demonstrate, for people at the start of a career
or switching into product design, is problem-solving ability and business
impact rather than visual skills alone
[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_03-why-should-you-read-this-book]] — the
same correction [[2019-08-04_ux-design-portfolios]] makes when it asks for the
messy process behind the polished design.

### Show impact, in numbers where possible

Use metrics and user quotes: reduced support calls, improved conversion rates,
before/after comparison [[2019-03-10_ux-researcher-portfolio]].
[[2023-01-15_maintain-ux-portfolio]] insists on capturing the before-state and
quantifiable KPIs, since portfolio effectiveness rests on whether the work
achieved its goals, not only on what was produced.
[[2025-01-17_tactics-ux-job-market]] pushes the same point into job-market terms:
convey measurable quantitative improvements on benchmarks such as customer
satisfaction or time on task, or qualitative recognition such as a promotion or
award.

### Demonstrate breadth, and real-world constraints

Show multiple research methodologies (qualitative and quantitative), multiple
scales from small guerrilla studies to large projects, and both independent and
team-based conditions [[2019-03-10_ux-researcher-portfolio]].
[[2025-01-17_tactics-ux-job-market]] is blunt about the kind of material that
counts for career changers: hypothetical redesigns are nearly worthless, and
freelance UX projects are the way to generate real portfolio material —
specifically because they show the ability to confront technology constraints,
collaborate with skeptics, and learn from real users.

On where that material comes from, the corpus disagrees.
[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_03-why-should-you-read-this-book]] tells
entry-level designers and career switchers to find tasks for building a better
portfolio, and
[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_24-chapter-5-tasks-list]] is the supply:
30-odd open-ended briefs spanning fintech, transportation, healthcare,
e-commerce, music streaming, travel, real estate and media, several carrying an
explicit technology constraint or a business-metric target.
[[2025-01-17_tactics-ux-job-market]] holds the opposite for career changers:
hypothetical redesigns are nearly worthless, and only real freelance projects
generate portfolio material, precisely because they show technology constraints,
skeptical collaborators and real users. The book does not answer that objection,
which was published seven years later; it simply does not treat a self-assigned
brief as second-rate material.
[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_30-additional-resources]] does not settle
it either way: it points designers new to the field or without prior work
experience to external resources on building a UX portfolio rather than covering
the question itself.

### Write for the person who will actually read it

[[2019-03-10_ux-researcher-portfolio]] asks for plain language without jargon,
explaining UX concepts so readers outside your industry and company can judge the
value of the work, and focusing on a few key data points when showing insights to
someone unfamiliar with the problem space.
[[2019-08-04_ux-design-portfolios]] adds that a portfolio serves several
audiences at once (hiring managers, recruiters, fellow professionals) and must be
scannable, since hiring managers rarely read word for word.

[[2026-01-16_ux-hiring-insights]] sharpens this into the central failure mode:
portfolios succeed by addressing the hiring manager's needs, not by impressing
other designers, and legibility matters more than visual complexity. Most first
reviewers are not designers at all but recruiters, PMs or founders looking for a
solution to their problem, not a lesson in what design is. "Craft" itself is
subjective — invisible details for one generation of reviewers, visual
impressiveness for another — so what registers as quality depends on the
audience. The same source recommends standard, easily parsed tools (Google Docs
rather than Figma) for application materials, treats "ATS-friendly" as a red
flag since ATS systems track data but do not decide interviews, and notes that
tailoring everything is unsustainable: assess your market position, identify your
primary audience, and concentrate effort where returns are highest.

[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_28-joel-califa-two-tips-for-getting-a-job]]
pushes the audience logic further, from the vantage point of someone who
reviewed dozens and sometimes hundreds of CVs and portfolios a week as a hiring
manager: treat the job search itself as a design process, in which you are the
product and the hiring manager is the user. Research each hiring manager's
background, the team's values, previous hires and the language of the posting,
then deliver a tailored experience; "spray and pray" applications do not work
for competitive roles. Polish the CV, portfolio and cover letter for design,
typos and clarity, because reviewers at that volume develop pattern recognition
and pet peeves. The same source pairs this with authentic rather than
transactional networking — genuinely connecting with people in design and
helping them without expecting returns — as the other half of what gets a foot
in the door, and is explicit that both take years of sustained effort.

That is where the corpus splits on tailoring.
[[2026-01-16_ux-hiring-insights]] argues that tailoring everything is
unsustainable and that effort should be concentrated on your primary audience,
while
[[2018-02-12_solving-design-exercises_28-joel-califa-two-tips-for-getting-a-job]]
asks for customisation on every application and accepts the cost.

### Choosing the format and the tool

[[2019-08-04_ux-design-portfolios]] lists three formats with different jobs:
web-based (most common and most findable), PDF or slide deck (easy to tailor to
a specific job), and physical artifacts (good for discussion during an
interview); pick according to the hiring manager's requirements and your own
comfort.
[[2024-08-06_340_Portfolio_L_outil_parfait_pour_les_designers,_PO,_PM_et_User_Researchers]]
approaches the choice from maintainability: the tool must allow format
flexibility, simple updates, one-click sharing and some control over
confidentiality, because a portfolio too hard to update leads to rebuilding it
from scratch every year. It recommends Notion on those grounds — plain in design,
but fast to produce structured content and excellent at putting content and
visuals forward. Its underlying claim is that for product roles (design, PM,
research) what counts is the content, not the visual styling of the site, and
that over-aesthetic portfolios are hard to maintain and fall into disuse.

### Maintain it continuously

[[2023-01-15_maintain-ux-portfolio]] treats the annual overhaul as the wrong unit
of work and proposes a new-year self-assessment instead. Capture deliverables all
year: sketches, whiteboards, research notes, prototypes, presentations, feedback,
performance metrics, photos of physical work. Take an expansive view of what
counts — wireframes, journey maps, personas, research plans, specifications,
presentations, evidence of professional activity — not only high-fidelity
designs. Run the self-assessment across five dimensions (skills/tools/abilities,
accomplishments, professional activities, career management, disappointments) and
land it in a roughly 30-slide deck with images and 3-4 sentences of speaker notes
per slide, covering process, collaborators and project goals without deep-diving
into any single project. That deck is source material for future portfolios, not
the portfolio itself.

[[2025-03-21_ux-layoff-guide]] gives the defensive reason for the same habit:
document projects and contributions while you still have system access, because
a layoff removes it without warning. It also argues UX teams are especially
exposed, being newer and politically weaker inside organizations, and that
maintained portfolio materials plus preserved relationships are what support
applications afterwards. [[2025-01-17_tactics-ux-job-market]] makes portfolio
upkeep part of ordinary employed practice, alongside documenting projects and
building financial reserves.

### Legal and confidentiality constraints

NDAs and employment contracts limit what can be shown.
[[2019-08-04_ux-design-portfolios]] offers three workarounds: show the process,
redact information, or remake the designs generically; students should include
internship projects with their real constraints.
[[2023-01-15_maintain-ux-portfolio]] adds that anonymizing, redacting or omitting
material covered by NDAs, contracts or privacy regulations is an obligation —
items can be flagged for later compliance work, but not skipped.
