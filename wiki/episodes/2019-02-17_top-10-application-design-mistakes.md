# Top 10 Application-Design Mistakes

## Summary

This article identifies ten common application-design mistakes observed across industries, based on fifteen years of usability research. Five issues (poor feedback, inconsistency, bad error messages, no defaults, hard-to-acquire targets) remain persistent problems, while five newer issues (unlabeled icons, overuse of modals, meaningless information, junk-drawer menus, proximity of destructive actions) have emerged with modern design trends. The article emphasizes that user research with target audiences is essential for domain-specific applications.

## Key Takeaways

- **Provide clear system feedback** — Applications must clearly show the current state, how commands are interpreted, and what is happening; edit modes require explicit feedback to clarify scope.
- **Maintain consistency throughout** — Users expect consistent terminology, control placement, rules, and feature availability; inconsistency causes confusion and frustration even for experienced users.
- **Write informative error messages** — Error messages must explain why an error occurred and how to fix it; vague "something went wrong" messages leave users stranded without guidance.
- **Offer meaningful default values** — Defaults speed interaction, teach users appropriate responses, and guide novices toward safe outcomes; they significantly reduce effort in repetitive tasks.
- **Use visible text labels with icons** — Most icons are ambiguous without labels; paired icons and labels increase click target size, aid recognition, and support learning.
- **Avoid modal dialog overuse** — Modals cover context users may need while editing; they reduce available information and force users to remember complex instructions, increasing cognitive load.

## Quotes

> Building applications that have both the depth to support complicated tasks *and* the intuitiveness to make it clear *how* to get that work done is a tremendous challenge.

> Remember the double-D rule: **differences are difficult**. When users have expectations for how something will behave or where they can access it, deviations from those expectations cause confusion, frustration, and increased cognitive load as people attempt to puzzle out the problem.

> Informative error messages not only help users fix their current problems, but they can also serve as a **teachable moment**. Although people won't invest time in reading and learning about your app's features, they will make the effort to understand an error situation if you explain it clearly, because they want to overcome the error.
