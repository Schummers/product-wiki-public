# Bad Icons: How to Identify and Improve Them

## Summary

Bad icons are ubiquitous on intranets and websites despite being costly to produce and often unhelpful. Bad icons reuse established meanings incorrectly, require esoteric references that demand inference, appear blurry or lack detail, repeat unnecessarily for every list item, or only work as a set without individual intelligibility. Costs of bad icons include design and research time, development effort, screen real estate, and added cognitive burden. Benefits should exceed these costs. Good icons are identifiable at a glance, memorable, and faster to recognize than text, but only when the concept maps clearly to a visual representation.

## Key Takeaways

- **Bad icon characteristics** — established competing meanings (star for rating, not templates), esoteric references requiring inference, blurry or low quality, repetition in lists where unnecessary, and icons that only make sense as a set.
- **True costs of icons** — design, research, and iteration; coding and quality testing; screen real estate displaced; potential information clutter and visual noise; each icon requires benefit exceeding all costs.
- **Why bad icons proliferate** — designers add icons to break up text-heavy pages, try to add visual interest to link clusters, or feel pressure to match trending design patterns.
- **When icons help** — fast recognition for familiar icons, faster than text for repeated tasks once learned, larger click targets combining word and icon, brand/style reinforcement through consistent visual system.
- **Good icon requirements** — concept naturally represents as icon, simple with minimal detail, obvious reference requiring no decoding, single fixation sufficient for understanding, fits small space without detail loss.

## Quotes

> the benefit the icon offers should be greater than the costs of having it

> The reference is obvious and easy to interpret at a glance, and does not require decoding, studying, or thinking. A web icon is not a painting in a museum ­— users shouldn't have to stare at it for a long time. One fixation should be enough to understand it.

> remember that good icons should be identifiable, quick to locate, and memorable
