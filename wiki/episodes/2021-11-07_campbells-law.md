# Campbell's Law: The Dark Side of Metric Fixation

## Summary

Campbell's law states that the more important a metric becomes in social decision-making, the more likely it is to be manipulated. When organizations rely on a single metric to measure success, people optimize for that metric rather than solving the underlying problem it was meant to capture. Real-world examples range from "teaching to the test" to rating systems being gamed by Uber drivers and Airbnb hosts to design teams intentionally making features hard to use to hit a target metric. The article explores how this dynamic plays out in design and offers guidance on avoiding metric obsession through combining multiple metrics, triangulating with qualitative data, and treating metrics as decision aids rather than decision determinants.

## Key Takeaways

- **Single metrics incentivize gaming** — When a metric determines success, people find ways to improve it that undermine its intended purpose. Managers might reduce hold time without improving overall patient wait time by muting calls.
- **Goodhart's Law reinforces the danger** — "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Optimizing a metric often corrupts what it was supposed to represent.
- **Design teams can be complicit** — Some companies intentionally design poor user experiences (dark patterns) to hit retention or "save" metrics, harming users while appearing successful on paper.
- **Combine metrics to reduce gaming** — Use multiple complementary metrics together. Satisfaction alone can be gamed; combined with behavior metrics and analytics, gaming becomes harder.
- **Triangulate with qualitative research** — Balance quantitative metrics with user interviews, usability tests, field studies, and diary studies to catch nuances metrics miss.
- **Treat metrics as tools, not masters** — Use metrics to inform decisions, but reserve judgment for humans who understand context, ethics, and long-term value.

## Quotes

> Campbell's law states that the more important a metric is in social decision making, the more likely it is to be manipulated.

> When a single metric is used to determine success or failure, human beings are likely to try to optimize their behavior to improve that metric — sometimes with ridiculous or dangerous consequences.

> Quantitative metrics are useful and necessary for successful businesses and designs. But when those metrics are optimized at the cost of all else, we fail our business and our users.
