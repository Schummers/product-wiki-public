# Solving Product Design Exercises: Step 1: Understand your goal (Why)

## Summary

Chapter 9 of *Solving Product Design Exercises* covers the first step in presenting a design exercise solution: understanding and communicating the goal. This chapter teaches how to define why a product matters, what problem it addresses, and what impact it creates for customers and the business, whether designing a new product or improving an existing one.

## Key Takeaways

- **Open with the "Why"** — a strong presentation starts by answering why the product or feature is important, what problem it solves, what impact it has on the world, how it benefits customers, and what business opportunity it creates
- **Vision-driven framing for improvements** — when improving an existing product, think about the company's vision and mission first, then explain how the improvement supports it and creates business value
- **Dual value propositions matter** — communicate both customer benefits and business benefits; the NYC MetroCard example combines societal impact (decreased pollution, better employment access) with business opportunity
- **Status quo context strengthens the argument** — when time permits, describe the current situation and existing problems before proposing the solution, making the opportunity more compelling
- **Leverage existing assets** — for platform improvements, emphasize how the solution uses existing audience and infrastructure; the LinkedIn marketplace example notes the company already has both freelancers and businesses, so the new feature leverages existing value

## Quotes

> I like how Erika Hall defines design "it's the gap between what currently exists and what you're aiming for".

> A great way to start your solution and presentation is to define what it is that you're aiming for and how it helps the business.

> Think about the vision, the "why" of the company and how your improvement supports it.

## Concepts

- [[Product Thinking]] — this chapter teaches the product-thinking approach of leading with vision, mission, and business opportunity when presenting design solutions
- [[Problem Framing]] — the chapter emphasizes articulating the problem clearly and the impact the solution creates, which is foundational to problem framing
- [[Value Proposition]] — the chapter frames the opening of a presentation as a customer benefit and a business opportunity stated together: the MetroCard example pairs societal impact (less pollution, better access to education and employment) with money saved for the city, the LinkedIn example pairs increased value to customers with another revenue stream for the company
