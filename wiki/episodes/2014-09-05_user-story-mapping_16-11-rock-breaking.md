# User Story Mapping: 11. Rock Breaking

## Summary

Patton uses the metaphor of breaking rocks to illustrate how stories move from vague ideas through discovery into development and release. Stories exist at different sizes depending on context: user-sized (addressing a real need), development-sized (a few days to build and test), and business-sized (a minimum viable solution addressing a business outcome). The chapter maps the complete journey: from opportunities through discovery (finding a small, viable solution), into delivery (detailed story workshops that right-size stories for sprints), through building (with ongoing conversations filling gaps), evaluation (with users and stakeholders), and release, then back to learning from how customers actually use what was built.

## Key Takeaways

- **Right Size Depends on Context** — A story is right-sized based on the conversation context: from the user's perspective, one that fulfills a real need; from development's perspective, one that takes a couple of days to build and test; from business perspective, one that helps achieve a business outcome. There is no universal "right size."

- **Conversations Break Down Stories** — Just as a mallet breaks a rock into smaller rocks at any scale, conversations are the tool for breaking down big stories. Precision about size classifications (epic, theme) is less important than maintaining the flexibility to use stories at every level of planning.

- **Opportunities Lead to Discovery** — Stories begin as opportunities (problems to solve or ideas to explore). Early opportunity conversations make go-forward or trash decisions. Discovery dives deeper to find a small, viable solution through prototyping, customer interviews, and technical exploration.

- **Story Workshops Right-Size for Delivery** — In delivery, deep-dive story workshops with developers, testers, and business analysts break stories into small, buildable pieces with agreed acceptance criteria. These workshops are "last best conversations" where detailed decisions are finalized before building.

- **Continuous Conversations During Build** — Even the last best story workshop cannot predict everything. Teams must continue having ad hoc conversations daily during development to fill gaps and make adjustments. Developers should ask questions; product people should observe progress and give feedback.

- **Evaluate at Multiple Levels** — After building, teams evaluate with themselves (quality and plan adherence), with users (does it solve their problem?), with stakeholders (is it on track for release?), and finally through metrics and user observation post-release (did the intended outcome materialize?).

## Quotes

> A right-sized story from a user's perspective is one that fulfills a need.

> A right-sized story from a development team's perspective is one that takes just a few days to build and test.

> Conversations are one of the best tools for breaking down big stories.

## Concepts

- [[Story Mapping]] — Patton describes story mapping as a practice that helps teams understand how people work today and then imagine how things will change with a new solution. It is used especially during discovery to explore the scope and shape of what to build.

- [[Release Planning]] — The chapter outlines the full journey from opportunity to release: opportunity discussions filter ideas, discovery identifies a minimum viable solution, story workshops prepare for delivery, teams build with ongoing conversation, and evaluation at multiple stages (internal quality, user feedback, stakeholder visibility, post-release metrics) informs the next cycle.

- [[Product Management]] — Patton emphasizes that product decisions require looking at the customer and user perspective (who they are, how they work, what they need), the technical perspective (what is feasible), and the business perspective (what outcomes matter). These perspectives must be brought together through conversations.

- [[Assumption Testing]] — During discovery, teams dig into assumptions about who the customers are, how they meet needs today, how a solution would change their world, and what the solution should look like. Prototypes, customer interviews, and technical spikes all serve to test and validate these assumptions.

- [[Product Outcome]] — Stories serve outcomes that users seek and businesses pursue. Evaluation asks whether the outcomes are actually being met, using metrics and conversations post-release. Learning from actual use feeds new opportunities back into the cycle.
