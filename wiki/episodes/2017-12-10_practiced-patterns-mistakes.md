# Variations on Practiced Patterns Cause Mistakes

## Summary

This article explores how human memory and habit creation make it difficult to unlearn practiced patterns, even when they are replaced with similar but different alternatives. Drawing from cognitive psychology, the article explains that well-practiced tasks require fewer cognitive resources to complete successfully and that familiarity allows people to rely on learned patterns rather than conscious deliberation. However, when a familiar design varies slightly from established conventions, users become confused because their mental models of how the interface "should" work no longer match reality. This creates a disconnect where users' expectations drive attention to screen areas where they expect to find information, but the interface has changed. The danger is greatest when interface changes are subtle: users may not realize they are making mistakes because they allocate fewer attentional resources to well-practiced tasks, causing errors to go unnoticed and uncorrected.

## Key Takeaways

- **Expertise Reduces Cognitive Load** — Well-practiced tasks require fewer cognitive resources; users can complete familiar interactions efficiently by relying on learned patterns rather than conscious analysis, which is how expertise works.
- **Mental Models Drive Behavior** — Users' mental models of how interfaces work, created through repetition, guide their attention; when design deviates from the model, users focus on the wrong screen areas and miss important information.
- **Selective Attention Hazards** — Users exhibit hot-potato scanning (eyes avoid screen areas that don't match their expectations) and banner blindness; when design violates expectations, users systematically ignore relevant content because their mental model says it "shouldn't be there."
- **Subtle Variations Cause Problems** — When design mimics a familiar pattern but deviates slightly (e.g., a Filter button styled like a Search Submit button), users apply their learned pattern and fail to notice the subtle difference, resulting in undetected errors.
- **Errors Go Unnoticed** — On well-practiced tasks where users allocate minimal attention, mistakes often go unnoticed because users rely on automatic processing; they don't have a chance to correct the error because they're not aware it happened.
- **Convergence or Complete Departure** — To avoid confusion, design should either fully comply with established patterns (allowing users to transfer existing mental models) or depart completely and obviously (forcing conscious processing and new learning).

## Quotes

> "it certainly is difficult to unlearn a long-practiced habit."

> "a well-practiced task requires fewer cognitive resources to be completed successfully."

> "When designs deviate from convention, users may not be able to bridge the Gulf of Execution to see how to approach their tasks in the interface, and thus will make mistakes."

## Concepts

- [[Mental Model]] — Users' internal representation of how an interface works; built through repeated experience, mental models guide attention and behavior; interface changes that violate mental models cause confusion and errors.
- [[Design Patterns]] — Recurring, established solutions to common design problems; users learn patterns through repetition and transfer this knowledge to new interfaces; deviating from established patterns creates friction and errors.
- [[Error Prevention]] — An unintended action that occurs when users intend one action but inadvertently take another, often because automatic processing is applied to a changed interface or slightly varied pattern.
- [[Cognitive Load]] — The mental effort required to complete a task; familiar patterns reduce cognitive load by allowing automatic processing; changed patterns require conscious attention and increase cognitive load.
- [[Habit]] — Automatic, practiced behavior; habits are efficient but brittle when the environment changes; unlearning a habit in order to adopt a new one requires conscious effort and is difficult for humans.
