# Design-System Maturity: A 6-Dimension Framework

## Summary

This article proposes an alternative to linear maturity models for design systems, treating design-system maturity as a multidimensional assessment across six independent dimensions rather than a sequential progression. The six dimensions are organizational alignment, team effectiveness, infrastructure robustness, governance, support, and adoption. The article argues that linear models fall short because maturity is not always forward progress (systems can regress), there is no universal narrative of growth (contexts differ), and adoption is never complete. The framework is meant to be used as a team exercise to assess current state, identify imbalances, and surface opportunities for improvement.

## Key Takeaways

- **Design systems mature across multiple dimensions, not in a line** — A system may be strong on infrastructure but weak on team effectiveness, or have excellent organizational alignment but poor support for users. These dimensions are independent, not sequential.
- **Maturity isn't always forward progress** — Systems regress when organizations restructure, budgets get cut, or priorities shift. Mergers can suddenly reintroduce foundational challenges. Linear models leave no room for regression or contextual maturity.
- **The six dimensions are organizational alignment, team effectiveness, infrastructure robustness, governance, support, and adoption** — Together, these form the system's maturity profile, which is a snapshot of how the system is doing at a given point in time.
- **Plot scores on a hexagonal radar chart to read the shape** — Symmetry reflects even development; valleys indicate structural constraints; spikes may signal uneven investment. A small, balanced shape is often more stable than a larger, uneven one.
- **Assess as a team to surface disagreements and build alignment** — Including design-system team members, product-team representatives, and key stakeholders creates diverse vantage points, surfaces meaningful disagreements, and builds shared ownership.
- **Reassess regularly to track longitudinal progress** — Assess quarterly or after organizational shifts. Maintain the baseline as a reference point and compare each assessment against it to reveal whether the system is strengthening, fragmenting, or adapting to constraints.

## Quotes

> "Maturity isn't always forward progress. Linear maturity models typically frame growth as a steady climb toward an ideal end state. In reality, many design systems regress in ways out of their builders' control: organizations restructure, business priorities shift, budgets get cut, and so on."

> "There is no universal narrative of system growth. A 10-person startup and a 10,000-person enterprise can both have a mature design system, but the conditions that define maturity can differ significantly."

> "What matters just as much is whether teams trust it enough to rely on it, whether governance holds steady through change, whether organizational support persists when competing priorities emerge, and whether the people behind the system are resourced to sustain and evolve the work."

## Concepts

- [[Design System]] — mature across multiple dimensions that work independently, requiring regular assessment and balanced development to remain coherent and useful.
- [[Stakeholder Engagement]] — including leadership sponsorship, funding stability, strategic positioning, and cross-functional buy-in, is one of the most foundational dimensions for system survival.
- [[Team Effectiveness]] — encompasses capacity, cross-functional expertise, collaboration, and staff wellbeing, and determines whether a system can sustain and scale over time.
