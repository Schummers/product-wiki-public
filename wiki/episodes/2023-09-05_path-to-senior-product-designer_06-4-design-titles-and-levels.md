# The Path to Senior Product Designer: 4. Design Titles and Levels

## Summary

This chapter maps the landscape of design titles and the internal level systems companies use to structure roles and careers. Design titles differ significantly across companies—the same work is called "product designer" at one company, "interaction designer" at another, "experience designer" at a third—so the book uses "product designer" and "designer" generically. Bigger companies use dual-track systems with equivalent grades for individual contributors and managers; they also employ opaque internal levels (L3, M6, L7, etc.) for compensation and promotion flexibility. The chapter sets out common IC and management titles with typical experience thresholds, then articulates what truly differentiates each seniority level: the scope and ambiguity of projects, degree of independence, impact range (self → team → org → company → industry), and the cost of failure. The chapter concludes with domain-specific language companies use to describe expectations for each level and walks through a healthcare example showing how the same project is split among designers at different seniority levels.

## Key Takeaways

- **Titles vary widely but cover similar work** — "Product designer," "interaction designer," and "experience designer" describe similar roles at different companies; bigger organizations use more granular titles than smaller ones.
- **IC and management tracks run parallel** — Companies structure grades so that senior IC and manager roles carry equivalent weight and compensation; a designer can advance within their track or move laterally between IC and management.
- **Internal levels add flexibility** — Private leveling systems (L3, L6, M6, PD1, PD2) are tied to compensation and allow promotions without title changes, enabling companies to reward growth discreetly.
- **Impact is the primary differentiator** — More important than years of experience, a designer's seniority is determined by the scope of their impact (from individual to industry-wide) and their ability to lead, initiate, and oversee work.
- **Project scope and autonomy increase with seniority** — Juniors work on small, well-defined, tactical tasks with supervision. Seniors lead ambitious, ambiguous projects with strategic scope. Principals initiate experimental initiatives and guide company strategy.
- **Cost of failure scales with seniority** — A junior's misstep triggers an additional review; a mid-level error delays development; a senior's mistake delays a feature shipment; a staff designer's affects the org's roadmap; a principal's affects the performance of design operations or the company roadmap.
- **Competency language differs by level** — Job descriptions use predictable phrasing: juniors "learn," "seek guidance," and "show understanding"; mid-level adds "comfortable" and "solid understanding"; seniors demonstrate "expert" skills and "identify gaps"; staff and principal use "independently," "proactively," and "leads/initiates."

## Quotes

> There are no strict rules for how many and what titles companies must have.

> Eventually, the most important predictor of a designer's seniority is the size of their impact and how it benefits the organization.

> What makes a designer senior is taking on projects with a bigger scope, completing them independently, with little guidance, and, as a result, having a bigger impact on the business.
