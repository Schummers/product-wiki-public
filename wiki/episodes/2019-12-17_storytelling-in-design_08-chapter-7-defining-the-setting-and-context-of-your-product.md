# Storytelling in Design: Chapter 7. Defining the Setting and Context of Your Product

## Summary

This chapter explores how context and setting shape product experiences, drawing parallels between traditional storytelling and digital design. Dahlström opens with a Foursquare anecdote in which one deliberate share silently turned check-in sharing on by default, then develops a framework for understanding context as a fundamental design lever. The chapter traces the history of context-aware computing from Xerox PARC's ubiquitous-computing research in the early '90s through contemporary applications, and illustrates contextual complexity through Netflix's recommendation challenges across different viewing scenarios.

## Key Takeaways

- **Designers have a responsibility to understand context** — Dahlström argues that as the world becomes noisier, designers must not add to it unnecessarily but instead use context to help users do what they want, not make assumptions or decide for them.
- **Apps should not change defaults without explicit user action** — Apps should analyze past user behavior to infer patterns; if a user shares once, that does not establish a pattern requiring Foursquare to turn sharing on by default.
- **Context is about the relationship between product and user** — In product design, context encompasses who is viewing the experience, what they are doing, where they are, when they are doing it, with whom, on what device, and what they watched or did before; she also quotes Don Norman on his three teapots, where the preferable design "depends upon the occasion, the context, and above all, upon my mood"; Dahlström gives a Netflix example where the same content recommendation fails because children (unlike adults) want to rewatch finished shows immediately.
- **Multiple journeys exist even within the same task** — Google's research found that user search journeys vary even within the same product category, widening and narrowing unpredictably, challenging the assumption that journeys follow a single linear path.
- **Context-aware computing origins** — Bill Schilit introduced context-aware computing in 1994, initially focused on location, but it has evolved to include social, health, and sensor data; Dahlström relays digital designer Tutti Taygerly's contrast between *Minority Report*'s interfaces, which put the burden of attention and control on the user, and *Her*, where the interface is so seamless and natural that the protagonist falls in love with it.
- **Working through context requires specificity** — Designing a contextual TV experience demands understanding who is watching (alone, with partner, with kids), when (weekday vs. weekend, morning vs. evening), what they watched before, and what they think about it; the process is complex because humans are complex, but understanding these details is essential.

## Quotes

> "As the world we live in, and design for, becomes ever more filled with noise, it's our responsibility as designers to make sure that we don't add to it unnecessarily but instead help users do what they want and not make assumptions or decide for them."

> "Understanding all of these parameters comes down to context, which is one of the most powerful and important aspects we have at our disposal as UX designers and product owners. Through context, we have the ability to ensure that we deliver value at the right time, in the right way, and on the right device."

Ami Ben David, co-founder and CEO of Owner, defining context for product design, quoted by Dahlström:

> "A contextual product understands the full story around a human experience, in order to bring users exactly what they want, with minimal interaction."

## Concepts

- [[Context of Use]] — Dahlström demonstrates how Foursquare's failure to account for context (a one-time share vs. a pattern of sharing) breaks user expectations; context determines whether sharing should default on or off. Julien Samson explains that context is a tool that helps authors build trust and interest with their readers, a principle that applies equally to product design.
- [[Context-Aware Design]] — The chapter traces context-aware computing from Xerox PARC research through contemporary use, defining it as systems that adapt behavior based on location, social, health, and sensor data; Dahlström argues that delivering the right content at the right time requires systems to know when to step back and anticipate needs without overwhelming.
- [[Customer Journey]] — Dahlström references Google's finding that user journeys are non-linear and vary even within the same task; she argues that designers must understand the full end-to-end journey as well as small contextual details that differ per user.
- [[Experience Map]] — The chapter recommends developing a setting and context chart, visualized as a customer experience map, to break the silos that come from teams not sharing a common understanding of where and when the experience takes place.
- [[Personalization]] — The chapter explores how context enables one-to-one experiences tailored to the individual user; through intelligent use of data (Dahlström cites Ami Ben David's definition), products can seem to know and anticipate users before they realize they need something.
