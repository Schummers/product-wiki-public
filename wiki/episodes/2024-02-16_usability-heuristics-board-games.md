# Usability Heuristics Applied to Board Games

## Summary

Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics, though developed for digital interfaces, apply to board-game design with instructive nuances. Board games reveal how breaking heuristics can increase fun when the context is recreation rather than productivity. For example, hiding information enhances competition and tension; abstract games avoid real-world matching; and high cognitive load can create engaging challenge. However, breaking heuristics must be intentional. Poor component design (like indistinguishable pieces for colorblind players), excessive player elimination, or convoluted rulebooks harm both fun and usability. The article emphasizes that designers face real constraints balancing manufacturing costs against usability (printing more cards increases cost and packaging size). Playtesting and iterative design—timeless UX techniques—remain essential for identifying error-prone rules and balancing game fairness.

## Key Takeaways

- **Visibility of status supports gameplay** — clear scoring and positioning via boards, tracks, and visible pieces reduce cognitive load so players focus on strategy.
- **Real-world matching aids learnability** — games anchored in familiar concepts (climbing towers requires climbing rules) help novices understand intuitive rules.
- **Player control drives replayability** — games offering more decision-making agency, like Dungeons and Dragons, sustain player interest longer than deterministic games like Candyland.
- **Breaking heuristics intentionally can increase fun** — risk and consequence create tension; deliberate challenge is entertainment in ways productivity tasks are not.
- **Accessibility matters even in games** — color-only coding excludes colorblind players; consistent icons and symbols aid inclusion and reduce memory load.
- **Documentation and learning are critical** — while rulebooks are tedious, clear organization and supporting videos reduce barriers to play.
