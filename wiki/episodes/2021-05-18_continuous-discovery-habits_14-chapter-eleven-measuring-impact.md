# Continuous Discovery Habits: Chapter Eleven: Measuring Impact

## Summary

Part 2 of *Continuous Discovery Habits*, this chapter explores how to instrument products to evaluate the success of discovery work and measure impact on desired outcomes. Torres illustrates how the distinction between leading indicators and business outcomes shapes what teams should measure. Through the AfterCollege case study, the chapter demonstrates how a product team tested whether simplifying job search questions (changing from "What type of job?" to "What did you study?") would improve search initiation and ultimately increase job placements—revealing that measuring impact requires tracking multiple metrics at different levels to stay true to the core business goal rather than getting distracted by successful intermediate steps.

## Key Takeaways

- **Don't measure everything from the start** — avoid waterfall instrumentation projects; instead start small by measuring what you need for current assumption tests and iterate toward measuring outcomes over time.
- **Separate people from actions** — decide whether a metric should count individuals or individual actions based on whether value comes from many people taking one action or one person taking many actions.
- **Measure the outcome, not just indicators** — while leading indicators like search starts and job views are useful for assumption testing, teams must also track metrics tied to the desired business outcome (in AfterCollege's case, actual hires, not just applications).
- **Discovery feeds delivery and delivery feeds discovery** — testing with live prototypes in production is a natural progression of discovery work and creates a feedback loop where live data informs further discovery.
- **Use incentive structures to measure hard things** — when the final outcome happens off-platform (like job hiring), find creative ways to collect that data (AfterCollege sent follow-up emails 21 days post-application to learn what happened).
- **Test the connection between product and business outcomes** — driving your product outcome is not enough; you must validate that progress on product outcomes actually drives the business outcome you care about.

## Quotes

> Your delusions, no matter how convincing, will wither under the harsh light of data.

> Just because the hire wasn't happening on our platform didn't mean it wasn't valuable for us to measure it. We knew it was what would create value for our students, our employees, and ultimately our own business. So, we chipped away at it. We weren't afraid to measure hard things—and you shouldn't be, either.

> This is why we say discovery feeds delivery and delivery feeds discovery. They aren't two distinct phases. You can't have one without the other.

## Concepts

- [[Product Outcome]] — Distinguishes between product outcomes and business outcomes, showing that intermediate metrics (like search starts, job views, applications) are valuable only when they drive the ultimate goal; effective measurement requires instrumenting products to evaluate both assumption tests and progress toward desired outcomes, emphasizing what matters rather than everything, and maintaining focus on the end goal (like increasing job placements) rather than becoming distracted by successful intermediate metrics.
- [[Assumption Testing]] — measuring success criteria for assumptions about whether customers will take specific actions (e.g., view or apply for jobs).
