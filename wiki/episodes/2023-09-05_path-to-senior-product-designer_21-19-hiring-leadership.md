# The Path to Senior Product Designer: 19. Hiring (Leadership)

## Summary

This chapter addresses hiring as a leadership competency available to individual contributors, not just managers. It maps the hiring process (sourcing, portfolio screening, screening call, professional interview, professional evaluation, senior management interview, offer, onboarding) and identifies where ICs can increase their impact. Sourcing talent leverages the designer's embedded position in the design community; portfolio screening and professional evaluation (whiteboard, take-home, portfolio review) let designers shape quality standards; and onboarding improvements ensure new hires reach peak performance quickly. The chapter frames participating in hiring as one of the best ways to build organizational impact, taste the management role, and prepare for future career paths, while acknowledging the substantial time commitment and complexity required.

## Key Takeaways

- **Leverage your design community access for sourcing** — Designers have better access to talent than recruiters through professional networks, meetups, and prior colleagues. Keep a constantly updated list of portfolios of designers you'd like to work with; identify opportunities when people are job-hunting (layoffs, unhappiness at current role); and proactively introduce strong candidates to your HR team or hiring manager.
- **Start with portfolio screening to own the pipeline beginning** — Offer to be the person who does initial portfolio evaluation, research candidates beyond their portfolio (Dribbble, Behance, case studies), and provide thoughtful feedback. This establishes credibility so you can gradually expand into other hiring steps. One approach is to send weekly emails with sourced candidates, gradually getting responsibility for curator role.
- **Conduct professional interviews with structure** — The chapter does not teach interviewing in general; the author walks through how he structures his own, at WeWork and elsewhere: explaining the meeting structure to the candidate, explaining the company and team, answering their questions (to learn what they care about), listening to them describe their background and approach, and summarizing next steps. Their questions reveal what they care about and how thoughtful they are; listening to how they talk about previous employers, teams, work and goals shows how they approach design, work and their career.
- **Participate in professional evaluation** — the chapter says a designer's professional level is normally evaluated via one of three options, sometimes combined: whiteboard challenge (live or non-live problem-solving), take-home exercise (high-fidelity design over several hours/days), and portfolio review (live presentation to interviewers). Start by suggesting exercise ideas, answering candidate questions during take-home exercises, or providing feedback to candidates—actions that build credibility to eventually own evaluation decisions.
- **Improve onboarding by identifying gaps and documenting processes** — Interview recent hires (last six months) to discover what confused or frustrated them, what they struggled with, and what they wish they'd known. Document onboarding in professional areas: team structure and goals, design processes, design systems, handoff standards, product and competition knowledge, customer insights. Connect new hires with domain experts. Add practical details (lunch place recommendations) to reduce cognitive load in their first weeks.
- **Minimize time to peak performance** — New hires cannot generate value while ramping up and still draw salary, so effective onboarding (buddy system, gap identification, documentation) quickly reduces this gap and lets the company profit from their investment sooner.

## Quotes

> The reality, however, is that an average recruiter's job is to browse LinkedIn and message people on there until someone answers.

> A candidate who has just started is unable to provide value yet they are still getting paid a salary. The business cannot yet profit from their investment during this first period. The goal of onboarding is to minimize this gap and get the candidate to reach their peak performance ASAP.

> Hiring is normally something managers are heavily involved in, so it's also a great way to get a taste of the management role.

## Concepts

- [[Design Hiring]] — This chapter defines hiring as a leadership competency available to individual contributors and outlines where designers can contribute: sourcing talent, portfolio screening, professional interviews, professional evaluation (whiteboard/take-home/portfolio review), and onboarding improvements.
- [[Design Leadership]] — Participating in hiring is presented as one of the best ways to build organizational impact and develop leadership competency, tasting the management role and preparing for eventual career moves to management or independent work.
- [[UX Career]] — The chapter positions hiring involvement as a career accelerator: it sets up designers for the management track if desired, and gives ICs experience with what managers do, better preparing them as interview candidates in future roles.
- [[Team Collaboration]] — Effective hiring brings good cultural fit and diverse skills to the team, and good onboarding (buddy system, documentation, expert connections) helps new hires integrate and build relationships quickly.
- [[Organizational Culture]] — Cultural fit appears as one of the concerns a senior management interview is often used to test, and onboarding documentation is presented as a way to transmit the team's structure, processes and rituals to new hires.
