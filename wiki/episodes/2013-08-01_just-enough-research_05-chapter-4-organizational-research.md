# Just Enough Research: Chapter 4: Organizational Research

## Summary

This chapter addresses how organizations function and how design work must fit into organizational context. Understanding organizational structure, goals, and politics is as critical to design success as user needs. The chapter covers stakeholder interviews—conversations with people whose roles are directly affected by the project—to uncover organizational drivers, approval processes, resource constraints, and potential resistance. It explains what stakeholders are, how to interview them (individually for candor), how to extract actionable insights from the interviews, and how to document business requirements. The chapter emphasizes that pretending organizational politics don't exist leads to failure, and that research in this space requires tact and strategic thinking.

## Key Takeaways

- **Stakeholders defined** — those without whose support the project will fail; include executives, managers, subject matter experts, customer-facing staff, and board members; be generous in selection to avoid late surprises.
- **Interview structure** — thirty minutes to an hour, private, with the interviewer focused on conversation and a note-taker present; open with purpose and reassurance about confidentiality, ask open-ended questions about role, relationships, and project success, and conclude by verifying expectations.
- **Organizational politics** — organizations are sets of individuals and rules; understanding how they work helps navigate turf wars and uncovers opportunities to influence success; the observer effect in organizational research can drive positive change by forcing reflection.
- **Business requirements** — must be cohesive, complete, consistent, current, unambiguous, feasible, and concise; they document what success looks like, metrics, completion criteria, scope (and what's out of scope), and risks; they prevent scope creep by making boundaries explicit.
- **Workflow understanding** — design decisions affect operations throughout the organization; work doesn't happen in a vacuum, so identify how the new system will change processes, what resources are required, and who must adapt.
- **Group versus individual interviews** — individual interviews yield candor in political environments; group interviews work for peers of equal influence sharing risk; email interviews are a fallback when access is impossible.

## Concepts

- [[Stakeholder Engagement]] — the chapter frames stakeholder interviews as foundational research; identifying and interviewing stakeholders surfaces organizational misalignments, reveals political risk, improves requirements gathering, and builds buy-in for the project.
- [[Research Methods]] — organizational research applies the same interview principles discussed in the previous chapter, but to stakeholders rather than end users; it surfaces business requirements and organizational constraints.
- [[Problem Framing]] — understanding organizational goals, incentives, and constraints shapes how the problem is framed; this chapter emphasizes surfacing the real goals versus stated goals and documenting success metrics.
- [[Organizational Culture]] — the chapter treats organizations as terrain (startups are islands, large corporations are continents with invisible dangers); understanding the structure, rules, and decision-making culture helps navigate and influence the project.
- [[Task Analysis]] — the chapter explains that workflow is the set of processes through which work gets done; design decisions affect operations throughout the organization, so understanding current workflow and how it will change is essential.
