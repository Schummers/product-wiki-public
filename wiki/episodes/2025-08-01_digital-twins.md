# Digital Twins: Simulating Humans with Generative AI

## Summary

A digital twin is a generative AI model based on large language models that acts as a proxy for a particular person, trained or primed with personal information to predict individual-level or population-level behaviors. The article explores the continuum between synthetic users (representing population segments) and digital twins (representing specific individuals), discusses use cases including missing-data imputation, shorter surveys, and journey orchestration, describes methods for building twins (prompt augmentation, retrieval-augmented generation, finetuning), and raises critical ethical questions about consent, misrepresentation, and bias. While digital twins show promise as faster and more scalable research tools, their realism and predictive power introduce serious questions about transparency, privacy, and fairness.

## Key Takeaways

- **Synthetic Users vs. Digital Twins on a Continuum** — Synthetic users represent population segments based on generic information, while digital twins represent specific individuals based on extensive data; the distinction is not binary but exists on a spectrum based on information richness.
- **Three Construction Methods** — Prompt augmentation adds personal context to LLM prompts; retrieval-augmented generation accesses rich data from external sources dynamically; finetuning retrains the model on domain-specific data and is most costly but may optimize for particular tasks.
- **Promising Use Cases** — Digital twins can fill missing survey data, support shorter surveys by inferring skipped responses, represent hard-to-reach participants, anticipate user reactions to touchpoints, and preemptively identify usability issues.
- **Current Limitations** — Synthetic users tend to fail at capturing the messy, nuanced nature of real human behavior; digital twins built on extensive interview data show more promise but research evidence is still emerging.
- **Serious Ethical Concerns** — Digital twins raise unresolved questions about consent, misrepresentation (especially if used beyond original intent), bias amplification, and fairness; the UX community must address these issues proactively as twins move from experimental to operational tools.

## Quotes

> A digital twin is a generative model — typically based on a large language model (LLM) — that attempts to act as a proxy for a particular person. This model can respond to new questions or situations in roughly the same way the individual would.

> Digital twins raise serious ethical questions that researchers and designers must not overlook. How should consent be managed when reusing a participant's data to create a long-lasting proxy?

> Used thoughtfully, digital twins could complement traditional methods and extend the reach of UX research into new, faster, and more adaptive territory — but only if we proceed with care and clarity about their limitations.
