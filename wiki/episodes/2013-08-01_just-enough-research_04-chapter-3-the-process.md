# Just Enough Research: Chapter 3: The Process

## Summary

This chapter presents a six-step framework for systematic research: defining the problem, selecting the approach, planning and preparing, collecting data, analyzing data, and reporting results. The chapter emphasizes that being methodical saves time and mental energy, and that even a month-long or single-morning research effort benefits from structure. It covers practical aspects of recruiting participants (including how to write and use screeners), the mechanics of data collection and storage, core research techniques (interviewing and usability testing), and analysis methods that involve the full team in finding patterns and insights.

## Key Takeaways

- **Six-step framework** — define the problem with outcome-based verbs ("describe," "evaluate," "identify"), select an appropriate approach based on the question and available resources, plan with clear roles and realistic contingencies, collect data systematically with consistent naming and backup storage, analyze by bringing the team together to find patterns and insights, and report findings in formats suited to decision-making.
- **Problem statements** — anchor research in what you want to find out, using verbs that indicate completion rather than open-ended exploration; you know when you have described something, whereas exploration is potentially infinite.
- **Recruiting quality** — since the sample size will probably be small, each participant has to be as good as possible, and participants are good to the extent they represent the target: if they do not match, the study will be useless, whatever you ask. Good participants share target user concerns, embody key characteristics, articulate clearly, and have relevant technology familiarity; screeners with behavioral questions filter better than demographics alone.
- **Data organization** — consistent naming conventions and immediate backup prevent loss; even primitive setups yield good results if participants are good, facilitation is sound, and analysis is thorough.
- **Usability testing basics** — it uncovers problems with labeling, structure, mental model, and flow, but cannot provide breakthrough vision or predict market success; testing works best iteratively and in the actual context where the product will be used, not in controlled labs.
- **Group analysis** — involving the full team in examining data generates shared insights and investment; structure the session with clear ground rules that enforce observation before interpretation and principles before solutions.

## Quotes

> being just a bit methodical will be the "extra" step that saves your precious time and brain

> If participants don't match your target, your study will be useless. You can learn valuable things by asking the right people the wrong questions. If you're talking to the wrong people, it doesn't matter what you ask.

> There is no reason to test in anything called a "usability lab," unless there's a danger your experiment will escape and start wreaking havoc. A usability lab gives you the illusion of control when what you are trying to find out is how well your ideas work in the wild.

## Concepts

- [[Research Planning]] — this chapter defines the planning phase as identifying the point person, deciding time and budget, determining participants and roles, and preparing for change when new facts emerge.
- [[Research Methods]] — the chapter presents the complete methodical cycle from problem definition through reporting, with emphasis on interviewing, usability testing, and literature review as core techniques.
- [[Participant Recruitment]] — the chapter emphasizes that good recruiting is central to research quality; a screener survey with behavioral and skill-based questions identifies better-matched participants than demographics alone.
- [[Usability Testing]] — the chapter describes usability testing as observing representative users attempting tasks to determine whether a product is usable and identify resolvable issues; it uncovers structural and flow problems but is not sufficient for successful product design.
- [[User Interviews]] — the chapter treats the semi-structured interview as a core technique, with prepared questions and topics but no strict script; the keys to success are preparation, structure, and conduct, and good interviewing requires basic social skills, some practice, and a modicum of self-awareness.
