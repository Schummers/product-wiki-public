# Just Enough Research: Chapter 5: User Research

## Summary

This chapter covers ethnographic research—studying users in their cultural and environmental context to understand behavior, needs, and priorities. It distinguishes ethnography from surveys, opinions, and focus groups, emphasizing that direct observation and interviews reveal actual behavior rather than stated preferences. The chapter introduces the Four Ds (Deep dive, Daily life, Data analysis, Drama) as principles for design ethnography, covers interview preparation and technique, explains contextual inquiry as site visits, and explicitly recommends against focus groups. The chapter argues that good interviewing is about listening, not talking, and that understanding mental models, habits, relationships, and environments is essential to designing products that work for real people.

## Key Takeaways

- **Ethnography versus assumption** — ethnographic research identifies patterns by observing and interviewing representative users in context; assumptions about users risk discrimination and alienation by embedding wrong guesses about age, gender, ethnicity, sexuality, and abilities into products.
- **The Four Ds** — Deep dive: get to know a small number of users very well; Daily life: observe in messy, unpredictable real environments, not conference rooms; Data analysis: sift through observations systematically to extract meaning; Drama: use narratives and personas to help teams act on the same understanding.
- **Never ask what people want** — the first rule of user research is never to ask anyone what they want: people want to be liked, so it is very possible the answer you get is what they think you want to hear or the one that reflects how they like to think of themselves; asking direct questions runs into defenses and produces pat, potentially useless answers.
- **Interview preparation** — create an interview guide with study description, demographic questions, warm-up questions, and primary topics; gather background on the domain and participant before the conversation.
- **Interview structure** — introduction and warm-up establish purpose and comfort, body uses open-ended questions and probing (asking "tell me more"), conclusion makes a gentle transition to the wrap-up, asks whether there is anything else, and thanks the participant; pauses allow the subject to fill silence; avoid talking about yourself.
- **Contextual inquiry** — observe users in their actual environment to see habits, work-arounds so habitual the person has forgotten them, and the small things you might not hear about in an interview.
- **Focus groups are theater** — unlike individual interviews or field observation, focus groups create artificial environments that invite social desirability bias and group performance; one poor recruit can tank a session.

## Quotes

> We want to learn about our target users as people existing in a cultural context. We want to understand how they behave and why. This is very different from gathering opinions.

> Most people are poor reporters or predictors of their own preferences and behavior when presented with speculative or counterfactual scenarios in the company of others.

> The goal of interviewing users is to learn about everything that might influence how the users might use what you're creating. Good interviewing is a skill you develop with practice. The great myth is that you need to be a good talker. Conducting a good interview is actually about shutting up.

## Concepts

- [[User Research]] — this chapter presents ethnographic research as the method for understanding users in context; it allows teams to replace assumptions with insight, understand true needs and priorities, learn how users see the world and use language, and develop personas.
- [[Research Methods]] — the chapter covers ethnographic interviews, contextual inquiry (site visits), and explains why focus groups fail; it emphasizes participant observation in natural settings as central to understanding behavior.
- [[Mental Model]] — the chapter defines mental models as an individual's pre-existing internal concepts and associations with systems and situations; understanding mental models helps design interfaces that hook into existing maps rather than requiring users to learn from scratch.
- [[Field Studies]] — the chapter describes contextual inquiry as conducting observations and interviews in the participant's actual environment, where habits and work-arounds are visible and can inform design of scenarios and features.
- [[Participant Recruitment]] — the chapter calls recruiting and screening the most time-consuming and least informative aspect of user research, and warns that one bad recruit can tank an entire focus group session, whereas in one-on-one interviews that recruit at least does not taint the pool.
- [[Bias in Research]] — the chapter argues that assumptions about users (age, gender, ethnicity, sexuality, abilities) can embed discrimination into products; user research replaces assumptions with actual insight about diverse individuals.
