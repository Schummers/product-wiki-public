# The UX of AI: Lessons from Perplexity

## Summary

Henry Modisett, head of design at Perplexity AI, discusses how narrowing product scope enables simpler, more effective AI experiences. Perplexity succeeds by focusing on information seeking rather than attempting to serve all use cases like ChatGPT. This focus allowed them to tailor their UI specifically for finding and trusting information: showing sources immediately, using footnotes, and presenting results in varied formats (text, images, video, maps). A key insight is the flexibility-usability tradeoff—more flexible products become harder to use. Perplexity also improved AI accessibility by designing the prompt input to resemble a search box, allowing non-technical users to succeed with simple keywords rather than complex prompts. The conversation also addresses common mistakes in AI product design, warning against automatically adding AI chatbots for their own sake rather than solving specific user problems.

## Key Takeaways

- **Product niches enable simpler design** — by focusing narrowly on information seeking rather than general-purpose use, Perplexity avoided complexity plaguing broader AI tools.
- **Tailor UI to the use case** — specific product scope allows UI customization: showing sources prominently, using footnotes, displaying varied content types based on query type.
- **Prompt writing is currently broken** — existing prompt engineering is a poor user experience; design should let users leverage familiar patterns (like search) rather than forcing prompt mastery.
- **External consistency reduces cognitive load** — users apply existing knowledge (search box behavior) to new interfaces; familiar patterns accelerate adoption.
- **Not everything should be chat** — most products do not benefit from anthropomorphized conversational AI; consider personalization, predictive analytics, and automation instead.
- **Clarity of purpose drives product success** — in the competitive AI landscape, knowing what you're great at and saying no to distracting capabilities is more valuable than feature breadth.

## Quotes

> Finding information as fast as possible has been a flag that we planted really early on in our product-design and engineering principles.

> People want information as fast as possible and want to trust that information. Well, then it's like actually quite a simple design problem and that's why the product feels quite simple.

> I think the thing I feel confident about is that not everything should be a chat. Actually, most products should not be a chat, and most products don't benefit from having an anthropomorphized concept.
