# Audio Signifiers for Voice Interaction

## Summary

Voice interfaces must help users understand what commands are available, bridging the "Gulf of Execution" — the gap between user intent and understanding what actions the system supports. In the absence of visual signifiers, audio signifiers take three forms: nonverbal earcons (distinctive beeps), explicit verbal cues (questions stating options), and implicit verbal cues (hints like "ok?" that mimic human speech). Earcons have limited effectiveness due to ambiguity. Explicit verbal cues are most understandable but tedious if used constantly. The most usable voice systems balance efficiency with clarity through techniques like guessing user intent, progressive disclosure, and sequential cues that avoid cognitive overload.

## Key Takeaways

- **Nonverbal sounds (earcons) have limited effectiveness** — they require context or repeated exposure to be understood, work best for confirmations in narrow, repetitive tasks, and cannot bidirectionally communicate user commands.
- **Explicit verbal signifiers are most understandable but verbose** — directly stating what users can do is clear, but lengthy option lists are ineffective because people forget early choices by the time they hear later ones.
- **Implicit verbal cues balance efficiency and clarity** — adding question words like "ok?" or "sound good?" to confirmation statements hints that users can correct information without explicitly listing all options.
- **Guessing user intent reduces steps but requires accuracy** — systems should only guess when they have sufficient information; inaccurate guesses force users to figure out correction commands.
- **Progressive disclosure keeps focus on current steps** — revealing options only when users indicate they're needed prevents overwhelming cognitive load from hearing all possibilities upfront.

## Quotes

> In graphical user interfaces (GUIs), designers can help people bridge the Gulf of Execution by providing visible signifiers, like distinctive colors for clickable text. When used appropriately, these techniques enable users to understand at a glance what actions are possible; conversely, research shows that diminished graphical signifiers lead to slower task times and cause click uncertainty in a GUI.

> Voice interfaces must minimize the time required to complete an action in order to be useful, because listening to signifiers takes longer than scanning a menu, and speaking a verbal command takes longer than clicking a button.

> Guessing, implicit signifiers, sequential cues, and progressive disclosure are all valid strategies for expediting voice commands, especially for secondary or easily reversible commands.

## Concepts

- [[Voice Interfaces]] — systems that accept voice commands require signifiers to help users understand available actions; audio cues must balance clarity with efficiency since auditory information takes more time to process.
- [[Affordance]] — in voice contexts, sound-based cues (earcons), explicit verbal suggestions, and implicit verbal hints guide users to available commands.
- [[Cognitive Load]] — voice systems must manage cognitive demands by using sequential rather than combined questions, progressive disclosure, and implicit cues that don't require users to remember long option lists.
