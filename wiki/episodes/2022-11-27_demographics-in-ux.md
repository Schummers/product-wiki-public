# Why and How to Use Demographics in UX

## Summary

Demographic information helps researchers understand the characteristics and context of user populations. However, collecting demographic data involves sensitivity—some questions require disclosure of personal identity information, and growing privacy concerns make participants apprehensive. This article provides guidelines for designing good demographic questions and establishing participant trust.

In UX research, demographic questions segment survey data, screen participant eligibility, and recruit diverse participant pools. Demographic data enables understanding whether findings generalize to the whole target population, supports data segmentation to identify patterns (e.g., do age groups use a feature differently?), and provides context about early adopters. However, researchers must be intentional about which demographics to collect, as unnecessary questions lengthen surveys, increase sensitivity, and lower response rates. Six guidelines address granularity of response ranges (use broad ranges unless there's justification), always including "Prefer not to say" options, using inclusive language around gender, rethinking the "Other" option, placing demographics at survey end (unless screening requires early filtering), and allowing "Select all that apply" for sensitive categories like race and gender.

## Key Takeaways

- **Collect only demographics you can justify using** — ask yourself how each demographic variable shapes results; unnecessary questions lower response rates and invade privacy.
- **Use broad response ranges** — narrow ranges (45-50 years) feel intrusive; 10-15 year ranges are acceptable unless developmental differences justify narrower bands; explain why you need specific information.
- **Always include "Prefer not to say"** — this option encourages completion by allowing participants to opt out of uncomfortable questions without abandoning the survey.
- **Use inclusive language for gender** — language evolves; include options like nonbinary and transgender; avoid implying people identifying with "Other" are abnormal.
- **Place demographics at survey end** — unless demographics determine eligibility, place them last so participants feel invested in the study before encountering sensitive questions.

## Quotes

> "Demographic questions help researchers understand the context around the data they collect. However, obtaining demographic information can be tricky."

> "People are now adopting new terminology around sensitive topics such as gender or race. Your questions should not make participants uncomfortable."

> "Researchers should be able to justify the need for the demographic information collected by their study."

## Concepts

- [[Demographic Data]] — demographic information segments data, screens participants, and builds context about user populations; careful design ensures questions are ethical and minimize participant burden.
- [[Persona]] — demographic data enables comparison across groups (age, location, income) to discover whether patterns differ by segment and whether findings generalize to whole populations.
- [[Participant Recruitment]] — screening questions (those determining eligibility) should appear early to avoid wasting participants' time; non-screening demographics belong at survey end.
- [[Study Design]] — thoughtful demographic-question design respects participant privacy, uses inclusive and evolving language, and collects only information that directly shapes interpretation of results.
