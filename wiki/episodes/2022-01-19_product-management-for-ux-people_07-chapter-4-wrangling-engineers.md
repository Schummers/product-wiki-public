# Product Management for UX People: CHAPTER 4: Wrangling Engineers

## Summary

This chapter reframes the PM's role with engineers as facilitation rather than command: your job is to provide focus, direction, meaning, clear goals, and well-scoped requests while treating engineers as core participants in ideation and problem-solving. Crumlish emphasizes that engineers are makers who need uninterrupted time for deep work; the PM's responsibility is to remove blockers, clarify nuances, and communicate asynchronously where possible. The chapter then walks through the operational rhythms that align teams with engineers: daily stand-ups, sprint ceremonies, monthly roadmap reviews, quarterly planning, and annual goal-setting—all reinforcing the Agile principle of iterative review and course-correction at every scale. Within sprints, the PM negotiates scope, manages acceptance, defines "done," and runs retrospectives to improve team processes. The final sections focus on the human side: earning engineers' respect by learning their language and technical constraints, asking smart questions to build understanding, and navigating estimation and negotiation as collaborative rather than combative.

## Key Takeaways

- **Your role is facilitation, not command.** Engineers do not report to you 99% of the time, nor should they. Instead, provide focus, direction, and meaning; make goals clear; ensure requests are well-scoped; and include engineers as core participants in ideation.
- **Engineers are makers who need uninterrupted time.** Avoid micro-interruptions; Crumlish names two channels instead, asynchronous written formats developers can review when it suits them, and the regular cadences of Agile scrum rituals.
- **Cadences are your primary channel for collaboration.** Daily stand-ups, sprint rituals, monthly roadmap reviews, quarterly planning, and annual goal-setting form a fractal of check-ins that reinforce the Agile principle of "iterate, review, recalibrate, repeat."
- **Definition of done requires team buy-in.** Establish a shared definition tied to the requirements documents that defined the original problem space; it also means "coded, tested, and accepted," without which chunks called done at the sprint boundary slip from sprint to sprint. Clarity here also helps escape the paralysis of perfectionism: "Good enough is good enough."
- **Retrospectives are not optional.** Facilitate end-of-sprint retros where teams reflect on what went well and what can improve; when practiced openly and supportively, they drive team cohesion and effectiveness.
- **Earning respect means asking smart questions.** Learn the technical lingo (like how engineers use "design" differently), study their folkways, and ask authentic questions that show understanding while building your own mental model of the technical architecture and constraints.
- **Estimation is a negotiation, not a demand.** Recognize what inspires and frightens engineers; attend to their patterns (some pad, some underestimate) and facilitate balanced discussion among the team to reach realistic effort assessments.

## Quotes

> Your job instead is to provide focus, direction, and meaning and to make sure that goals are clear, requests are well-scoped, and engineers are core participants in the conversation and ideation needed to craft solutions.

> At regular intervals, the team reflects on how to become more effective, then tunes and adjusts its behavior accordingly.

> If you approach this with humility, you will find that you can meet your engineers more than halfway and present your goals and needs in terms they understand and appreciate.

## Concepts

- [[Team Collaboration]] — PMs align with engineers through cadences, sprint ceremonies, and retrospectives; success depends on showing respect, earning trust through smart questions, and facilitating asynchronous communication that preserves deep work time. Ken Norton, a product consultant and educator who spent many years at Google Ventures, supplies the chapter's mantra for that service role, "bring the donuts": PMs often have to do the grimy work that would otherwise fall through the cracks, and are "more humble servants than 'CEOs of the product.'" Ana Giraldo-Wingler, product manager at Coforma, describes her day navigating UX stand-ups, engineering stand-ups, design and product stakeholder meetings, and co-design sessions with government clients while managing contract modifications and coordinating with API partners—illustrating the integration of the PM's communication and facilitation role across multiple moving parts.
- [[Agile Development]] — The chapter details the fractal of cadences (daily stand-ups, sprints of 2–4 weeks, monthly roadmap reviews, quarterly planning, annual goal-setting) and how each reinforces the "iterate, review, recalibrate" cycle; Estimating sprint capacity is a negotiation, not a demand; different engineers pad or underestimate differently, and the PM's job is to facilitate balanced discussion and recognize that estimates are best-effort assessments of where to invest time and resources.
