# Storytelling in Design: Chapter 9. Visualizing the Shape of Your Product Experience

## Summary

This chapter applies narrative structure from traditional storytelling to product design by exploring how stories have emotional shapes and rhythm. Dahlström surveys Campbell's Hero's Journey, Kurt Vonnegut's graph-based story shapes, and Christopher Booker's seven basic plots, then translates these frameworks into product design through experience goals and the mapping of happy and unhappy journeys. The Sony Ericsson example (where the team defined experience goals like "the website knows me and what I want") illustrates how visualizing emotional shape helps teams align on vision and prioritize features.

## Key Takeaways

- **All great stories have a shape and emotional ebb and flow** — As Nancy Duarte suggests, all great talks have a certain shape to them, and Dahlström holds that the same applies to stories and to the experiences we design. In 2016 a group of students at the Computational Story Lab at the University of Vermont set out to test Vonnegut's thesis: rather than look at story structure they analysed the emotional arc invoked by the words used, across a filtered subset of 1,327 stories from Project Gutenberg's fiction collection, and concluded there is strong support for six core emotional arcs.
- **Campbell's Hero's Journey describes an outer and inner journey** — Joseph Campbell's 1949 framework includes 17 stages, and the chapter also carries screenwriter Chris Vogler's interpretation of the Hero's Inner Journey, in which he argues the protagonist changes right from the beginning; the outer journey is the steps and actions users take to complete a task, while the inner journey is thoughts and emotions that ultimately determine whether a user takes action or abandons the product for a competitor.
- **Vonnegut's story shapes provide eight patterns** — Kurt Vonnegut's rejected master's thesis plotted stories on a graph with ill-to-good fortune on the vertical axis and beginning-to-end on the horizontal; the eight shapes the chapter lays out are Man in Hole, Boy Meets Girl, From Bad to Worse, Which Way Is Up?, Creation Story, Old Testament, New Testament and Cinderella.
- **Booker's Seven Basic Plots offer further archetypes** — Christopher Booker's 34-year analysis identifies Overcoming the Monster, Voyage and Return, Rags to Riches, The Quest, Tragedy, Comedy, and Rebirth; some are less relevant to product design (hopefully not Tragedy), but The Quest applies well to information-seeking and purchase journeys.
- **Experience goals map the emotional journey across product life cycle stages** — On the Sony Ericsson project Dahlström's team defined overarching experience goals (".com knows me and what I want") and broke each into experience statements for points across the product life cycle; the chapter's worked example is the consideration stage, ".com gives me relevant recommendations." The method she recommends is to formulate three to five such goals. The statements are then mapped across three emotional levels: hygiene (qualifiers that must be there), feel good (differentiators that evoke positive response), and delight (euphoric touches).
- **Mapping happy and unhappy journeys reveals risks and opportunities** — Plotting both ideal and worst-case scenarios forces designers to clarify what constitutes good and bad outcomes; it helps identify where support, guidance, and improvement are needed, turning invisible problems into explicit ones.

## Quotes

Joseph Campbell describing the basic narrative pattern in *The Hero With a Thousand Faces* (1949), quoted by Dahlström:

> "A hero ventures forth from the world of common day into a region of supernatural wonder: fabulous forces are there encountered and a decisive victory is won: the hero comes back from this mysterious adventure with the power to bestow boons on his fellow man."

> "To fully understand what users need, we first need to understand the humans behind the users. We need to know their backstories and be able to identify their desired outcomes at each point, as well as what it is they are trying to avoid."

> Good needs bad, rain needs sunshine, and just as the boat won't sail by moving backward and forward, "delights" and "feel goods" wouldn't exist unless they were put in contrast with each other and the hygiene factors.

## Concepts

- [[Experience Shape]] — Dahlström demonstrates that visualizing emotional shape helps designers and teams see the ebb and flow of the user experience; by borrowing story structures (Campbell, Vonnegut, Booker), the transfer Dahlström actually makes is The Quest, which applies well to a user needing to find information, book something or buy something; she notes other plots are less relevant to product design (hopefully not Tragedy).
- [[Emotional Design]] — The chapter shows that mapping experience statements across hygiene-feel good-delight levels creates a visual representation of emotional progression; Dahlström's argument against putting everything at the delight level is one of contrast, not of user fatigue: "delights" and "feel goods" wouldn't exist unless they were put in contrast with each other and with the hygiene factors.
- [[Red Thread]] — Dahlström stresses that experience goals create a "through line" running through the entire product, ensuring that at any point in the experience, designers can articulate how that moment translates as an emotional feeling; this alignment is crucial for both teams and stakeholders.
- [[Customer Journey]] — The chapter applies experience goals across the stages of the product life cycle; each stage receives its own experience statements mapped to hygiene, feel good and delight levels, so that at any given point in a website or app you can say how that moment translates as an emotive feeling.
- [[Narrative Structure]] — Dahlström argues that defining narrative structure early in the project (before wireframing or prototyping) helps teams think through emotional rhythm; she recommends five steps: brainstorm experience goals, identify patterns, formulate three to five overarching goals, define and map detailed experience statements, and review for emotional realism.
