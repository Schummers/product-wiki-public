# Product Management for UX People: CHAPTER 9: Healthy Collaborative Tension on the Product UX Spectrum

## Summary

This chapter addresses the perennial question of where to draw the line between product management and UX design. Rather than offering a single answer, Crumlish argues that healthy teams require clear ownership and decision-making boundaries—even when collaboration is maximal. The chapter maps the spectrum of overlap between disciplines and then sorts real-world product-UX relationships into three categories: the good (clearly defined roles with negotiated ownership), the bad (well-intentioned misalignment and duplication), and the ugly (toxic command-and-control environments). Throughout, the chapter emphasizes that "lines" exist not to prevent collaboration but to clarify who makes what decision. It also addresses hybrid roles (product managers who still design, or UX designers becoming PMs), organizational reporting structures, and the reality that achieving this collaborative ideal remains the exception rather than the rule.

## Key Takeaways

- **Collaboration and clarity are not opposites.** Teams should work shoulder-to-shoulder on diagrams, interviews, and design exploration, but still establish clear ownership: the product manager decides readiness to ship, the UX strategist makes the final decision on the navigation taxonomy. Crumlish adds the caveat that no such task must be owned by one role or the other in the abstract; it comes down to the specific people involved, and ownership has to be clarified and agreed case by case. Lines clarify decision-making, not collaboration.

- **Product-UX overlap is real and inevitable.** Both disciplines engage in research, modeling, and specification work. Without clarity on who owns what, this overlap generates conflicts, duplication, wasted cycles, and infinite tug-of-war loops that only end when someone higher up forces resolution.

- **Three real-world categories exist.** The good: clearly defined roles, well-understood ownership, negotiated final say, and the challenge to sustain this culture as the team scales. The bad: the majority state, where well-meaning teams haven't done the work to understand each other's disciplines, creating miscommunication and springing deliverables on one another. The ugly: toxic feature factories where product titles function as project managers or overseers in command-and-control environments, leaving no room for reframing questions or appealing to vision.

- **Shared outcomes may do the work instead, per Matt LeMay.** As a counterpoint to defining role details, LeMay says that "on the healthiest teams, the *team's shared outcomes* are clearly defined and well understood" and that people then naturally figure out how to work together toward them.

- **In the "bad," working agreements replace perfect rules.** Healthy teams establish rough consensus on ground rules, treat them as testable hypotheses, and iterate on them through retrospectives. This requires individual practitioners to name uncomfortable truths and leadership commitment to facilitate the conversation across every level.

- **In the "ugly," survival strategies exist.** Pick your battles; don't declare war on every design violation. Get data that hasn't been cherry-picked to an agenda. Define goals and metrics. Track outcomes and demand accountability when decisions fail. This buys integrity and breathing room until you find a better environment.

- **Adam Connor, VP Design at Rocket Mortgage, frames the relationship as symbiotic:** Design and research answer "what are the audience's needs and possibilities?" Product management answers "which opportunities make sense to focus on given business priorities and capacity?" Engineering answers "how do we build stably and scalably?" Each discipline is an active participant in the others' conversations, not a limiting factor but an enabling one.

- **UX reporting structure matters less than the relationship, though not everyone agrees.** Crumlish's position is that whether UX reports to product, to a design director, or as a peer under a head of product, the core challenge remains achieving aligned communication and collaboration. Caio B. Nishihara, design lead at Ambev Tech, takes a firmer line in the same chapter: reporting into product is common, but a company that sees design as a strategic driver "should keep it out of the product report line to simply avoid 'top-down effects.'" A product leader with UX background must resist the temptation to still be the designer; that's a different job now.

- **The "frankenjob" is both a golden opportunity and incredibly difficult.** Hybrid roles (product manager who is still designing, or UX person responsible for product decisions) can establish aligned disciplines at the outset, but constant context-shifting, doubled workload, and the impossibility of an honest disagreement with a peer who is also you make them "incredibly difficult." Bring in a peer as soon as the team scales.

- **Kristen Ramirez, senior UX designer at Procore Technologies, emphasizes systems thinking:** Understanding business processes, technology constraints, and how objects relate to each other across the whole ecosystem is what allows design to avoid "pigeonholing" solutions. This bridges UX, engineering, and business and requires learning the languages of other disciplines.

## Quotes

> The relationships between these things is symbiotic. Each needs the others for both inputs and outputs. It isn't an assembly line of one passing decisions on to the next.

> Matt LeMay reframes this in a way that may be more realistic about how role details get nailed down: "I think that on the healthiest teams, the *team's shared outcomes* are clearly defined and well understood. People naturally figure out how to work together toward those outcomes."

> You need to kind of be able to model it, to really say, 'I don't think I totally understand these bits. Let me try to draw something for you to show these relationships.' That's what I'm always about: trying to pick up the languages of other people that I work with.

## Concepts

- [[Team Collaboration]] — the chapter argues that product-UX collaboration is maximized through clearly negotiated ownership, shared goals, and working agreements that iterate based on retrospectives; it maps the spectrum from good teams (clear roles, well-understood boundaries, negotiated final say) through bad teams (well-intentioned but misaligned) to ugly teams (toxic command-and-control).

- [[Organizational Culture]] — examined through the lens of how product and UX interact: the culture preserves itself as teams scale (the good), requires difficult conversations and alignment work (the bad), or entrenches dysfunction and command-and-control (the ugly); the chapter offers strategies for surviving ugly environments (pick battles, document goals, track metrics) while seeking escape.

- [[Design Leadership]] — addresses where UX sits in the org chart, whether hybrid roles work (they are a "frankenjob," a golden opportunity that is also incredibly difficult, so bring in a peer as the team scales), how a PM with UX background must resist still being the designer, and how peer relationships matter more than reporting structure; includes perspectives from Adam Connor (VP Design at Rocket Mortgage), Caio B. Nishihara (design lead at Ambev Tech), and Crumlish's own commentary on functioning as an art director without overstepping into design solutions.
