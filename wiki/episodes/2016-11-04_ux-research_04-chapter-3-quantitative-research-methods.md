# UX Research: Chapter 3: Quantitative Research Methods

## Summary

This chapter introduces quantitative research as one of the two types of research found in product design, alongside qualitative research. It defines quantitative research as the study of what can be measured and observed, with results that are consistent and generally agreed upon by all parties involved. It covers where to find quantitative data (site analytics, user flows, demographics, geography), what quantitative research cannot do (it doesn't tell designers how to fix problems, why they exist, or share information not actively measured), and three focuses for research: insight-driven (seeking to understand the problem space through KPIs), evaluative (measuring design against established benchmarks), and generative (creating new designs through research). The chapter then details various quantitative methods including system analytics, surveys, tree jacking, eye tracking, A/B testing, and card sorting, and concludes with guidance on when these methods work best (large participant numbers, tangible measurable outcomes) and when to avoid them (understanding motivations, small populations, entirely new markets).

## Key Takeaways

- **Quantitative Research Definition** — the study of what can be measured and observed, with results that are consistent and generally agreed upon by all parties involved, in contrast to subjective measurements like personality
- **What Quantitative Research Cannot Do** — while informative, it doesn't tell how to fix issues, doesn't explain why things happen, and shares only information that is actively measured and tracked
- **Three Research Focuses** — insight-driven research seeks to understand the problem space and identify opportunities through benchmarks and KPIs; evaluative research measures how design stands up to those KPIs; generative research balances subjective design recommendations with quantifiable, measurable gains
- **System Analytics** — passively capture data on user flows, demographics, and geography; provide insight-driven research thanks to their low cost of entry and, assuming correct tagging on the backend, their depth of information, though they show what happened but not why
- **Surveys** — take a proactive approach to gathering data around how a system meets or fails to meet expectations, offering more open formats than analytics allow
- **Additional Methods** — tree jacking evaluates navigation and terminology through iterative evaluation of proposed solutions; eye tracking uses cameras to follow eye movement on screen-based products; A/B testing presents two versions to identify a leading option; card sorting lets participants organize topics based on their own understanding
- **When Quantitative Methods Work Best** — when a large number of participants can be accessed for statistically significant outcomes and when the research question has a tangible, measurable outcome
- **When to Avoid** — when seeking to understand user motivations or comprehension (use qualitative methods), with small user populations where analytics may not be statistically significant, or with entirely new markets where analytics may not exist

## Quotes

> Quantitative research is simply defined as the study of what can be measured and observed. More specifically, quantitative research means the results will be consistent and generally agreed on by all parties involved.

> While very informative, quantitative research doesn't tell us how to fix things, doesn't tell us why things happen, and doesn't share information that isn't asked for.

> Quantitative research is a valuable tool. As we continue to explore the facets of research, keep in mind this is one side of the coin.

## Concepts

- [[Quantitative Research]] — the chapter's primary topic, defining and situating quantitative research as one side of a complementary pair with qualitative methods, covering its definition, applications, and limitations
- [[Analytics]] — system analytics are presented as a major quantitative method for passive data collection on user flows, demographics, and geography, providing low-cost insight into how customers access and use products
- [[Survey Design]] — surveys are covered as an evaluative and insight-driven method that allows active, proactive collection of data on customer expectations and service quality, with various tools available for implementation
- [[A-B Testing]] — a version of site analytics where two different versions of a page are presented to customers so researchers can identify a leading option, focused on a specific question with established KPIs, though limited to a retroactive look at behavior rather than the "why" behind it
- [[Card Sorting]] — card sorting is explored as a generative method where participants arrange topics in logical chunks based on their understanding, with variations between open and closed approaches and moderated and unmoderated formats
