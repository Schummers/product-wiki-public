# Collecting Metrics During Qualitative Studies

## Summary

Collecting quantitative metrics (task success, time on task, satisfaction ratings) during qualitative usability studies is acceptable with two critical conditions: report numbers anecdotally, not statistically, and explicitly specify findings may not reflect the entire user population. Qualitative and quantitative studies have fundamentally different goals, structures, and participant requirements: qualitative focuses on identifying problems with 5 representative users and flexible tasks; quantitative focuses on population estimates with 40+ participants and standardized tasks. Reporting individual values ("one participant spent 8 minutes") is appropriate, but aggregated statistics ("average time was 4 minutes 23 seconds") are misleading because they imply population-level findings that small-sample data cannot support.

## Key Takeaways

- **Different Study Types Have Different Goals** — Qualitative studies aim to identify problems with ~5 participants and flexible tasks; quantitative studies aim to estimate metrics for populations with 40+ participants and standardized tasks; blending them creates meaningless data.
- **Report Individual Values Only** — Anecdotal reporting of individual metrics ("one participant spent 8 minutes") helps communicate severity without falsely implying population estimates.
- **Never Report Averages from Small Samples** — Reporting "average time was 4 minutes 23 seconds" falsely implies you tested a large sample and can generalize to the population; this claim is unsupported by small-sample data.
- **Avoid Success Percentages** — Reporting "16.7% completed successfully" (1 of 6) misrepresents the data; instead report "only 1 out of 6 participants" which communicates difficulty without percentage implication.
- **Maintain Flexibility in Qualitative Studies** — Qualitative studies must allow facilitator flexibility, task modification per participant, and iterative adjustments; this flexibility is necessary for identifying problems but increases noise preventing statistical analysis.
- **Run Dedicated Quantitative Studies for Metrics** — If metrics are important, conduct separate quantitative usability tests with remote unmoderated methods making them more practical than traditional approaches.

## Quotes

> The short answer is: Yes, as long as (1) you report those numbers anecdotally, not statistically; and (2) you are careful to specify that those numbers may not reflect the behavior of your entire user population.

> In qualitative usability testing, collecting metrics can be helpful in telling a story as you report your results.

> In some cases, UX teams ask this question because they hope to blur the lines between quantitative and qualitative research to save time or resources.

## Concepts

- [[Qualitative Research]] — Methodology focused on identifying problems and opportunities through insights and anecdotes from representative users; flexible by nature.
- [[Quantitative Metrics]] — Numerical assessments of experience aspects requiring large samples and standardized conditions to produce valid population estimates.
- [[Usability Testing]] — Can be conducted qualitatively (finding problems) or quantitatively (measuring population performance); each requires different study structure.
- [[Study Design]] — Fundamental differences in sample size, task standardization, and facilitator flexibility distinguish qualitative from quantitative studies; blending creates invalid data.
- [[Data Reporting]] — Individual anecdotal reporting is appropriate for small samples; aggregated statistics falsely imply population-level generalizability from small-sample data.
