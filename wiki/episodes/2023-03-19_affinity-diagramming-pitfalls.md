# Avoiding 3 Common Pitfalls of Affinity Diagramming

## Summary

Affinity diagramming (also called affinity mapping or the KJ method) is an effective technique for organizing and synthesizing large amounts of research or design data by clustering related items and assigning group labels. However, the method is frequently executed poorly, compromising result validity. This article identifies three common pitfalls: conducting the exercise without a clear focus question (leading to ambiguous groupings), keyword matching (clustering based on shared words rather than meaningful concepts), and groupthink (where dominant personalities override diverse perspectives). For each pitfall, the author explains why it occurs and provides practical facilitation strategies to prevent it, such as posting the focus question visibly, educating participants about keyword matching, and managing dominant contributors.

## Key Takeaways

- **Focus questions are essential** — without a clear question guiding the sorting (e.g., "What usability issues exist?" vs. "Where did users encounter issues?"), teams create ambiguous groupings that serve no strategic purpose.
- **Keyword matching produces shallow groups** — clustering based on shared words (e.g., grouping "Apple," "Applesauce," and "Pineapple") rather than meaningful relationships results in useless categories.
- **Descriptive labels prevent keyword matching** — enforcing specific label rules (e.g., "labels must be verbs describing the problem") forces meaningful categorization beyond word similarity.
- **Groupthink kills diverse perspectives** — dominant personalities (high-authority HIPPOs, eager enthusiasts, enlightened experts) lead other participants to conform, eliminating the diversity that makes affinity diagramming valuable.
- **Multiple rounds serve complex data** — conducting separate affinity-diagramming rounds with different focus questions (e.g., "Where did issues occur?" then "What problems are they?") helps organize large datasets.
- **Organized chaos precedes consensus** — the early period where different participants sort using different strategies produces superior results; premature alignment behind one framework suppresses better approaches.
