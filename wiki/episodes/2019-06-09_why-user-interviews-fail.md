# Why User Interviews Fail

## Summary

User interviews are a generative research method that produces knowledge about users' thoughts, beliefs, mental models, and experiences. However, interviews can fail to deliver valuable insights when conducted poorly, leading to bad design decisions. The article outlines four common issues: wrong purpose (using interviews to answer questions that require observational methods), lack of stakeholder buy-in, poor planning, and inadequate analysis. Each issue carries practical solutions for improvement.

## Key Takeaways

- **Wrong Purpose** — interviews are not suitable for answering questions about user behavior (like color preference or purchase intent); observational methods or usability testing should be used instead, with research questions determining the appropriate method.
- **No Stakeholder Buy-in** — stakeholders often lack understanding of different research methods and may dismiss interview findings with skepticism; education and involvement of stakeholders in research prioritization increases acceptance of findings.
- **Poor Planning** — interviews without planning meander and produce superficial findings; guides should contain a few well-constructed, open-ended questions, and sessions should be piloted and spaced throughout the week to maintain quality.
- **Poor Analysis** — unanalyzed interviews lose insights to bias; recording, transcription, and systematic team-based analysis using highlighted transcripts and thematic clustering improves data quality.
- **Interviewer Skill** — interviewing requires practice; reviewing your own recordings and getting feedback from mentors helps identify ineffective questions and improve overall interview style.

## Quotes

> Your research question should always dictate which research method you use. For example, if you want to know whether users would be able to use a design, then you should watch them interacting with it instead of interviewing them. On the other hand, if you want to understand people's thoughts, impressions, or perceptions of an experience, then there's no better method than performing a well-run interview.

> It's more likely that the findings will be accepted as stakeholders have had a hand in shaping the research, and therefore feel some ownership.

> Analyzing transcripts is much better than relying on patchy notes or memory. You'll stay closer to the data for longer, and you'll be less likely to leap to conclusions that aren't supported by the data.
