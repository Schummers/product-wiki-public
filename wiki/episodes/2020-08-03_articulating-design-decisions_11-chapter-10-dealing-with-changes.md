# Articulating Design Decisions: Chapter 10. Dealing with Changes

## Summary

Chapter 10 addresses the inevitable reality that designers must accommodate changes to their work that they disagree with. Greever examines why stakeholders request changes (misunderstanding, superior alternatives, unmet needs, unreasonableness, or simply wanting to be heard), and presents strategies for handling unwanted feedback without compromising user experience. The chapter borrows Stephen Covey's "Emotional Bank Account" (from *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People*) and applies it to the designer's trust with stakeholders, teaches designers to recognize when they are genuinely wrong, and emphasizes managing expectations as critical to long-term success.

## Key Takeaways

- **Parkinson's Law of Triviality** — Stakeholders often focus disproportionate time and energy on trivial, visible issues (like "bike-shedding") rather than complex problems, because accessible issues are easier to grasp and comment on.
- **Painting a duck, and why Greever rejects it** — He recounts two tricks the phenomenon has produced: "painting a duck" (Jeff Atwood's term, from Interplay Entertainment: adding an obviously flawed element so the stakeholder removes that instead of the rest) and the 1940s Disney "hairy arms". He then rules both out: "we shouldn't deceive or manipulate our stakeholders in these ways." What he keeps is the underlying point, that all relationships are give and take, and that these situations are better avoided upstream by setting context and fidelity properly and telling stakeholders what feedback is useful.
- **Plan for the changes instead** — The legitimate version of anticipation is to know in advance how you will absorb the changes that will come, for example with pre-planned "real estate", an extra user study, or a reevaluation of the approach.
- **They want to be heard** — Often the real reason stakeholders push for changes is not that their suggestion is better, but that they need reassurance their voice matters and is being taken seriously by the designer.
- **Make changes a design challenge** — When forced to implement changes, treat them as new constraints that improve the design rather than obstacles, by understanding the stakeholder's underlying need and finding a better solution together.
- **The emotional bank account** — Greever takes the term from Stephen Covey's *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People* and applies it to the designer-stakeholder relationship. Every positive interaction is a deposit (agreeing with the stakeholder, metrics improving as promised, showing a design they love) and every negative one a withdrawal (a missed deadline, a failure to follow up on feedback, refusing a change you believe is wrong for the user, which is a withdrawal even when you turn out to be right); maintaining a positive balance is what lets designers choose their battles and disagree when it matters.
- **Managing expectations outranks getting it right** — Greever states it in italics: "Your ability to properly set, adjust, and communicate expectations is more important than your ability to create the perfect solution every time." Stakeholders need to understand *how* a change will be done, what the approach is, and *when* it will be finished, set at the outset and maintained throughout. He illustrates it with a designer he hired and eventually had to let go, who answered concerns defensively and stopped communicating in favour of shipping more features; taking the project over, Greever fixed it purely by managing conversations — listening, a publicly visible prioritised list with time estimates, telling people ahead of time when he could not deliver — and found people wanted confidence in the direction rather than more output faster.
- **Recognize when you're wrong** — Three red flags indicate genuine error: the problem still exists, users don't understand the design, or the majority of stakeholders disagree; admitting mistakes directly and focusing on solutions rebuilds trust faster than defensiveness.

## Quotes

> Your goal is to maintain a positive balance with them at all times.

> It's much better to anticipate these changes and know how to react before they even occur.

> When you own the company, you can take those risks. But until then, allow other people to influence the project, too.

## Concepts

- [[Design Critique]] — the chapter details methods for assessing which feedback to implement, distinguishing between genuine business needs and casual suggestions, and engaging stakeholders in refining their own ideas into better solutions; Greever explores how to evaluate feedback fairly, including recognizing that "unreasonable" stakeholders are often simply unheard rather than irrational, and that the designer's job includes making space for voices to be valued.
- [[Design Leadership]] — refusing to accept all changes indiscriminately and instead treating stakeholder requests as design opportunities requires leadership; the designer must balance stakeholder needs against user needs and communicate that reasoning clearly.
- [[Cognitive Psychology]] — the chapter draws on concepts like Parkinson's Law of Triviality to explain why people default to commenting on trivial visible elements, and why people need to feel heard regardless of whether their suggestion is implementable.
- [[Stakeholder Engagement]] — managing the "emotional bank account" with stakeholders is foundational; admitting mistakes openly, keeping promises, and showing stakeholder ideas were considered builds the trust necessary to make difficult design decisions later.
