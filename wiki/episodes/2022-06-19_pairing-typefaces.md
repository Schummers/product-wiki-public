# The Dos and Don'ts of Pairing Typefaces

## Summary

Choosing and pairing typefaces can feel overwhelming due to many options and classification types. This article provides practical guidelines for selecting and combining typefaces. It covers six typeface classifications, strategies for supporting multiple languages, assessing character distinctness, mixing decorative fonts with neutrals, using font weights for visual hierarchy, and assigning distinct roles to typefaces. The goal is to maximize usability without sacrificing aesthetics through confident, informed design decisions.

## Key Takeaways

- **Six classifications exist** — Serif, sans-serif, slab-serif, script, display, and monospaced typefaces each have distinct characteristics; understanding these helps narrow typeface selection.
- **Support multiple languages** — Choose typefaces that support multiple languages even for single-language sites, since users may translate content; test appearance in different languages before finalizing.
- **Character distinctness matters** — Test how typefaces differentiate numbers and letters, especially the 1, I, and l; this is critical for alphanumeric data and financial or healthcare contexts.
- **Mix decorative with neutral** — Reserve decorative typefaces for headers and illustrations only; pair them with neutral serif or sans-serif for body copy to maintain readability.
- **Font weights create hierarchy** — Using one typeface with multiple weights (light, regular, bold, italic) creates visual hierarchy and balance without needing multiple typefaces.
- **Assign distinct roles** — Each typeface should have a clear role; using two typefaces for body copy makes designs look inconsistent and diminishes trust.
- **Use design systems** — Maintain consistency across products and sites using design systems or style guides that define typeface patterns and roles.

## Quotes

> A typeface, also known as a font family, can set the overall tone and personality of your site or application, as well as increase (or decrease) readability.

> Reserve decorative typefaces that have a lot of personality for less-utilized elements, such as headers or illustrations. Decorative typefaces are difficult to read at small sizes and should never be used for body copy.
