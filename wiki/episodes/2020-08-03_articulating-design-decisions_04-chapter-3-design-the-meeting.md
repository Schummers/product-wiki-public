# Articulating Design Decisions: Chapter 3. Design the Meeting

## Summary

Chapter 3 addresses how to structure and facilitate design meetings with stakeholders to maximize their receptiveness and cognitive capacity. Rather than simply presenting work, the chapter argues that designers must "design the meeting" itself: set appropriate context, manage attention, anticipate reactions, and build support networks. The core principle treats stakeholder meetings like interface design, removing cognitive load so stakeholders can focus on making sound design decisions rather than being distracted by unclear goals, messy visuals, or uncertainty about what feedback is needed.

## Key Takeaways

- **Set the context** — Begin meetings by stating the goal, summarizing previous decisions, showing where you are in the design process on a timeline, specifying what feedback is needed at this stage, and restating the goal. This refresh prevents stakeholders from mental switching costs across multiple meetings.
- **Manage cognitive load** — Apply memory principles (primacy and recency, repetition, surprise, mixed media) to structure the meeting so stakeholders retain what matters. Breaking content into distinct chunks with visual transitions makes information stickier than one long presentation.
- **Remove distractions** — Placeholder content (stock images, lorem ipsum) and visual clutter can derail entire conversations. Invest the extra time to use real copy, aligned layouts, and appropriate imagery so stakeholders focus on real design problems, not placeholder minutiae.
- **Anticipate and prepare for reactions** — Write down objections you expect, prepare alternative designs to address them, and gather supporting data. Most people react predictably based on their role and values, so preparation pays off.
- **Build a support network** — Include people in the meeting who agree with your decisions and are prepared to advocate for them. One-on-one alignment beforehand ensures others can represent your work when the formal meeting happens.
- **Practice the meeting** — Use a printed agenda, practice speaking out loud, and hold a brief team huddle before the meeting. This prep reduces your own cognitive load so you can be present and articulate in the moment.

## Quotes

> We should try to remove as much of the clutter, options, or roadblocks as possible so that our stakeholders' brains are freed to focus on the primary task of the meeting: supporting our design decisions.

> Setting the context doesn't have to be time-consuming. It can be done in a few short sentences or with a single slide. For less formal design reviews, I try to at least make myself a few bullets as talking points to cover these.

> Distractions should only happen once. If you go to two meetings and the same distraction comes up both times, you're doing it wrong.

## Concepts

- [[Design Presentation]] — The chapter emphasizes treating a design meeting as a designed experience that manages stakeholder attention and cognitive load rather than a simple presentation.
- [[Cognitive Load]] — Reducing stakeholder cognitive load is the central framework; the chapter applies this UX principle directly to meeting structure.
- [[Stakeholder Engagement]] — Successful stakeholder buy-in depends on setting context, anticipating reactions, and ensuring stakeholders feel heard and supported.
- [[Design Critique]] — The feedback phase of a meeting requires removing distractions and uncovering real problems so critique becomes constructive.
- [[Workshop Facilitation]] — Meeting structure (breaking into chunks, creating visual transitions, timing pauses) is core facilitation technique.
- [[Active Listening]] — Anticipating what stakeholders will say and letting them talk fully are prerequisites to guiding productive conversation.
