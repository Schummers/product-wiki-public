# Continuous Discovery Habits: Chapter Three: Focusing on Outcomes Over Outputs

## Summary

Torres explores the distinction between business outcomes, product outcomes, and traction metrics, using Sonja's story at tails.com to illustrate the challenges of transitioning to an outcome mindset. She explains how outcomes should be negotiated between product leaders and product trios, addresses whether S.M.A.R.T. goals are necessary, and provides guidance for teams at different maturity levels. The chapter covers the research on goal-setting, particularly the finding that learning goals are more effective than performance goals when facing complex, unfamiliar challenges. Torres also identifies anti-patterns to avoid, such as pursuing too many outcomes at once, ping-ponging between outcomes, setting individual outcomes, and choosing outputs as outcomes.

## Key Takeaways

- **Three outcome types** — Business outcomes measure company progress (often lagging indicators), product outcomes measure how well the product drives business results (leading indicators), and traction metrics track usage of specific features; product trios should be assigned product outcomes, not business outcomes or traction metrics.
- **Leading vs. lagging indicators** — Teams make more progress on leading indicators that predict the direction of lagging business outcomes, allowing faster feedback cycles than waiting for business-level metrics.
- **Learning then performance goals** — Research shows that when teams face complex, new outcomes, starting with learning goals (discover strategies that work) is more effective than jumping to specific, challenging performance goals; only after discovering strategies should teams commit to performance targets.
- **Two-way negotiation** — Product leaders should communicate business context and strategic priorities while product trios should communicate customer knowledge and realistic impact estimates; both inputs are essential for setting appropriate outcomes.
- **Autonomy through outcomes** — Outcomes give teams autonomy to find the best solutions, communicate uncertainty rather than false certainty, and help teams align around priorities without dictating specific features.
- **Anti-patterns to avoid** — Do not pursue too many outcomes simultaneously, ping-pong between outcomes quarter-to-quarter, assign outcomes to individuals instead of teams, confuse outputs with outcomes, or focus on one outcome to the detriment of health metrics.

## Quotes

## Concepts

- [[Product Outcome]] — Torres distinguishes product outcomes from business outcomes and traction metrics, explaining when each is appropriate and how they should be chosen through negotiation between leaders and teams.
- [[Decision Making]] — Setting outcomes requires making strategic decisions about which customer needs matter most to the business, and these decisions should reflect both business context and team capacity.
- [[Stakeholder Engagement]] — Product leaders must communicate business priorities while allowing teams autonomy to explore solutions; showing work on an opportunity solution tree helps bring stakeholders along.
- [[Product Trio]] — Outcomes should be assigned to the trio as a unit, not to individual members, to ensure collaboration between product managers, designers, and engineers in pursuing shared goals.
