# Continuous Discovery Habits: Chapter Ten: Testing Assumptions, Not Ideas

## Summary

This chapter teaches teams how to test assumptions underlying multiple ideas rather than testing entire ideas one at a time. Torres explains how to design assumption tests that simulate an experience and evaluate whether participants behave as expected, while emphasizing the importance of defining success criteria upfront to avoid confirmation bias. The chapter advocates for starting with small, fast tests to gather early signals before investing in larger experiments, discusses false positives and false negatives in small-sample testing, and clarifies that product teams seek to mitigate risk, not establish scientific truth. It concludes with practical testing methods (unmoderated user testing, one-question surveys, data-mining) and common anti-patterns to avoid.

## Key Takeaways

- **Test assumptions across sets of ideas** — Testing one idea at a time invites confirmation bias and escalation of commitment; instead, systematically test assumptions shared across multiple ideas to compare and contrast ideas and select clear front-runners.
- **Simulate and evaluate behavior** — A strong assumption test simulates the specific moment in time when a customer would behave according to your assumption; success is evaluated not by what people say they would do but by how they actually behave in the simulation.
- **Define success criteria upfront** — Specify in advance how many participants you will test with and how many must exhibit the desired behavior (e.g., "at least 3 out of 10"); this aligns the team around what results mean and guards against confirmation bias.
- **Start small and iterate** — Begin with fast, small-sample tests (5–10 participants) completed in a day or two to gather early signals; only invest in larger-scale experiments if early signals warrant the additional time and cost.
- **False positives and negatives are manageable** — Small-sample tests may yield false positives (test passes but assumption is faulty) or false negatives (test fails but assumption is valid); these errors are acceptable because teams run multiple tests and can use triangulation (multiple research methods on one assumption) to validate findings.
- **Multiple testing methods** — Unmoderated user testing, one-question surveys, and data-mining are effective rapid testing tools; teams should choose methods based on what will provide reliable data without excessive time investment.

## Quotes

> If we test only one idea at a time, confirmation bias will rear its ugly head. We'll be more likely to notice the confirming evidence and miss the disconfirming evidence.

> The burden of truth is too much. You are simply trying to reduce risk.

> Our goal as a product team is not to seek truth but to mitigate risk.

## Concepts

- [[Assumption Testing]] — The chapter teaches teams to break ideas into testable assumptions and design small, fast experiments to collect evidence about each assumption; this approach enables rapid iteration and reduces the risk of investing time in flawed ideas.
- [[Assumption Mapping]] — Referenced as the tool (from Chapter 9) that displays assumptions along a spectrum of known to unknown, with the goal of moving risky assumptions to the "more known" side through successive rounds of testing.
- [[Decision Making]] — The chapter emphasizes how assumption tests provide data that teams use to make go/no-go decisions about ideas and opportunities; success criteria defined upfront ensure the team agrees on what the results mean and avoids individual bias in interpretation.
- [[Prioritization]] — Testing assumptions across sets of ideas requires comparing and contrasting results to identify which ideas are most promising; the chapter advocates for looking for "clear front runners" rather than pursuing all ideas equally.
