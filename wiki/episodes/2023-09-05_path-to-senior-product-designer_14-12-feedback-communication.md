# The Path to Senior Product Designer: 12. Feedback (Communication)

## Summary

This chapter addresses feedback as a critical mechanism for ensuring solution alignment, quality, and successful shipping. It covers both sides of the feedback exchange: providing feedback (non-design and design) and receiving it (understanding, evaluating, prioritizing, and responding). The chapter emphasizes structured frameworks for making feedback more effective and introduces a method for tracking and learning from feedback patterns to identify areas for personal growth.

## Key Takeaways

- **One framework for non-design feedback, branching at the last step** — Provide evidence (describe the behavior and give concrete examples), articulate impact, and for positive feedback encourage continuation or for negative feedback position a solution as a question. Start conversations with openings like "Can we talk about X?" or "I'd be happy to give you some feedback; do you have time now?"
- **Receiving design feedback requires processing structure** — Understand the root cause by asking clarifying questions; evaluate relevancy against project context (readiness level, scope, original request); prioritize using signals like "How strongly do you feel about it?", a habit the author says he picked up from an ex-colleague, or tagging with severity levels (#fyi, #suggestion, #recommendation, #plea); and respond in one of four categories: Yes, Yes but later, Requires research, or No.
- **Providing design feedback follows seven steps** — Ensure it's valuable and relevant; understand what led to the current solution; raise problems with evidence; provide severity status; and offer solutions when appropriate. Ask clarifying questions like "Could you explain the reasoning behind…?" to avoid suggesting solutions before understanding the designer's intent.
- **Feedback prioritization requires a severity system** — Without a clear system, designers become overwhelmed. Methods include asking how strongly someone feels, using severity phrases from "It's not critical" to "I think it's critical we…," or adopting tagging systems like HubSpot's FlashTags (#fyi, #suggestion, #recommendation, #plea).
- **Learn from feedback by tracking patterns** — Document feedback on things in your control, categorize it (oral/written communication, UX skills, strategy, etc.), and calculate frequency and severity scores. Patterns reveal areas for improvement more reliably than individual incidents.

## Quotes

> "A study featured in Harvard Business Review shows that people do want to receive feedback, especially negative feedback. According to the study, no one likes to provide negative feedback, but everyone wants to hear it."

> "Design may be the role that has to deal with the most feedback inside the organization."

> "To learn from feedback, document what you thought was the right thing to ship and what was eventually shipped, and then find the delta between the two. That delta is an indicator of what areas you could potentially improve."

## Concepts

- [[Design Critique]] — Structured meetings where feedback is given and received; the chapter emphasizes frameworks for extracting value from critiques by understanding root causes, evaluating relevancy, and prioritizing feedback before responding.
- [[Team Collaboration]] — Feedback frameworks that improve teamwork and product quality; both positive and negative feedback, when delivered with evidence and impact, increase team efficiency by removing unnecessary discussion cycles and aligning on solutions.
- [[Performance Review]] — Tracking feedback patterns over time to identify areas for personal improvement; categorizing feedback by type and severity reveals which skills or competencies need development, turning individual feedback into growth signals.
