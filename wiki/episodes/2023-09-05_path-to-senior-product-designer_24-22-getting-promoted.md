# The Path to Senior Product Designer: 22. Getting Promoted

## Summary

This chapter explains that promotion requires not just strong work but active communication of that work to multiple stakeholders. The chapter presents a five-step process: understanding promotion criteria, communicating to your manager your desire for promotion, building a growth plan aligned with promotion criteria, executing that plan at the target role's level, and documenting your achievements. Central to the chapter is the concept that you must do the work of the higher role before receiving the promotion, as exemplified by Square's principle that "Promotions are descriptive not prescriptive." The chapter then addresses the often-overlooked competency of self-advocacy or visibility, frames promotion as a three-part work process (producing, organizing, self-promoting), and provides concrete tools for gathering support and building a case, culminating in a detailed format for an "accomplishments document" that translates work into business impact.

## Key Takeaways

- **Promotion requires a formal request and a documented case, not just strong performance** — seeking promotion is appropriate and viewed positively in healthy organizations; you must ask for it, set a target date with your manager, and consistently communicate progress.
- **You must perform at the target role's level before receiving the promotion** — promotions don't unlock responsibilities; the work at the higher level comes first, which means you need to proactively seek opportunities that increase your scope and impact.
- **Self-advocacy and visibility are critical and often overlooked competencies** — while distinct from your actual work, visibility determines whether people know about and can champion your achievements; Shreyas Doshi identifies three work types (producing, organizing, self-promoting), and Ray Dalio notes that professionals manage impressions alongside their actual job.
- **Support networks significantly improve promotion likelihood** — colleagues can offer experience and opportunities; your manager provides education and ongoing feedback; a sponsor (with 21% higher promotion likelihood according to Harvard Business Review) advocates behind the scenes and makes introductions to key people.
- **Business impact is the lens for translating all work into promotion arguments** — the chapter provides a framework showing that design drives business impact through acquisition, conversion, retention, reducing churn, support efforts, and cost reduction; documenting achievements through this lens (effort, output, impact) makes the promotion case more compelling.

## Quotes

> Unfortunately, if you skipped right to this chapter without implementing the work I've described so far, this will not get you promoted.

> Promotions are descriptive not prescriptive [...] Promotions don't unlock new responsibilities; the new responsibilities and increased scope come first and then we recognize it with a promotion.

> In most companies people are doing two jobs: their actual job and the job of managing others' impressions of how they're doing their job.

## Concepts

- [[Promotion]] — presents a comprehensive five-step process, identifies self-advocacy as a critical often-overlooked competency, provides a framework for building a promotion case centered on business impact, and introduces the accomplishments document format with sections for projects, work, initiatives, and learnings.
- [[Performance Review]] — describes how accomplishments should be documented systematically and shared in one-on-ones before the formal review, turning the review into a summarization of already-known achievements rather than a surprise.
- [[Business Impact]] — provides detailed frameworks showing how design decisions drive revenue (acquisition, conversion, retention, churn reduction) and savings (support reduction, cost reduction, labor reduction), emphasizes translating every achievement through this lens.
- [[Stakeholder Engagement]] — covers gathering support from colleagues, managers, and sponsors; distinguishes that managers cannot single-handedly promote you and that promotions are decided by committees at most companies; emphasizes building relationships with key stakeholders.
- [[Design Advocacy]] — discusses visibility-building strategies including mentorship, documentation, knowledge sharing, interest groups, progress updates, peer feedback, hiring participation, and external initiatives that raise awareness of your work.
