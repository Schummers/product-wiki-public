# 5 Facilitation Principles for Both UX Workshops and User Tests

## Summary

While UX workshops and user tests have distinct goals and participant structures, the core facilitation skills transfer between them. UX designers often feel stronger in workshop facilitation but weaker in research moderation, while UX researchers may feel the opposite. The article identifies five facilitation principles that apply to both contexts: beginning with goals or research questions, following a guide, being open to improvisation, encouraging action, and employing intentional silence. These principles reflect that both workshops and tests require careful planning combined with flexibility to respond to participant behavior and emerging insights.

## Key Takeaways

- **Begin with goals, not activities** — Define what you want to achieve before planning workshop activities or user tasks. Post and review goals to keep participants focused and aligned throughout.
- **Follow a detailed guide** — Skilled facilitators prepare extensively with guides including agendas, timing, research questions, backup activities, happy paths for tasks, and post-task questions. Preparation makes facilitation look effortless.
- **Improvise when needed** — While planning is essential, great facilitators know when to deviate. If an activity isn't working, provide more direction. If a test participant explores an unexpected area revealing valuable insights, encourage that exploration.
- **Encourage action** — Both workshops and tests require participants to *do* things, not just talk about them. Frame tasks as commands ("Find an insurance policy") rather than questions ("How would you find an insurance policy?").
- **Use intentional silence strategically** — Silence allows space for thinking, prevents leading participants, and signals that the facilitator is listening. Count to 7 before interrupting; often the participant will self-resolve the situation.

## Quotes

> Humans like to connect and share. Being with other people and not talking to them can be uncomfortable.

> Know that silence does not equal lack of engagement. Rather, it could mean that the participant is thinking.

> If you believe you are good at one but not the other, capitalize on the skills you are confident that you already have and try them out in a different context.

## Concepts

- [[Workshop Facilitation]] — Workshop facilitation relies on core competencies that transfer across workshops and user tests, including starting with goals, preparing guides, and supporting collaborative thinking.
- [[Usability Testing]] — User test facilitation benefits from goal-driven planning and specific strategies including intentional silence (counting to 7 before speaking), visual cues, encouragement to act, and providing backup activities.
