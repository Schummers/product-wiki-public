# Getting into the Discovery Mindset

## Summary

Maria Rosala explores the critical mindset shift required for effective discovery work: moving from a solution-focused approach to an exploration-focused approach. The article identifies two cognitive biases that derail discovery when teams start with solutions in mind: anchoring (interpreting research through the lens of a preferred solution) and confirmation bias (seeking evidence that validates the chosen solution while ignoring contradictory evidence). Rosala provides three concrete methods to foster a discovery mindset: reframing goals around problems rather than solutions, explicitly surfacing assumptions and unknowns, and "iceboxing" solution ideas until research is complete.

## Key Takeaways

- **Solution mindset causes discovery to fail** — when teams begin with a preferred solution, they conduct anchored research, seek confirming evidence, and risk building the wrong thing.
- **Cognitive biases distort research** — anchoring causes teams to test solutions instead of exploring problems; confirmation bias causes teams to ignore evidence that contradicts their preferred direction.
- **Reframe goals around problems, not solutions** — starting discovery with a problem-focused goal (e.g., "provide users with data to make good decisions") rather than a solution goal (e.g., "identify how an AI chatbot can help") opens the discovery space.
- **Surface and prioritize unknowns explicitly** — at the start of discovery, teams should identify what they're assuming versus what they actually know, then prioritize which unknowns are most important and risky.
- **Icebox solution ideas until research is done** — documenting solution ideas at the beginning of discovery, then agreeing to "freeze" them until after research is complete, frees teams from anchoring while preserving the ideas for later consideration.
- **Unknown unknowns drive real insights** — the most valuable discoveries often surface unexpected insights the team didn't know to look for; maintaining openness to surprise is essential.

## Quotes

> Discovery helps us decide what to build or what direction to pursue. To perform discovery well, we have to adopt a particular mindset: an exploration mindset and an openness to learn new things.

> Starting discovery with a solution in mind can limit your ability to discover valuable insights about your users and their needs and the problem you want to solve. You also risk building the wrong thing.

> A discovery mindset means that a team is open to learning

- **That their assumptions are wrong**, whether those assumptions are about users' problems, needs, or the best solution.
- **New things they hadn't expected to learn** (these are known as *unknown unknowns*). *Unknown unknowns* can sometimes change the trajectory of a discovery as a team gains new, important knowledge about the problem domain.

## Concepts

- [[Problem Framing]] — the process of defining and articulating the problem space before proposing solutions, ensuring research focuses on understanding the real problem rather than validating assumptions.
- [[Cognitive Bias]] — systematic patterns in how teams and individuals think that can distort discovery and design decisions, including anchoring and confirmation bias.
- [[Solution Thinking]] — the tendency to focus on solutions early in the design process, which can prevent teams from fully understanding user needs and exploring alternative approaches.
