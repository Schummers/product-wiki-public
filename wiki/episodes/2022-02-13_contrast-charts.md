# Contrast: One of the 3Cs for Better Charts

## Summary

This article is the third in a three-part series on the "3 Cs for Better Charts": context, clutter-free, and contrast. Chart design should guide viewers' attention to key findings using highlighting, annotations, and contrast. Data editor Amanda Cox from the New York Times emphasizes that "the annotation layer is the most important thing," noting that explicitly telling audiences what to pay attention to is helpful and reduces cognitive burden compared to making viewers search for meaning. Nielsen Norman Group provides guidance on using color, titles, and callouts as tools to create contrast and draw attention to intended messages.

## Key Takeaways

- **Use color strategically to highlight key data** — Following Jonathan Schwabish's advice to "start with gray," initially make all chart elements grayscale, then strategically add color only to values or series most important to your point.
- **Active titles state findings, not just describe data** — Titles should describe implications and takeaways (e.g., "Participants struggled to find past bills") rather than passive descriptions (e.g., "Success rates by task"); active titles tell the conclusion rather than forcing viewers to search for it.
- **Connect titles to data through color** — Using the same color in chart titles as in the highlighted data value helps viewers quickly connect those elements and understand the takeaway.
- **Callouts add context to important events** — Annotations can mark redesigns, sales spikes, external events, or other contextual information on charts; callouts ensure that context is retained if the chart is shared beyond a presentation.
- **Avoid bias and manipulation through honesty** — Emphasizing findings is different from manipulating charts to mislead; always be transparent about data and don't ignore findings that contradict expectations.
- **Test charts with intended audiences** — Show charts to audience members and ask them to summarize their interpretation; if unclear, return to the 3 Cs to determine what changes (more context, less clutter, more contrast) are needed.

## Quotes

> "Tell me what I should look at. […] Otherwise, you put too much of the burden on someone and say, 'You go figure it out.' […] We don't want to make scavenger hunts."

> "Designing a chart to emphasize a finding is not the same as manipulating a chart to make it misleading. Don't abuse these techniques to trick your audience."

> "It's always best to be honest and upfront about your data. If there's a data point or finding that doesn't conform with your expectations, don't ignore it. Transparency is always the best policy when it comes to reporting research."

## Concepts

- [[Data Visualization]] — Adding contrast through color, titles, and callouts guides viewers' attention to key findings; annotation layers are the most important tool for making data communicative.
- [[Visual Contrast]] — Strategic use of color (starting with gray, then selectively highlighting key values), typography emphasis, and annotations creates contrast directing viewer attention to intended messages.
- [[Chart Annotation]] — Callouts and labels added to charts provide context, mark important events, and ensure context persists if charts are shared; reduces cognitive load on viewers to interpret meaning.
- [[Data Communication]] — Effective chart communication requires active titles stating conclusions, strategic highlighting, and explicit guidance on what viewers should understand; testing with audiences reveals whether charts achieve their intended message.
