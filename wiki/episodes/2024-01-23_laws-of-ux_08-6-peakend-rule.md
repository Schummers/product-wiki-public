# Laws of UX: 6. Peak–End Rule

## Summary

Chapter 6 of Jon Yablonski's Laws of UX explores the peak-end rule, a principle stemming from research on how people evaluate experiences. Rather than judging an experience based on the total sum or average of every moment, people recall and evaluate experiences primarily based on how they felt at the peak emotional moment and at the end. This observation, first explored in 1993 by Daniel Kahneman and colleagues, has significant implications for product design, where strategic focus on emotional peaks and conclusions can leave lasting positive impressions on users.

## Key Takeaways

- **Peak-End Evaluation** — Users judge experiences largely by the emotional intensity at the peak and the end, not the total duration or average experience. In a randomized colonoscopy study, patients whose procedure was extended by three extra minutes with the scope tip left in experienced the final moments as less painful, rated the whole experience as less unpleasant, and were more likely to return for subsequent procedures than patients who underwent the standard, shorter version.

- **Memory Over Duration** — In the 1993 hand-immersion experiment by Kahneman and colleagues, participants were more willing to repeat the second trial (60 seconds in 14°C water plus 30 seconds as the water was warmed to 15°C) than the first (60 seconds in 14°C water), despite the longer exposure; the authors concluded they simply preferred the memory of it.

- **Design for Key Moments** — Products should deliberately design around emotional peaks and endings. Spotify Wrapped, Duolingo's gamified achievement celebrations, and Legendary Level rewards exemplify using peak moments to create lasting positive impressions.

- **Mitigate Negativity** — Humans experience negativity bias: we register and dwell on negative events more readily than positive ones. Designs must prevent negative peaks through proactive guidance (like real-time password validation in Mailchimp) or humanize unavoidable friction with brand personality.

- **Uber's Wait Time Strategy** — Uber addresses waiting's negative emotional peak by combining idleness aversion (entertaining animations), operational transparency (estimated times, calculation clarity), and the goal gradient effect (progressive updates), reducing post-request cancellation rates.

- **Journey Mapping Technique** — Identifying emotional peaks requires journey mapping: visualizing the user's perspective (persona and expectations), their experience (actions, mindset, emotions), and actionable insights. The emotional layer captures where peaks occur.

## Quotes

> People judge an experience largely based on how they felt at its peak and at its end, rather than on the total sum or average of every moment of the experience.

> Our memories are rarely a perfectly accurate record of events.

> We remember intensely emotional events more than less emotional events, and this has an effect on how we perceive an experience: we recall not the sum of how we felt throughout the experience but the average of how we felt during its peak emotional moments and at its end.

## Concepts

- [[Peak-End Rule]] — The core principle that experience evaluation hinges on peak emotional moments and endings rather than total duration.

- [[Cognitive Bias]] — A cognitive bias impairing memory recall; people remember intense emotional events more vividly, directly driving peak-end evaluation; The human tendency to register and dwell on negative events more readily; must be mitigated through preventive guidance or reframing.

- [[Emotional Design]] — Chapter focuses on designing to create positive emotional peaks and endings that leave lasting impressions on users.

- [[Journey Mapping]] — A qualitative design technique to visualize the user experience across phases, mindset, and emotional states, identifying peaks for strategic focus.
