# Ideation for Everyday Design Challenges

## Summary

Ideation is the process of generating a broad set of ideas on a given topic without evaluating them. Unlike brainstorming (a formal method), ideation is an umbrella term for many approaches to idea generation. The article emphasizes that ideation is about quantity over quality, conducted within the Explore phase of design thinking after user research has defined the problem. Ideation should occur before prototyping and is critical to parallel design, where multiple solutions are explored rather than settling on the first idea.

## Key Takeaways

- **Ideation prioritizes quantity over quality** — generating many ideas increases the likelihood that one will seed a great solution; the focus is on breadth rather than judging individual ideas.
- **Ideas must not be evaluated during generation** — judgment stifles creativity; postponing evaluation creates psychological safety for participants to contribute without fear.
- **Ideas must be recorded and documented** — tangible artifacts (notes, sketches, sticky notes) capture ideas before they dissipate and serve as inspiration for further ideas during the session.
- **Collaboration generates diverse ideas** — while individuals can ideate alone, groups produce greater quantity and variety; diverse backgrounds and expertise create wider solution space exploration.
- **Ideation requires grounded problem statements** — ideas must be grounded in user research and real needs, not technology or trends; skipping research leads to worthless ideas.
- **Parallel design yields better outcomes** — exploring multiple solutions before designing increases the probability of discovering optimal paths rather than merely acceptable ones.
- **Stepping away improves evaluation** — after ideation, breaks between generation and evaluation allow for better decision-making without fatigue and signal phase transitions.

## Quotes

> Ideation: The process of generating a broad set of ideas on a given topic, with no attempt to judge or evaluate them.

> If you don't understand your users, the ideas you generate will be worthless: they may not be on target, and, even worse, you won't have the correct evaluation criteria for determining which idea is the best and should be implemented.

> It is much easier to scale back a crazy idea that addresses a true user need, than to try to make a mundane idea desirable.
