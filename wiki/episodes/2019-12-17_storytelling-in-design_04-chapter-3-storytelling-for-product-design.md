# Storytelling in Design: Chapter 3. Storytelling for Product Design

## Summary

As devices proliferate and users expect seamless omnichannel experiences, product design must borrow storytelling techniques to handle complexity. This chapter shows how the beginning-middle-end structure Aristotle defined is being reshaped by on-demand consumption, audience participation, data-driven personalization, and multiplatform delivery. Rather than a designer controlling the story, users now choose when, where, and how they engage—and expect products to follow them across devices.

## Key Takeaways

- **The shift to on-demand consumption** — Users now expect "tap of a button" access to products and services on their own schedule, any device, any location. Dahlström cites data showing that the share of global web pages served on a mobile phone rose from 0.7% in 2009 to 52.2% in 2018; elsewhere in the chapter she notes that the average smartphone user in the US checks their phone 150 times a day. This reshapes product design from broadcast to always-available accessibility.

- **The audience as storytellers** — YouTube, Twitch, Snapchat, Facebook and Instagram have made it easy for anyone to publish and reach an audience, and the platforms keep evolving with them: Instagram launched IGTV in 2018 for long-form vertical video up to an hour, against the 15 seconds of Instagram Stories. Where long-form video once took months and a considerable budget, brands now have to be nimble and fast to keep up with how quickly their users produce content. Dahlström's example is Rosey Blair's July 2018 tweet series about a seat switch on a plane, which over nine hundred thousand people had liked at the time of writing (the tweet has since been deleted) and which media from the BBC to Buzzfeed picked up.

- **The protagonist is the user** — Immersive experiences (VR, AR, interactive theater like Punchdrunk's *Sleep No More*) place the user inside the story rather than as observer. The strongest product messaging is indirect: "what using this will enable you to do" rather than company-focused promotion. The most effective products and campaigns focus on users and customers, not the brand.

- **Participative and interactive storytelling** — In a Latitude study about what audiences want, 79% suggested wanting interactions that would let them influence the characters' decisions and the way the story developed, or become characters themselves. Dahlström notes this mirrors the choose-your-own-adventure (CYOA) genre and modern examples like Steven Soderbergh's *Mosaic* (interactive iOS/Android app). Products must be flexible enough to support user-driven navigation rather than single happy paths.

- **Data-driven storytelling: combine data with expertise, not vice versa** — Netflix analyzed the organic viewing behaviour of its entire platform and identified that people who watched the original BBC *House of Cards* also watched films directed by David Fincher and starring Kevin Spacey, which led to the new version. In a TED talk contrasting this with Amazon, which looked at a controlled subset of pilot episodes for *Alpha House*, data scientist Sebastian Wernicke argued that data alone is good only for dissecting a problem and understanding its variables: industry expertise has to be combined with it. With *House of Cards*, the data didn't specifically direct Netflix to license the show, it pointed the company in the right direction.

- **New formats demand new disciplines** — Snapchat's 10-second limit, Instagram Stories' 15-second (or 24-hour) windows, and vertical video changed brand content strategy. After Instagram followed Snapchat with 15-second "Stories" that disappeared after 24 hours, both platforms adapted: Snapchat let users save snaps as "Memories," which Instagram called "Highlights," and Snapchat's original 10-second limit was removed in 2017. Brands shifted budgets from TV to ephemeral stories. Listening to users' format preferences is critical to staying relevant.

- **Transmedia storytelling spreads story across platforms with different entry points and continuity** — The Norwegian show *Skam* aired clips in real time (same time as the characters' in-world events), with parallel Instagram accounts and text messages on the website. Users could enter the story anywhere and explore across platforms. 82% of audiences prefer complementary apps over duplicated content, so each platform's role must be distinct.

## Concepts

- [[Narrative Structure]] — The chapter moves beyond the beginning, middle and end "just as Aristotle defined it" to show how on-demand, participative, and data-driven platforms are fragmenting the linear story. Product design must now handle branching narratives where users choose entry points and sequence.

- [[Storytelling]] — Dahlström defines *transmedia storytelling* as a technique used to tell stories across multiple platforms—TV, radio, novels, games, online social media, a website—where the audience can enter through different entry points and keep exploring across platforms. *Skam* is the primary example: clips published in real time on the show's website, in-world text messages the characters sent each other readable on that same website, and their Instagram accounts, each channel playing a distinct narrative role.

- [[Nonlinear Storytelling]] — The chapter covers how choose-your-own-adventure (CYOA) and interactive media allow audiences to "influence the characters' decisions and thereby the way the story developed." Dahlström cites *Mosaic* and user desire for agency, then notes the nuance: co-creation can often shorten the production process, but in instances like the AXE Anarchy campaign (users' choices turned into a comic book by the agency Razorfish) it can also take longer, and *Mosaic*'s reviews were mixed, some finding the storyline confusing and the plot easy to miss bits of.

- [[Omnichannel Experience]] — Dahlström opens the section on the analogy that "in a well-told story, everything from big to small details comes together," then defines omnichannel experiences as cross-channel experiences spanning social media, physical locations, ecommerce, mobile apps and desktops. Online and offline increasingly merge; companies that optimize so users move seamlessly across channels gain a competitive advantage, in higher conversion and increased brand loyalty.
