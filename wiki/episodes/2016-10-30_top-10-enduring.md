# Top 10 Enduring Web-Design Mistakes

## Summary

Despite two decades of usability research and documented best practices, the same web design mistakes persist across modern websites. A large-scale usability study of 215 participants across 43 diverse sites identified these persistent problems: they stem not from lack of knowledge but from organizational challenges, poor content strategy, inadequate search infrastructure, and insufficient user research integration. Modern design patterns and aesthetics change, but underlying user needs remain constant.

## Key Takeaways

- **Unexpected content locations** — When site structure doesn't match users' mental models of how information should be organized, people cannot locate what they need; card sorting and usability testing can reveal structure that makes sense to users.
- **Competing links and categories** — Similar category or link names force users to guess which path to take; clear differentiation, tested through card sorting and usability testing, ensures users select the right section.
- **Islands of information** — Related information scattered across the site with no links between pieces leaves users unable to discover supplementary material; consolidating content and linking between related pieces improves discoverability.
- **Repetitive links** — Poor workflows force users to select the same information multiple times in sequence; the quality of clicks matters far more than quantity, and users should get closer to their goal with each action.
- **Hidden fees and prices** — People want to know costs before committing to a complex process; progressive disclosure of pricing information prevents abandonment and frustration.
- **Stranding users on microsites** — Separate subsites can trap users who don't realize they've left the main site and can't find their way back; clear return paths and consistent navigation prevent confusion.
- **Poor search results** — Search that doesn't match user queries or searches only partial site content wastes user time and can lead to reliance on inaccurate information; review search logs regularly and tag content properly.
- **Flawed filters and facets** — A one-size-fits-all approach to filtering criteria fails different user needs; enable both "include" and "exclude" options, and tailor facets to content types.
- **Overwhelming users with information** — Dense walls of text and cluttered pages prevent scanning; use short sentences, bulleted lists, headings, and bold keywords to structure information for scanning.
- **Hidden links** — Links styled to resemble advertisements are ignored due to banner blindness; keep link styling consistent with site design rather than making them visually prominent in ways that appear ad-like.

## Quotes

> People can't use information they can't find.

> Remember that users scan , not read, online.

> Many, if not all, of the mistakes listed above could be easily identified by including user research and usability testing in the website-development process and paying attention to the findings from that research.

## Concepts

- [[Information Architecture]] — Aligning site structure with user mental models is critical; surprises in content location stem from organization based on company thinking rather than user expectations.
- [[Navigation Design]] — Competing categories, repetitive links, and disappearing navigation are persistent problems that basic usability testing can identify and fix.
- [[Search]] — Site search remains a major weakness; search logs reveal what users actually seek and show where results fail to match queries or content isn't indexed.
- [[Content Strategy]] — Overlapping category names and scattered, undifferentiated content stem from poor content strategy; consolidating related information and clear labeling prevent user confusion and duplication.
