# The Path to Senior Product Designer: 15. Shipping (Ownership)

## Summary

This chapter addresses the tactical and strategic aspects of shipping work faster and more consistently. It teaches designers to protect their time from meetings and interruptions, optimize their personal productivity rhythms, manage the tension between quality and speed, and track how long work actually takes. The core premise is that time is non-renewable, so shipping more requires intentional decisions about what to say no to and when to ship "good enough" rather than perfect.

## Key Takeaways

- **Time Audit for Shipping** — Map your current time allocation across activities (designing, meetings, learning, mentoring), compare it to your ideal allocation, and identify recurring meetings as a good target, since cancelling one frees time at scale with a single decision.
- **Protect Half-Day Blocks** — Citing Paul Graham's essay Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule: managers' days consist of one-hour slots, while makers (engineers, designers, writers) need half-day slots to make significant progress; scheduling meetings at the beginning or end of your work sprint preserves significant unbroken time.
- **Productivity Peaks and Valleys** — Find when you are most productive (early morning, afternoon), protect those hours for challenging work, use low-productivity hours for routine tasks, and experiment with work intervals like 90-minute sprints followed by rest.
- **"Good Enough" Over Perfect** — A design is often "good enough to ship" before designers are satisfied with it; the point at which design achieves its goal often comes before designers are satisfied, and everything after that is low ROI work.
- **Time Tracking Predicts Future Estimates** — Track time on projects so you accumulate data on how long similar tasks actually take (accounting for revisions, meetings, feedback cycles), which becomes the most accurate way to estimate future work.

## Quotes

> According to a study by John Pencavel, of Stanford University, productivity falls sharply above 50 work hours and any additional hour worked above 55 hours does not contribute at all to overall productivity.

> "Done is better than perfect" said one of the posters in the Facebook headquarters in the early days of the company.

> Makers (engineers, designers, writers), on the other hand, need half-day slots to make significant progress.

## Concepts

- [[Design Ownership]] — shipping work completely and being accountable for what gets built, which requires being protective of time and making conscious trade-offs between quality and speed to move projects across the finish line.
- [[Productivity]] — deliberately optimizing when and how you work by protecting peak hours, removing distractions, using work intervals that match your biology, and declining interruptions.
- [[Design Strategy]] — making deliberate decisions about what qualifies as "good enough to ship" and understanding the business goal that the design needs to achieve so you can stop iterating at the right point.
- [[Business Impact]] — the chapter treats work done past the point where a design already achieves its goal as low-ROI effort, and argues that understanding the business side is what lets a designer judge when a design is good enough to ship.
