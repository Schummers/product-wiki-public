# When to Use Which User-Experience Research Methods

## Summary

UX has many research methods available, but teams often use only one or two they know best. A three-dimensional framework helps determine which method to use: attitudinal versus behavioral (what people say versus what they do), qualitative versus quantitative (direct observation versus measurement), and context of use (natural, scripted, limited, or decontextualized). Methods map to product-development phases: generative methods for strategy, formative methods for design, and summative methods for launch and assessment. The article describes 20 popular UX methods positioned along these dimensions.

## Key Takeaways

- **Three dimensions organize methods** — Attitudinal versus behavioral distinguishes beliefs from actions; qualitative versus quantitative distinguishes direct observation from measurement; context varies from natural to decontextualized use.
- **Behavioral data preferred** — While attitudinal methods like surveys and focus groups have value, behavioral methods like usability testing and field studies usually provide more reliable insights about actual use.
- **Qualitative answers why, quantitative answers how much** — Qualitative methods (interviews, testing) are better for understanding reasons and fixes; quantitative methods (surveys, analytics) better answer scale and impact questions.
- **Context affects validity** — Natural use provides external validity; scripted use focuses insights on specific areas; limited use isolates specific aspects; decontextualized studies examine broader issues beyond usage.
- **Methods map to development phases** — Generative methods (interviews, field studies) inform strategy; formative methods (card sorting, usability testing) improve design; summative methods (benchmarking, A/B testing) measure performance.
- **Combine methods** — Most projects benefit from multiple methods providing complementary insights; combining attitudinal and behavioral, qualitative and quantitative strengthens understanding.
- **Adjust to context** — Method selection depends on study goals, available resources, and team familiarity; guidelines are general, not rigid classifications.

## Quotes

> The field of user experience has a wide range of research methods available, ranging from tried-and-true methods such as lab-based usability testing to those that have been more recently developed, such as unmoderated UX assessments.

> While it's not realistic to use the full set of methods on a given project, nearly all projects would benefit from multiple research methods and from combining insights.

> These classifications are meant to help you make the best choice at the right time.
