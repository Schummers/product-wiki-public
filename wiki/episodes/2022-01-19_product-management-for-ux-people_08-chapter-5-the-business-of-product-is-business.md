# Product Management for UX People: CHAPTER 5: The Business of Product Is Business

## Summary

This chapter challenges the common UX resistance to "business" and makes the case that sustainable value requires both customer value and business value. Crumlish walks through the market side of product management: defining a target market (a specific enough segment to be meaningful, not "everyone"), sizing it with humility about what "1%" actually costs, and understanding that reaching a million customers from a hundred-million-person pool is not inherently easier than reaching a million from a four-million-person pool. Finding product-market fit—which he says is subjective, but on which there is broad consensus among investors and product folk that around 40% of users saying they would be "very disappointed" to lose your product marks it—is the first job of early-stage product work; time spent optimizing a product that has not yet found fit is wasted. The chapter then covers go-to-market strategy (coordinating messaging, partnerships, pricing, and resources to reach the target market), customer obsession (deep relationships with users and the teams that support them), and the choice between launching new products and optimizing existing ones—recognizing that different PMs thrive in each mode. The final sections address business operations (project and program management), financial literacy (understanding cost, value, and sustainability without necessarily owning P&L), and the specific demands of B2B product management, where the end user and the payer are often different people, relationships are specific and known, and change management matters far more than it does in B2C.

## Key Takeaways

- **Business and user value are not contradictory.** Sustainable value accrues to both customers and the business itself; if the business goes under, customers lose the product anyway. Getting the balance right means neither raking too much value off the top (compromising quality) nor leaving too much on the table (causing business failure).
- **Market sizing requires rigor and humility.** Defining a target market specifically (e.g., "adults in the US aged 18–35 thinking about buying their first new car") helps you craft experiences that reach it. However, Crumlish argues that reaching a million customers out of a hundred million is "not significantly easier (or even necessarily easier at all)" than reaching a million out of four million; the ease of capturing "a mere 1%" is an illusion based on a huge denominator.
- **Product-market fit is the first job.** Around 40% of users saying they would be "very disappointed" to lose your product is a broad consensus marker of fit. Before achieving fit, optimization and heavy engineering are wasted effort; the job is to experiment relentlessly and listen to what customers and non-customers are telling you.
- **Zeroing in on fit is a process.** Survey users on disappointment; ask those who love your product what they love; segment your market and see which segments are overrepresented among your most dedicated users; then focus on and convert more customers like them (Rahul Vohra's "product/market fit engine").
- **Go-to-market strategy is comprehensive and coordinated.** It promotes awareness, enables discovery, communicates the customer as the hero of their journey, coordinates with partners, produces marketing materials, sets pricing, and aligns personnel and budgets—not just a launch event but a coordinated orchestration.
- **Customer obsession means organized relationships with users and the teams that touch them.** Set up formal syncing with customer support, customer success, and community managers; build informal back-channel relationships; treat user data (surveys, reviews, feedback, behavior analytics) as organizational gold.
- **Launching and optimizing require different energies.** It is "a generally accepted notion among product leaders" that some PMs are better at launching new things and others at optimizing what is already running; Crumlish suggests getting a sense of where you fall, while insisting these are not mutually exclusive tendencies. Hà Phan, product leader and general manager at Pluralsight, says she long saw herself as the person who starts things and never optimizes, until building a platform made it a long game in which she does both.
- **B2B has different rules.** The end user often is not the payer; customers are specific people at specific companies, not the general public; user delight does not directly influence the ability or willingness to pay; and change management and training matter enormously because customers' employees must be able to work with your product.

At this stage, it makes no sense to optimize things or to invest in heavily engineered aspects of the solution because you really don't know yet how badly people want what you're offering.

## Concepts

- [[Product-Market Fit]] — Measured roughly by the 40% "very disappointed" threshold; the search for fit is the primary job before launch, and optimization is wasted effort until fit is found; Crumlish flags that the concept, though useful, is subjective and somewhat arbitrary (not a hard yes/no), and Rahul Vohra's "product/market fit engine" (surveying on disappointment, asking what early lovers cherish, segmenting by those attributes, then focusing) operationalizes the search.
- [[Business Strategy]] — Market sizing requires defining a target market tightly enough to be actionable while recognizing that a million customers out of a hundred million is "not significantly easier (or even necessarily easier at all)" to reach than a million out of four million; sustainability demands that both customer and business value be cultivated, neither raked off the top nor left on the table. Jay Zaveri, Chief Product Officer at CloudOn, validated market demand for "Word on the iPad" by running a cheap Google AdWords campaign with the message "Word is coming to the iPad! Sign up to be notified when it's here" (a smokescreen test), gathering tens of thousands of email addresses from eager users before the product existed—a go-to-market advantage that also informed the subsequent launch orchestration.
- [[User Research]] — Gauging market interest is user research by another name (identifying customers, pain points, needs, mental models); customer obsession means ravenous consumption of survey data, app reviews, social-media feedback, and qualitative insights from support, success, and community teams.
- [[Product Strategy]] — PMs differ in whether they thrive launching new products into uncertainty or optimizing existing ones; Crumlish insists the two are not mutually exclusive. Hà Phan, product leader and general manager at Pluralsight, reframes this: what looks like choosing to always start (launch) things is actually building a platform or system, which is inherently a long game where you are always starting new things and always optimizing old things simultaneously.
- [[Stakeholder Engagement]] — B2B product management reframes stakeholders: the end user and the payer are often different people, customers are known specific people at specific companies (not the general public), and the PM must coordinate with account managers, understand change management and training burden on the customer's side, and present change in terms both the end user and customer executives can support. Noreen Whysel, start-up founder and UX/product consultant, notes that UX skills transfer directly to stakeholder management: stakeholders are "users" too, and the same techniques for understanding user needs apply to understanding business stakeholder needs. Clement Kao, product manager at Blend (software for banks), describes his B2B day navigating account managers, managing user access through accounts rather than direct recruitment, coordinating feature rollouts with customer training and change management, and handling production fires while considering legal, compliance, and information security impacts—illustrating the complexity of coordinating across internal teams and customer organizations.
