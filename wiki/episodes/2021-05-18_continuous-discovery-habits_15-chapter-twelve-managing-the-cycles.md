# Continuous Discovery Habits: Chapter Twelve: Managing the Cycles

## Summary

Part 2 of *Continuous Discovery Habits*, this chapter addresses the reality that discovery is a messy, non-linear process where teams must loop back to earlier steps when they encounter surprises. Through four real-world case studies—Simply Business, CarMax, Farm Credit Services of America, and Snagajob—Torres demonstrates how teams manage the iterative cycles of discovery when assumptions prove wrong or new constraints emerge. Rather than following discovery sequentially and moving straight to delivery, effective teams recognize they will encounter forks in the road that require them to revisit opportunity prioritization, refocus on different customer segments, or break larger opportunities into smaller ones.

## Key Takeaways

- **Not all validated opportunities need solutions now** — Simply Business discovered that customers complained about late payments but didn't want third-party help solving the problem; killing this opportunity quickly after testing saved weeks of wasted development work.
- **Separate temporal opportunity selection** — CarMax's team learned to distinguish between "now, next, and future" opportunities, deferring cosmetic-condition solutions that would require greater investment until they had validated the foundational need with vehicle-specific data.
- **Reconcile conflicting customer needs with business needs** — Farm Credit Services succeeded by finding "What can I afford?" as a digital opportunity where customers were already willing to engage online, even though they valued human contact elsewhere in the loan process.
- **Break big problems into sub-opportunities** — Snagajob's team decomposed "I can't get in touch with candidates" into smaller problems (calling ineffective, need for text communication, scheduling friction, no-show management), solving each incrementally.
- **Weekly customer interviews enable quick pivots** — Simply Business's continuous interviewing habit meant that when they killed one opportunity on Tuesday, they could choose a new one on Wednesday and test it with already-scheduled Thursday interviews.
- **Small iteration can compound into big impact** — Snagajob's success came not from solving one "big, hairy problem" all at once but from chipping away at sub-opportunities until multiple small changes created meaningful business impact.

## Quotes

> We killed an opportunity on Tuesday, chose a new one on Wednesday, and used our already-scheduled interviews on Thursday to learn about the new opportunity.

> The fruit of discovery work is often the time we save when we decide not to build something.

> With time, their discovery work helped them successfully make the case to invest in vehicle-specific solutions.

## Concepts

- [[Opportunity Solution Tree]] — the chapter emphasizes how using opportunity solution trees helps teams navigate the iterative cycles by allowing them to assess and reassess the opportunity space when assumptions fail or new constraints emerge.
- [[Product Discovery]] — the messy reality of continuous discovery is that teams loop back through earlier habits (interviewing, opportunity assessment, brainstorming) multiple times as they learn.
- [[Assumption Testing]] — the quick tests that validate (or invalidate) which opportunities customers actually need solved, enabling teams to pivot rapidly without overinvesting.
- [[Product Trio]] — effective teams have discipline to recognize when they're going down a troublesome path; the trio structure facilitates this collaborative course-correction.
- [[Iterative Design]] — breaking large opportunities into sub-opportunities and addressing them incrementally allows teams to deliver value while laying groundwork for bigger solutions.
