# User Story Mapping: 2. Plan to Build Less

## Summary

Patton illustrates the power of story mapping to plan less work through the story of Globo.com, Brazil's largest media company, which faces unmovable deadlines (sports events, television premieres, elections). Rather than being faster than everyone else, Globo.com succeeds by being smart about doing less. Patton describes how eight teams from three divisions (Sports, News, Entertainment) used story mapping to visualize their interdependent work on rebuilding a massive content management system. The map revealed shared understanding of dependencies and identified holes in planning—finding work that teams thought others were handling but nobody was. When teams realized the full map would take over a year to build, Patton guided them to focus on specific outcomes rather than all features. By asking "What outcomes do we need for the upcoming Brazilian election?" they shifted from planning to build everything to slicing the map with blue painter's tape into staged releases, each with specific target outcomes. This release roadmap let them tackle all necessary work incrementally while seeing real benefit with each release. Patton emphasizes that prioritizing outcomes, not features, is the secret to breaking down large scope. He then defines minimum viable product (MVP) three ways: a bad definition (the crappiest product), and two good definitions—the smallest release that achieves desired outcomes, and (per Eric Ries) the smallest experiment to test assumptions; he notes Frank Robinson is credited with coining the term and that the definitions of Ries and Steve Blank now dominate, while everyone, himself included, still seems confused by it. Patton stresses that defining MVP requires guessing about customer behavior, feature viability, and what makes users happy; uncertainty is unavoidable. A sidebar contributed by Chris Shinkle of SEP, about FORUM Credit Union, illustrates story mapping applied to build shared understanding around priorities, using color-coded sticky notes to distinguish differentiator, spoiler, cost reducer, and table stakes features—revealing hundreds of thousands in savings before coding began.

## Key Takeaways

- **Story mapping reveals shared understanding across teams** — Multiple teams building interdependent systems can see dependencies and find holes in planning (work no one is taking) only by visualizing the complete map together.
- **Focus outcomes, not features, to slice releases** — When work is overwhelming, ask what outcomes matter for a specific moment or user, then slice the map to show which features are necessary for each outcome-focused release.
- **Scope doesn't creep; understanding grows** — What looks like scope creep is actually discovering holes in planning; story mapping uncovers what teams didn't understand together, which would have caused problems later.
- **MVP is inherently a guessing game** — The minimum viable product or solution is the smallest release that achieves desired outcomes, but defining "minimum" and "viable" requires guessing about customer behavior, feasibility, and what makes people happy; a third definition frames MVP as an experiment to test assumptions.

## Quotes
