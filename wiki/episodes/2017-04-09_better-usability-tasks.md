# Write Better Qualitative Usability Tasks: Top 10 Mistakes to Avoid

## Summary

The wording and construction of tasks in qualitative usability studies directly impacts study validity and findings. Poor task design can bias participants, change outcomes, produce misleading results, or harm participant well-being. This article identifies ten common mistakes to avoid when writing usability test tasks, ranging from priming participants with interface language to creating emotionally triggering scenarios. The key principle is to write tasks that reflect real-world user goals rather than internal system thinking, and to start with the user's end goal rather than the feature being tested.

## Key Takeaways

- **Never use interface language** — Remove exact words from your UI to avoid priming participants and testing reading comprehension rather than navigation and labeling effectiveness.
- **Don't tell users what to do** — Let participants discover steps like registration or downloads naturally; revealing them in advance misses valuable feedback about user surprise or annoyance.
- **Keep tasks timely** — Ensure dates and events referenced in tasks are current during testing; outdated content makes sites or tasks seem stale and unrealistic.
- **Make tasks require effort** — Tasks should make users work and process information, not just locate it; test real cognitive activities, not simple navigation.
- **Avoid unnecessary scenarios** — Short context can clarify, but elaborate backstories add complexity and may signal an unrealistic task if too much explanation is needed to justify it.
- **Use user-centric language** — Eliminate marketing phrases, business jargon, corporate acronyms, and internal terminology; use language users would actually employ.
- **Avoid emotional and offensive content** — Don't reference specific relationships, joke with participants, use famous names, or touch sensitive topics like politics, health, religion, or money.
- **Phrase as imperatives, not questions** — Use "Find X" not "How would you find X"; you want to observe what users do, not what they say they would theoretically do.

## Quotes

> How the task is written directly impacts the success of the study. If you give study participants bad instructions, you can bias them and completely change the outcome of the study.

> A task needs to accurately and adequately reflect the researcher's goal, as well as provide clear instructions about what participants need to do.

> Consider the user's end goal rather than the task's end goal. Rather than focusing on the section or the feature you want to test, consider why people would use that section or feature.
