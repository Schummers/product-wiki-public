# Don't "Validate" Designs; Test Them

## Summary

The word "validate" in user research creates subtle but powerful priming effects that bias both study participants and research teams against discovering problems. When researchers frame testing as validation, participants interpret the design as finished and may hesitate to report issues. Teams assume the design works and rationalize away problems as minor. User research should instead focus on learning what works and what doesn't, with teams expecting to find both positive findings and substantial problems. Good user research uncovers issues that drive design improvements, not proof that a design is correct.

## Key Takeaways

- **"Validate" primes participants to withhold criticism** — the word suggests a design is finished and being checked for correctness, making participants less likely to report problems.
- **The word primes teams to dismiss findings** — researchers framing work as validation unconsciously look to prove the design works, reasoning away negative findings as minor issues.
- **User studies should find both positives and problems** — discovering nothing wrong indicates a methodology problem, not a perfect design.
- **Denying issues is not the same as deferring them** — teams can acknowledge usability problems, rate severity, and triage what to fix now versus later.
- **Alternative language reframes research constructively** — use words like "test," "examine," "study," or "see where the design is successful and unsuccessful" to encourage genuine learning.

## Quotes

> User research is as much a mindset as it is a science and an art. In a user study, attitude greatly affects participants, your team's reactions to their behaviors, and follow-up actions on study findings.

> If a usability study found nothing to improve in a design then that only proved one thing: that the test was done wrong.

> A sentiment better than "Let's validate this design," is "Let's learn what works and what doesn't work well for users and why."
