# Beyond the NPS: Measuring Perceived Usability with the SUS, NASA-TLX, and the Single Ease Question After Tasks and Usability Tests

## Summary

This article provides guidance on three established questionnaires for measuring perceived usability: the System Usability Scale (SUS) for post-test overall impressions, the Single Ease Question (SEQ) for post-task satisfaction, and the NASA-TLX for measuring workload on complex tasks. The article explains when to use each instrument, noting that these subjective metrics should be combined with objective performance data to provide a complete picture. Standard questionnaires are preferable to custom ones because they have demonstrated validity, reliability, and sensitivity.

## Key Takeaways

- **Post-test vs. post-task questionnaires** — post-test instruments capture overall impressions after an entire session, while post-task questionnaires collect immediate feedback after each task and are less subject to the peak-end effect.
- **System Usability Scale (SUS)** — a 10-question post-test instrument with a 0-100 score; an average score across 500 studies is 68, and a score of 80 or higher places a site in the top 10% of usability.
- **Single Ease Question (SEQ)** — a simple 7-point post-task questionnaire that efficiently measures perceived task difficulty and allows comparison of which interface sections are most problematic.
- **NASA-TLX** — a complex 6-question post-task instrument best suited for measuring workload in high-consequence environments like healthcare and aerospace, not typical consumer UX.
- **Sample size requirements** — quantitative metrics require larger sample sizes (typically 20-30 users) to reach statistical significance, unlike qualitative testing which uses 5 users.

## Quotes

> The SUS has been around since the command-line interface days of the 1980s, and has been repeatedly demonstrated experimentally to be valid and reliable.

> For most practical UX research, we recommend simple satisfaction questionnaires, with as few questions as possible.

> Like all quantitative metrics, low sample sizes (like the 5 users we typically recommend for each round of qualitative usability testing) are unlikely to provide statistically significant or meaningful results.
