# Secondary Research in UX

## Summary

Secondary research, also known as desk research or literature review in academic contexts, involves gathering prior research findings and relevant information for new projects. It provides background, context, and scaffolding for initiatives, allowing practitioners to stand on the shoulders of giants rather than reinvent solutions. Secondary research includes both internal resources (past primary research projects, customer-journey maps, service blueprints, workshop deliverables) and external resources (academic journals, public libraries, datasets, white papers). Nielsen Norman Group provides a step-by-step guide for conducting secondary research: choose a research topic and write a problem statement, identify internal and external resources, collect and analyze data critically, and refine the problem statement to determine what still requires investigation.

## Key Takeaways

- **Secondary research should precede primary research** — A standard first step in rigorous research practice, secondary research is also cost-effective even in casual settings; it uncovers what others have already studied and identifies gaps requiring new investigation.
- **Evaluate source validity and limitations carefully** — Peer-reviewed publications (academic journals, conferences) are most reliable with documented methods, data-collection techniques, and limitations; blog posts may be opinion pieces not backed by primary research; check for confidence intervals, statistical significance, and validity.
- **Different audiences and contexts affect generalizability** — Findings valid for one demographic may not apply to others; design patterns tested with young adults might not work for older users; secondary research reveals where findings need validation in your context.
- **Internal and external research are equally important** — Internal resources (past projects, existing data, customer-service logs, search logs) often reveal patterns and previous work; both require critical evaluation for inherent biases, omissions, and limitations.
- **Secondary research guides primary-research planning** — After collecting and analyzing secondary sources, synthesize findings and refine the problem statement to identify what information still needs investigation through primary methods.
- **Secondary research is not a substitute for primary research** — Best practice is doing both; though secondary research is cost-effective and quick, its quality depends on source quality, so sources must be evaluated before use.

## Quotes

> "You don't have to do all the user-research work yourself. If somebody else already ran a study (and published it), grab it!"

> "Secondary research allows us to stand on the shoulders of giants and not to reinvent the wheel every time we initiate a new program or plan a study."

> "It is important to note that secondary research is not a substitute for primary research. It is always better to do both. Although secondary research is often cost-effective and quick, its quality depends to a large extent on the quality of your sources."

## Concepts

- [[Secondary Research]] — Gathering prior research findings and information from internal and external sources to provide background, context, and scaffolding for new projects without reinventing previous work.
- [[Research Methods]] — Secondary research involves identifying reliable sources (peer-reviewed publications, public datasets, academic databases) and critically evaluating them for validity, methodology, and generalizability to your context.
- [[Literature Review]] — Synthesizing findings from secondary sources reveals industry trends, identifies gaps in knowledge, and guides primary research planning by determining what still needs investigation.
- [[Knowledge Synthesis]] — Critically evaluating and comparing previous findings from both internal and external sources helps identify patterns, inconsistencies, and areas where research needs to be conducted within your specific organizational context.
