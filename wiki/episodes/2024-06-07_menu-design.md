# Menu-Design Checklist: 17 UX Guidelines

## Summary

This practical checklist covers 17 evidence-based guidelines for designing usable navigation menus across desktop and mobile platforms. The guidelines address visibility (making menus visible on large screens, placing them in expected locations), orientation (indicating current location), communication (clear labels, visual cues), and manipulation (adequate touch targets, click-based interactions). Following these principles helps users navigate effectively and understand site scope while avoiding common pitfalls like hidden navigation, unclear current location, and overly innovative but confusing menu patterns.

## Key Takeaways

- **Make navigation visible on desktop** — hamburger menus should not hide navigation on large screens; visible menus help users understand site scope and available options.
- **Place menus in expected locations** — primary navigation belongs in headers (desktop) or left side (applications); utility navigation appears at top; local navigation on left; footer navigation at bottom.
- **Indicate current location clearly** — users need visual feedback showing where they are in the information architecture; breadcrumbs and highlighted current pages serve this purpose.
- **Use clear, familiar link labels** — avoid jargon, internal terminology, or overly creative labels; users should immediately understand what each link leads to.
- **Make links big enough to click** — links should be at least 1cm x 1cm for adequate touch targets; small or closely-spaced links frustrate users and increase error rates.
- **Avoid multi-level cascading menus** — dropdown menus with multiple levels are difficult to manipulate; use mega menus or landing pages instead.
- **Avoid gimmicky navigation patterns** — novel menu designs may impress stakeholders but confuse users; familiar patterns are almost always better.

## Quotes

> "Too often, we observe users struggling with menus that are confusing, difficult to manipulate, or hard to find."

> "Out of sight means out of mind. The hamburger menu is a necessary evil on smaller screens, where we must balance content and navigation with limited screen real estate."

> "While clients or stakeholders may be temporarily impressed by novel menu designs, users tend to be far more impressed by great content that is attractively presented and easily accessed with familiar menus."
