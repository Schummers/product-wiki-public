# The Power Law of Learning: Consistency vs. Innovation in User Interfaces

## Summary

The power law of learning states that task performance improves with repetition following a predictable mathematical curve, and this pattern holds across countless human-factors experiments spanning centuries. When users encounter a consistent interface pattern across multiple websites or applications, they benefit from accumulated practice, reaching saturation faster than they would with a novel design. This explains why consistency is so valued in UX design despite appearing boring to designers.

Innovation in interface design carries real costs: new patterns require more repetitions to learn, and users must decide whether to invest effort in mastering the novel design before accessing its benefits. The decision to deviate from standards should only be made if the new design performs substantially better once learned, users will encounter it frequently enough to practice, or the brand's perceived value justifies the learning investment.

## Key Takeaways

- **Power law describes learning progress** — Time to perform a task decreases with repetitions following a power law; steep learning curves (fast improvement) are good, while shallow curves indicate poor learnability.
- **Saturation points vary by design** — Different interfaces reach plateaus at different repetition counts; design A may be learnable quickly but design B might offer superior performance once learned, requiring more practice to reach its plateau.
- **Consistency leverages prior practice** — By using standard patterns, designers give users one more repetition of a highly common element they've already practiced across dozens of other websites, accelerating learning.
- **Innovation incurs learning costs** — Novel patterns force users to start at the steep part of a new learning curve while the familiar alternative is already at saturation, requiring significant practice before the novel design proves superior.
- **Three conditions for successful innovation** — The new design must perform substantially better once learned, users must encounter it frequently enough to reach saturation, or the brand's perceived value must justify the cost.
- **Frequency determines innovation feasibility** — Innovation works better when users interact daily (building practice quickly) or when major companies impose updates (captive audience); occasional users may abandon new designs before benefiting.

## Quotes

> The power law of learning says that (1) the time it takes to perform a task decreases with the number of repetitions of that task; and (2) the decrease follows the shape of a power law.

> By being consistent with other sites, you're giving users one more repetition of a highly common UI element, and you're also reaping the benefits of practice on all these other websites.

> The path to innovation is circuitous and costly, and if your users won't have many learning opportunities, they may never reach that optimal-performance plateau accessible only after learning has happened.

## Concepts

- [[Learnability]] — Task performance improvements follow power laws that are consistent across human behavior; understanding learning curves helps predict interface learnability.
- [[Design Consistency]] — Consistency is not merely aesthetic preference but a consequence of how human memory and learning work; repeated practice with familiar patterns accelerates learning.
- [[Innovation]] — Innovation in interface design is possible but requires careful analysis of whether the new pattern will perform substantially better and whether users will practice it enough to reach its saturation point.
- [[Mental Model]] — Users develop mental models based on patterns they practice across multiple websites; familiar patterns activate more readily in memory and users expect interfaces to follow patterns they've previously learned.
