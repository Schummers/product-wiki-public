# Laws of UX: 4. Hick's Law

## Summary

Hick's Law establishes that decision time increases logarithmically with the number of choices available. Formulated in 1952 by psychologists William Edmund Hick and Ray Hyman, the law demonstrates that complex interfaces with too many options result in longer decision times and higher cognitive load. The chapter connects this principle to the paradox of choice and provides practical design strategies: minimize choices at critical moments, break complex tasks into steps, highlight recommended options, and avoid oversimplifying to the point of abstraction or loss of clarity.

## Key Takeaways

- **Logarithmic Decision Time** — The formula RT = a + b log₂(n) calculates response time based on the number of stimuli (n) and task-dependent constants. More options increase decision time logarithmically, not linearly.

- **Paradox of Choice** — Sheena Iyengar and Mark Lepper's jam study (2000) found shoppers were 10 times more likely to purchase when shown 6 jam varieties versus 24. More options lead to choice overload despite the assumption that more choice improves satisfaction.

- **Progressive Disclosure** — Reveal choices at the right time, not all at once. Google Search keeps the initial interface focused on the search box and shows filter options (images, videos, news) only after results appear.

- **Task Decomposition** — Break complex processes into smaller steps. Notion's onboarding uses a progressive checklist to teach new users without overwhelming them with all available features simultaneously.

- **Social Proof and Recommendation** — Netflix addressed 18-minute decision paralysis by highlighting "Trending Now" and "Popular on Netflix," providing weight to specific options and reducing the cognitive burden of choice.

- **Oversimplification Risks** — While removing unnecessary complexity improves usability, simplifying too far creates abstraction. Icon-only navigation confuses users; text labels with icons provide clarity and aid recognition.

## Quotes

> The time it takes to make a decision increases with the number and complexity of choices available.

> More choice leads to choice overload.

> It's important not to take simplification too far. When an interface has been simplified to the point of abstraction, it's no longer clear what actions are available, what the next steps are, or where to find specific information.

## Concepts

- [[Hick's Law]] — principle that decision time increases logarithmically with the number of choices; fundamental to interface complexity and user decision-making; the psychological phenomenon where abundance of options reduces satisfaction and increases decision paralysis rather than improving outcomes.
- [[Cognitive Load]] — the mental effort required to process choices and make decisions; higher choice counts increase cognitive load.
- [[Decision Making]] — the cognitive process users undertake when selecting from available options; more options increase time and mental strain.
- [[Progressive Disclosure]] — revealing choices, information, and features gradually over time rather than presenting all options simultaneously.
- [[Card Sorting]] — user research method where participants organize topics into groups reflecting their mental models; identifies information architecture expectations.
