# Clean the Sludge from Decision-Making Workflows

## Summary

This article introduces the concept of "sludge" in decision-making workflows, drawing on behavioral economist Richard Thaler's insight that designers should focus less on nudging users toward desired choices and more on removing barriers that make good decisions difficult. The article explains how sludge manifests across industries from SaaS to healthcare, provides frameworks for understanding decision complexity, and recommends UX strategies like calculators, comparison tables, and recommendations to help users make satisfying choices.

## Key Takeaways

- **Reduce sludge instead of creating nudges** — Thaler suggests designers shift focus from nudging users toward "correct" choices to eliminating the barriers that make otherwise good decisions difficult.
- **Simple workflows yield business benefits** — reducing sludge in decision-making leads to greater user satisfaction, increased retention, lower customer support needs, and fewer refunds.
- **Decision complexity depends on context** — understanding users' prior experience, the decision's impact, how many options they compare, and how easily they differentiate options all predict how difficult a decision will be.
- **Different product types present different decisions** — SaaS tools require subscription plan choices, ecommerce requires product selection, education requires course selection, and each domain has specific decision-making patterns.
- **Necessary and unnecessary decisions differ** — university course selection is a necessary decision where good choice architecture directly benefits users, while clothing browsing is optional where abandonment is a failure.

## Quotes

> Poorly designed decision-making workflows feel a lot like sludge-filled pipes: clogged up, confusing, and overwhelming.

> It might be more productive to shift from creating nudges to reducing sludge, that is, eliminating the barriers that make otherwise good decisions difficult.

## Concepts

- [[Behavioral Economics]] — Frameworks and tools drawn from research about how people actually make decisions simplify how users evaluate and select among options, improving design outcomes.
- [[Decision Design]] — addresses how to structure information and interfaces to match the complexity of specific decisions.
