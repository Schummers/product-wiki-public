# User Story Mapping: Preface

## Summary

Patton explains that he set out to write a brief guide to story mapping but discovered that clarifying widespread misunderstandings about user stories required a much larger book. Since Kent Beck introduced stories a decade and a half before this publication, they have become ubiquitous in Agile adoption—and equally misunderstood and misused. Patton's goal is to correct misconceptions about stories and how they are used in Agile and Lean development, thereby restoring the value of story mapping. He positions the book for product managers, UX practitioners, POs, business analysts, IT project managers, Agile coaches, and everyone involved in Agile processes. The preface identifies five common traps created by story misconceptions: losing the big picture (resulting in "Franken-products"), unclear delivery timelines, replacing conversation documentation with undocumented discussion, mistaking acceptance criteria for shared understanding, and dismissing stories in non-consumer products. Patton frames his motivation as helping people whose work lives have become worse due to these misconceptions, having spent twenty years in software development learning what he now wants to teach.

## Key Takeaways

- **Stories are more misunderstood than ever** — Patton identifies stories as increasingly popular but misunderstood and misused in Agile adoption over the decade and a half since Kent Beck introduced them.
- **Five common traps exist** — Patton identifies these misconceptions: losing the big picture and assembling "Franken-products," unclear delivery timelines, avoiding documentation and then forgetting conversations, confusing acceptance criteria with shared understanding (teams don't finish planned work), and dismissing stories for non-consumer products.
- **The book serves multiple roles** — Product managers and UX practitioners need story maps to bridge vision and tactical details; POs and BAs need them to align stakeholders; process coaches need them for team improvement; everyone needs to understand that stories are about better conversations, not requirements writing.

## Quotes

> Building a map is dead simple. Working together with others, I'll tell the story of a product, writing each big step the users take in the story on sticky notes in a left-to-right flow. Then, we'll go back and talk about the details of each step, and write those details down on sticky notes and place them vertically under each step.

> In the decade and a half since stories were first described by Kent Beck, they're more popular—and more misunderstood and misused—than ever before.

> You and everyone else will learn that stories aren't a way to write better requirements, but a way to organize and have better conversations.

## Concepts

- [[User Story]] — Patton introduces stories as a common but widely misunderstood practice in Agile, and positions the book as correcting misconceptions about their role and use.
- [[Story Mapping]] — Patton describes story mapping as a simple-to-execute practice that organizes stories into a left-to-right narrative with top-to-bottom details, making them more useful for Agile backlogs.
- [[Product Management]] — Patton identifies product managers, UX practitioners, and other strategic roles as needing story maps to bridge from high-level vision to executable detail and shared team understanding.
