# Storytelling in Design: Chapter 4. The Emotional Aspect of Product Design

## Summary

Emotion, not reason, drives decision-making and product loyalty. This chapter explores how storytelling techniques can evoke specific emotions in product design, drawing on Don Norman's work on emotional design and Aaron Walter's pyramid of user needs. Dahlström shows that product design must address emotional needs (visceral, behavioral, reflective) just as stories do, and groups, as her own avowedly generalized categorization, four moment types (unhappy, happy, neutral, reflective) where emotion plays a key part.

## Key Takeaways

- **Emotion precedes cognition and is necessary for decision-making** — Dahlström cites neuroscientist Antonio Damasio: emotion is a necessary ingredient in almost all decisions. When we choose a brand, we rely on emotional response and personal feelings, not facts or features. Without emotion, decision-making ability is impaired—we cannot choose between equally valid options. This is why a brand is "nothing more than a mental representation of a product in the consumer's mind," not its specs.

- **Affective and cognitive systems operate in parallel and influence each other** — Norman's plank example illustrates this: standing on a plank at ground level feels safe (cognitive and affective agree), but 100 meters up, fear (affective) dominates despite cognitive reasoning that it is the same plank. Affect usually drives cognition. This has implications for product design: stressed users lose flexibility in how they approach problem-solving, while people in a relaxed situation are more tolerant of difficulties and problems.

- **Positive affect makes people more forgiving and creative; negative affect narrows cognition** — When relaxed and in positive affect, people tolerate design problems better and think breadth-first (creative). Stress and negative affect enhance depth-first processing, minimize distractions, and impair problem-solving flexibility. Dahlström's conclusion is not that beauty carries stressful contexts: good human-centered design practice becomes more essential where the task may be stressful, and she notes later in the chapter that aesthetically pleasing design alone won't be enough, since Norman and Walter both require the design to be usable as well.

- **Aaron Walter's pyramid of user needs builds on Maslow: pleasure comes last, after functional, reliable, and usable layers** — Dahlström explains Walter's framework: products must first solve a problem (functional), work reliably, be easy to learn and use. Only then can the pleasure layer deliver "delights." Surface delights (animations, micro copy, icons) can be experienced even in an otherwise poor experience; deep delights (seamless flow, no friction, magical cohesion) happen when all lower needs are met and users reach a state of flow—rare and powerful.

- **Norman's three emotional levels apply to all products: visceral (initial gut reaction), behavioral (usability and function), reflective (impact on life and values)** — Dahlström shows how each level requires different design focus. Visceral is aesthetics and perceived quality. Behavioral is usability and learnability. Reflective is the lasting emotional impression and desire. Together they shape how we connect with a product; Dahlström's rule is to address the user's cognitive ability on each level and put it into the context of the experience.

- **Emotions are shared triggers for virality and word-of-mouth** — Dahlström cites Andrea Lehr, a Promotions Supervisor at Frac.tl, who referenced a study of one hundred viral Reddit images. Lehr found virality to be a combination of emotional connection paired with the dimensions of arousal and dominance; the ten top emotional responses to viral images, in order, are happiness, surprise, admiration, satisfaction, hope, love, "happiness for," concentration, pride and gratitude. She illustrates with "the dress" of 2015 (its seller saw organic traffic rise 420% and sales 560%). Negative emotions like hate, reproach and resentment were far less common in viral content, but can still play a role when they hit the right combination of arousal and dominance.

- **Dahlström's own four categories of moments where emotion plays a key part: unhappy, happy, neutral, reflective** — She states this is her grouping and a generalization: depending on the product, a moment she calls neutral may deserve more focus as a happy one, or the reverse (an Amazon purchase confirmation page does little to evoke happiness and works as utility, where Typeform deliberately adds delight). Unhappy moments (barriers, errors, confusing UX) evoke anger, fear, sadness. Happy moments (first impressions, task completion, delights) evoke joy, surprise, love. Neutral moments (routine tasks, low cognitive load) allow users to move without friction and provide contrast. Reflective moments (permissions, payment, cancellation), as Per Axbom argues, should intentionally add friction in design to ensure users confirm their decisions. Designers must sequence these, not attempt constant happiness.

- **Five storytelling techniques evoke specific emotions: spark imagination, add personality, focus on outcomes not features, make messaging about the user, raise "What if"** — Dahlström shows how to apply narrative craft to design. Spark imagination by inviting users to see themselves in the scenario (as scary movies do by not showing the threat). Add personality through copy, iconography and animation: Walter argues that "personality is the platform for emotion," and an emotional response makes the experience feel like there's a human being on the other side rather than a machine; positive emotional stimuli build trust over time and, as Norman pointed out, make users more forgiving when things go wrong. Focus on benefits (outcomes the user achieves) rather than product specs. Make messaging user-centric, not brand-centric (Dove's "Real Beauty," Team America's "Gold in the US"; Squarespace, in Melanie Deziel's reading, sells not the sharing of business information but empowering people to build something themselves, which can help dreams come true). Raise aspirational "What if" scenarios (Apple's pre-charged phone was a simple "what if" that reset user expectations).

## Concepts

- [[Emotional Design]] — Dahlström draws on Don Norman's *Emotional Design* (2004) to argue that products must evoke emotion to create connection and loyalty. She covers Norman's three emotional levels (visceral, behavioral, reflective) and shows how positive emotions build trust and forgiveness while negative emotions narrow cognition and reduce problem-solving flexibility. Emotional design is not a luxury detail, but in stressful contexts Dahlström stresses that pleasing aesthetics alone won't be enough: Norman and Walter both require the design to be usable as well.

- [[Experience Shape]] — The chapter frames the emotional arc of a product experience as a story with shape: unhappy moments at barriers, happy moments at completions, neutral moments that allow flow, and reflective moments that pause for confirmation. The shape itself is explicitly deferred: Dahlström points to Chapter 5 for narrative structures and plot points and to Chapter 9 for visualizing the shape of an experience. The mapping she recommends here is running the "What if…?" exercise as a full end-to-end review over a customer experience or journey map.

- [[Design Presentation]] — Dahlström covers how to present products and ideas so they spark imagination and emotional resonance. She cites Hoa Loranger's Nielsen Norman work on focusing presentations on outcomes and benefits rather than features; Melanie Deziel on shifting from product-centric to user-centric messaging (credibility drops from 74% to 29% when product promotion enters). Effective presentation makes the audience imagine themselves in the scenario and feel the emotional benefit.

- [[Empathy]] — Dahlström emphasizes empathy as essential to design: understanding what users are feeling now, what barriers hold them back, and what "What if" scenarios would elevate them. She cites Alessandro Suraci, a visual designer at Google, on how hard commitment is and how the excuse for not acting is usually nearer at hand than the act itself; from there she argues in her own voice that UX designers need to know the typical situation narrative and what users are feeling in order to move them into action. Empathy for the user's emotional journey (not just their tasks) is what allows designers to craft moments that resonate and build long-term loyalty.
