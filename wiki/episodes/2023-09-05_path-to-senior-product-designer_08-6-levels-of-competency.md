# The Path to Senior Product Designer: 6. Levels of Competency

## Summary

Chapter 6 shifts from *what* companies evaluate designers on to *how much* of each competency is required at each seniority level. The chapter provides a generic competency progression scale—Aware, Novice, Intermediate, Advanced, Expert—that designers can apply to any skill, not just those detailed in the book. It then walks the competencies the book tracks (craft, communication, collaboration, ownership, strategy, mentorship, leadership, community) across IC career levels: junior, mid-level, senior, staff, and principal. The author cautions that these definitions vary by company size and should guide self-evaluation rather than be treated as strict checklists. The chapter concludes with a standalone summary of senior designer competencies for designers seeking a quick reference at that level.

The emphasis is on revealing which competencies designers are falling behind on relative to their current level, so they can prioritize them in a growth plan. The author also notes that the competency progression applies to IC tracks only; managers follow a different path and are mostly omitted from the detailed analysis.

## Key Takeaways

- **The generic progression scale is Aware, Novice, Intermediate, Advanced, Expert** — these levels allow designers to self-evaluate any skill, not just those in the book; Aware means the individual understands the skill and learns with guidance, while Expert means they guide others and are recognized as an authority.
- **Competency expectations differ significantly across IC levels** — junior designers demonstrate foundational and theoretical knowledge with guidance; mid-level designers apply competencies independently and contribute to standards; senior designers mentor others and shape organizational expectations; staff and principal levels add strategic scope and cross-organizational influence.
- **Craft remains specific to product designers** — while the progression scale and all other competencies apply to any designer role, craft (visual design, UX design, tools and process) is particular to product design.
- **The chapter avoids strict leveling** — definitions vary by company size, and the competency descriptions are meant to provide direction and reveal gaps, not to be tick-boxes for promotion.
- **Senior designers mentor, contribute to standards, and show leadership within their scope** — the senior level marks a shift from individual execution to peer influence, knowledge transfer, and shaping team and organizational practices.

## Quotes

> The list of competency levels exists to provide you with direction, but it shouldn't be used as a strict set of parameters where you have to tick all the boxes in order to move to the next level.

> In the specifications below, try to ignore the specific titles and just consider them as levels. Our goal will not be to identify which title matches your competency level but rather which competencies you're falling behind on in relative terms, so you can add them to your growth plan.

> Since senior designer is a part of the IC track, from now on, to make it less overwhelming, I'll omit the manager role from my analysis and only talk about IC roles.
