# Design Thinking: The Learner's Journey

## Summary

Based on research with 1,067 practitioners, this article describes a four-stage learning journey for design-thinking practitioners: newcomer, adopter, leader, and grandmaster. Mastery is defined as the combination of competence (how good one is) and confidence (how good one thinks one is); both are required to effectively use design thinking. Practitioners progress nonlinearly through stages, experiencing fluctuations in confidence. The framework helps learners understand their position in their journey and helps educators design appropriate learning experiences tailored to each stage.

## Key Takeaways

- **Four stages characterize the design-thinking journey** — newcomers have surface-level understanding, adopters practice by the book, leaders think adaptively and teach others, and grandmasters view design thinking as flexible scaffolding while creating new applications.
- **Mastery requires both competence and confidence** — practitioners must align their actual skill level with their perception of their skill; high competence with low confidence limits growth, as does the reverse.
- **Progress is nonlinear and turbulent** — mastery fluctuates especially during the adopter phase when practitioners first experiment with design thinking and frequently encounter failure.
- **Adopters struggle most with incomplete understanding** — failure occurs when adopters jump to conclusions, use the wrong activity at the wrong time, or lack organizational buy-in; most learning happens through failure in this phase.
- **Leaders begin thinking adaptively** — they facilitate design-thinking activities with others, initiate workshops, involve users and stakeholders, and practice with general ease and confidence.
- **Educators should target current stage, not future stages** — overwhelm occurs when learners jump ahead; appropriate learning experiences without excessive complexity are key to sustainable progress.
