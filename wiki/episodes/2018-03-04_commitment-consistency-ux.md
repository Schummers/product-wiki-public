# The Principle of Commitment and Behavioral Consistency

## Summary

Behavioral consistency is a powerful heuristic where people feel compelled to behave in ways that align with their past decisions and commitments. This principle operates at both individual and social levels, with social pressure being especially strong. In UX, designers can leverage this principle by getting users to make small, low-stakes initial commitments, then gradually increasing commitment as trust builds. However, care must be taken to match commitment levels with user trust and perceived value, avoiding high-stakes requests that backfire.

## Key Takeaways

- **Consistency motivation** — people experience personal pressure to remain consistent with their stated commitments and social pressure to avoid appearing inconsistent, making them more likely to follow through on what they've committed to.
- **Small initial commitment** — the most effective commitments are low-stakes and easy to make initially, creating momentum toward larger commitments; users who write a review without logging in are more likely to create an account than if asked upfront.
- **Public vs. private pressure** — promises made publicly and involving others create stronger consistency pressure than private commitments; even small promises made in front of friends increase follow-through.
- **Trust and interaction cost** — organizations must carefully match commitment requests to user trust level; early in relationships, even small requests like entering an email feel high-stakes, but strong brands can ask for more.
- **Interaction cost minimization** — even low-stakes commitments will be rejected if they require significant work; designing frictionless commitment paths is essential for conversion.

## Quotes

> Once we have made a choice or taken a stand, we will encounter personal and interpersonal pressures to behave consistently with that commitment.

> Inconsistency is seen as an undesirable trait and is associated with irrationality, deceit, and even incompetence; it can generate reactions of disappointment, anger, and confusion.

> An all-or-nothing design will deliver nothing from most users.
