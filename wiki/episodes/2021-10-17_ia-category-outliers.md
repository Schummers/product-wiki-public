# How to Handle Category Outliers in Your IA

## Summary

Information architects often face decisions about how to handle items that don't fit cleanly into established categories. Card-sort research reveals outliers — items that participants weakly associate with categories or that cluster unexpectedly. Rather than forcing outliers into strict logical categories, effective IA design draws on categorization theory, which shows that human categories are based on family resemblance rather than fixed shared characteristics. The article presents three practical approaches for handling outliers: creating subcategories, leaving outliers within larger categories with supporting search or metadata, or using polyhierarchy to list items in multiple places.

## Key Takeaways

- **Human categories aren't strictly logical** — People form categories based on family resemblance, where members share overlapping characteristics but not all members share the same traits. Categories can shift boundaries when new items arrive.
- **Noncentral category members still belong** — Items don't need to be perfect exemplars of their category to belong there. The popemobile is an unusual car but still recognizably a car, just as outliers can belong to categories even if they're atypical.
- **Approach 1: Create dedicated subcategories** — Isolating outliers into specific subcategories works when they have low family resemblance to the main group, but risks creating deep, confusing information structures with sparsely populated categories.
- **Approach 2: Keep outliers in main categories** — Leave weakly associated items in their broader category and support findability through robust search, facets, and metadata. This respects users' mental models and maintains discoverability.
- **Approach 3: Use polyhierarchy strategically** — List outliers in both their own subcategory and the main category, but be cautious about excessive overlap that burdens users trying to understand the distinction.

## Quotes

> When figuring out what to do about category outliers, it's worth backing up and understanding what happens when people group things together. First, allow me to disabuse you of the common assumption that people develop firm, logical boundaries between categories and that those firm boundaries are defined by common properties of all the items that got grouped together. This implicit assumption is logical;however, it is not true.

> Noncentral category members are still relatively easily recognizable as members of their categories, but not as easily recognizable as other central members of the same category. That's the key distinction — noncentral category members can still belong to the category, even if they aren't the best examples of it.

> Sometimes, it is a question of whether to create a subcategory for just one or two items that are a little bit different than the other items.
