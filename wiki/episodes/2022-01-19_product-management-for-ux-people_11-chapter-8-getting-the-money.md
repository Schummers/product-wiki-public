# Product Management for UX People: CHAPTER 8: Getting the Money

## Summary

This chapter addresses the financial dimension of product management, which many UX designers find uncomfortable but is essential to understand. Money is the lifeblood of product organizations across all sectors—commercial, nonprofit, and government. The chapter covers profit and loss (P&L) responsibilities, common revenue models, the journey to break-even, revenue lifecycles from establishing through optimizing to sunsetting, and the reality that financial considerations must be integrated into the product manager's mental model. Throughout, Crumlish emphasizes that while most product managers do not directly control P&L, they must be acutely aware of the financial constraints and opportunities that shape what is possible for their product.

## Key Takeaways

- **Revenue models are diverse and testable.** Common approaches include free trials with paid conversion, freemium paywalls, in-app purchases, enterprise licenses by seat or usage, and subscription-based models. The right model depends on customer needs and constraints.

- **Crumlish's experience at 7 Cups involved five distinct revenue experiments:** (1) donations through a "compassion jar" UI, which generated minimal "pin money"; (2) upgrades to a premium "Growth Path" subscription (monthly, yearly, lifetime options), which generated substantial revenue but could not achieve profitability alone; (3) white-label SaaS for universities, which introduced larger transactions but demanded resources and created tension with the consumer roadmap; (4) a therapist directory competing with Psychology Today, which failed due to lack of competitive leverage; and (5) paid professional therapy as a freemium tier, which took the company to break-even.

- **Break-even is a pivot point, not an ending.** When 7 Cups reached break-even through paid therapy, Crumlish describes it as "ramen profitable"—keeping expenses minimal, doing everything "scrappy, not crappy," and paying themselves below market rates. Technically profitable meant bringing in one penny more than they spent, so the runway was "theoretically infinite"; Crumlish notes that paying below market rates "is itself not real sustainability in the long run." The break-even milestone created three possible directions: organic growth with small bets (where we're going, there are no roads), optimization of the core business model to healthy sustainability (the band is just fantastic), or pursuit of breakthrough 100x opportunities (go for broke).

- **Multiple revenue streams and business lines are common.** The path to profitability for 7 Cups involved layering experiments—B2C donations, B2C upgrades, B2B SaaS, professional services—each operating on different pay cycles (monthly and annual), compounding to eventual success.

- **Revenue lifecycles shape product strategy.** Products move through establishing (R&D with costs, no revenue), growth phases, optimization and plateauing of established revenue, and eventual sunsetting when they no longer break even and have no viable future. Each stage demands different proficiencies: scaling revenue at launch, fine-tuning a well-oiled machine once established, and managing legal and financial obligations during wind-down.

- **The "innovator's dilemma" constrains incumbent products.** Crumlish attributes the term to Clay Christensen and frames it as a long-term risk of complacency: established revenue streams create risk aversion; incumbents cannot afford to cannibalize existing revenue, opening opportunities for disruptive upstarts. Sunsetting a product requires parallel skills to building it: identifying decay patterns, winding down commitments, preserving legal and security obligations, and removing the product entirely from the business.

- **B. Pagels-Minor disagrees with the chapter twice, and Crumlish keeps both objections.** On the premise that UX designers are uncomfortable with money, Pagels-Minor pushes back: "I generally disagree with this. Many UX designers I work with are just as savvy and thoughtful about how to monetize as many product managers," and Crumlish concedes it is probably true and that he may be betraying his own narrow experience. On break-even as the goal, the "From the Trenches" sidebar carries Pagels-Minor, then a Netflix product manager, calling break-even "pretty illogical in modern tech companies" given that Amazon and Netflix were deliberately unprofitable for years, and arguing the goal is more likely market penetration. Crumlish accepts the point and asks the reader to treat his story as one particular path, not the success goal.

- **A fintech PM's own day closes the chapter** — The "A Day in the Life of a Fintech PM" box is Michael Curry's, head of product at 100x Group, an international crypto (financial tech) start-up.

- **Money is a material product managers work with.** Even nonprofits and government agencies spend money to develop features and products. Recognizing the financial dimension as a design material—alongside code and pixels—changes how product managers think about constraints and possibilities.

## Concepts

- [[Revenue Model]] — this chapter lists nine common models (free-in-beta then paid, free trial then paid, free trial then recurring subscription, paid subscription with no trial, free with in-app purchases, free with a paid Pro upgrade, freemium paywall, enterprise licences by seat, enterprise licences by usage) and emphasizes that models are testable and pivotable rather than fixed; includes Crumlish's case study of how the right model (paid therapy) differs from failed or marginal attempts (donations, directory).

- [[Business Strategy]] — the chapter frames P&L literacy, revenue lifecycle management (establishing, sustaining, sunsetting), and the tension between growth and profitability as core product strategy decisions; includes Crumlish's narrative of choosing among three directions at break-even and the organizational dynamics (VC dependency, investor anxiety, resource trade-offs) that shaped 7 Cups' choices.

- [[Sustainable Growth]] — covers the progression from unsustainable launch phases through optimizing established revenue streams to the long-term survivability of a business model; "ramen profitable" as a milestone that makes the runway theoretically infinite while resting on below-market pay, which Crumlish says is not real sustainability in the long run.
