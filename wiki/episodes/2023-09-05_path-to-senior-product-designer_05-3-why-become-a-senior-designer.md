# The Path to Senior Product Designer: 3. Why Become a Senior Designer?

## Summary

This chapter explains why reaching the senior designer level is strategic for nearly any long-term career goal. While better compensation is the immediate benefit, senior designer status is a required step for advancing to staff or principal roles on the individual contributor track, and a prerequisite for most management positions. Even for paths outside traditional seniority—freelancing, starting a business, specializing, or moving to product management—being a senior designer substantially improves the odds of success. The chapter presents a table in which the author, from his own experience, estimates the minimum competency levels each career path requires, addresses the trade-off between time spent developing these skills and the cost of waiting, and argues that if you are uncertain about your long-term goal, progressing to senior is a safe bet because it equips you with capabilities useful across all paths.

## Key Takeaways

- **Senior is a required step for IC advancement** — To reach staff designer or principal designer, you must first be a senior designer.
- **Senior is nearly required for management** — In most companies, becoming a design manager requires prior experience as a senior designer, though in rare cases promotion from mid-level is possible if soft skills are highly developed.
- **Senior strengthens most other paths** — For freelancers, business owners, and product managers, being a senior designer is not strictly required but puts you in a much stronger position to succeed, as it develops breadth across dimensions like communication, strategy, collaboration, leadership, and ownership.
- **Competency differences by path** — A table the author says he built from his own experience rates, on a one-to-five scale, the minimum level of eight competencies (design craft, communication, collaboration, ownership, strategy, mentorship, leadership, community and self-marketing) for junior, mid, senior, PM, freelancer and owner.
- **Consider the cost of time** — Spending one or two years improving skills before a major change carries a real cost. If a specific opportunity arises, take it even if you feel unready, because learning by doing often yields faster growth than additional time at your current company.
- **Senior is a safe default if unsure** — If you cannot yet see beyond the next career step, progressing to senior keeps all options open and builds skills useful across all paths.
- **Company experience has lasting benefits** — Working at a well-known company teaches you organizational processes (valuable for future freelancers and founders), and a recognizable name on your resume opens doors with clients and investors.
