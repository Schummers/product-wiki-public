# How to Get Stakeholders to Sketch: A Magic Formula

## Summary

Collaborative sketching in design workshops yields multiple benefits including increased buy-in, idea diversity from varied perspectives, shared ownership, and faster design processes. However, non-designers often resist sketching, claiming they cannot draw. A formula combining four variables reduces participant anxiety and enables productive group sketching: fat markers, tiny spaces, time limits, and ugly examples. These constraints work together to emphasize idea generation over refinement, discourage overthinking, and remove perception that sketches must be aesthetically polished.

## Key Takeaways

- **Benefits of collaborative sketching** — increases buy-in for design decisions, brings diversity of ideas, creates shared ownership, speeds design processes, exposes design to non-designers.
- **Magic formula** — fat markers plus tiny spaces plus time limits plus ugly examples equals comfortable sketchers; each variable reinforces low-fidelity emphasis and reduces intimidation.
- **Fat markers variable** — thick-tip markers limit detail and refinement; prevent participants from getting overly granular; encourage rough output inviting conversation rather than suggesting final ideas.
- **Tiny spaces variable** — restrict drawing space using index cards, sticky notes, or 8-up sheets; shrinking available space shrinks participant anxiety and emphasizes idea generation over solutions.
- **Time limits variable** — most important variable; prevents overthinking and self-censoring; use visible countdown timers; research shows people expand to fill time with no quality or quantity gains.
- **Ugly examples variable** — show low-fidelity, hastily drawn sketches composed of basic shapes; avoid polished examples that send silent message that sketching is only for creative roles.

## Quotes

> Bringing people from a diversity of backgrounds together to explore ideas via sketching provides a range of outcomes that enhance UX professionals' ability to be effective in their role.

> If you've ever attempted to spontaneously engage nondesigners in a quick sketching activity, you've probably heard this in response: "I'm not a designer! I can't draw!"

> Simply calling the activity sketching rather than designing sets the stage for the (more principled) instructions. The less intimidating vocabulary lowers the barrier to participating.
