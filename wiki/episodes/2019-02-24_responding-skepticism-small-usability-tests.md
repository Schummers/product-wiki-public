# \"But You Tested with Only 5 Users!\": Responding to Skepticism About Findings From Small Studies

## Summary

This article provides techniques for addressing stakeholder skepticism about findings from qualitative usability tests with small participant samples. Common objections involve concerns about participant representativeness or sample size adequacy to prove problems. The article recommends proactive explanation of methodology, direct observation of sessions, recruiting representative participants, analyzing single-user findings systematically, and using quantitative data or UX theory to support qualitative results.

## Key Takeaways

- **Explain methodology before testing** — Preparing stakeholders with clear explanations of qualitative usability testing methods is more persuasive than defending findings after skepticism has formed.
- **Direct observation builds confidence** — Arranging for teams to observe testing sessions allows them to see real users struggle and develop empathy, making findings more credible than written reports alone.
- **Recruit genuinely representative participants** — Test with actual users or carefully screen participants to match target audience characteristics; share recruitment details with stakeholders in advance.
- **Don't discount single-user findings** — Seeing only one person experience a problem doesn't make it invalid; consider fix cost, problem severity, competitive analysis, and usability theory to evaluate significance.
- **Check independent data sources** — Support qualitative findings with usage analytics, support requests, and event tracking to estimate problem frequency and impact.
- **Reference established UX theory** — Relate test observations to published usability guidelines and heuristics to demonstrate that issues align with broader research and not just isolated instances.

## Quotes

> Usability testing with a small number of participants is an incredibly efficient way to improve an interface.

> Rather than trying to prove exactly how bad each problem is by repeating tests with a lot of users, we will focus on quickly discovering common issues, so we can fix them and improve the design.

> It's pointless to discover design problems if the team doesn't believe the findings and therefore doesn't do anything to fix the issues.
