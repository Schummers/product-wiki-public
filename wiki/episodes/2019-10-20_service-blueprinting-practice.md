# Service Blueprinting in Practice: Who, When, What

## Summary

Based on research with 97 practitioners, this article reveals how service blueprints are actually used in practice across industries. The article identifies three mental models practitioners hold (blueprint as artifact, framework, or collaborative tool), determines that design teams most commonly own blueprinting initiatives, and shows that fewer than expected teams include all necessary stakeholders. The article also documents five key moments in the design lifecycle where blueprints add value, from defining research plans to implementation, and highlights three primary benefits practitioners report.

## Key Takeaways

- **Three mental models of service blueprinting** — practitioners view blueprints as artifacts/visualizations (56%), exercises/frameworks for analysis (20%), or collaborative communication tools (15%); the artifact-focused view risks missing process benefits.
- **Design teams lead initiatives** — service blueprinting is most commonly owned by design teams, followed by product/project management and research; yet fewer than half of organizations involve senior leadership, customer support, marketing, and sales.
- **Five uses across the design lifecycle** — blueprints help define research plans by identifying knowledge gaps, enable empathy through discovering colleague activities, prioritize improvements by revealing gaps and redundancies, inform ideation of future services, and guide implementation and strategic decisions.
- **Shared understanding and alignment** — practitioners most frequently report that blueprints create a common language across departments, often becoming a single source of truth for cross-functional teams.
- **Holistic thinking and project planning** — blueprints make invisible service components visible, reframe experiences beyond silos, and help identify service dependencies and improvement opportunities for roadmap prioritization.

## Quotes

> Service blueprints help departments align behind a common goal, empower team members, and educate stakeholders about the experience as it exists today (especially where complications or redundancies often occur).

> The process of blueprinting is as important as the resulting blueprint itself; it should force conversation, create a need for research and investigation, and foster a shared language and understanding.

> Service blueprints can be used to identify and communicate service weaknesses or redundancies, serve as a guiding source of truth, and inform organizational roadmaps.
