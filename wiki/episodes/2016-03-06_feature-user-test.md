# Getting Users to a Specific Feature in a Usability Test

## Summary

When testing a specific feature, the temptation is to send users directly to the target page to save time and focus study scope. However, this approach typically produces weak data about the feature's real-world usability. Users behave differently when pre-positioned, and getting to the feature is itself a critical part of using it. In real life, users judge every page skeptically upon arrival and must decide if it's worth engaging. A study of embedded calculators found that 36% of task failures occurred during navigation, not interaction. In most cases, letting users navigate naturally from a broader starting point yields richer insights.

## Key Takeaways

- **Navigation problems are part of feature usability** — getting to a feature is often the biggest barrier; skipping this misses major issues and fails to reflect real-world usage.
- **User behavior differs when pre-positioned** — being sent directly to a target page primes users differently than discovering it naturally; they won't experience the skeptical evaluation and intention-judging that real users do.
- **Broader study scope yields deeper insights** — in a study of embedded apps, researchers missed a critical finding (36% of failures were navigation-related) by focusing narrowly on the apps themselves.
- **Every page requires judgment** — users must evaluate whether a page is worth their time before engaging; when researchers designate a page as correct, this natural skepticism disappears.
- **Use bookmarks as a neutral shortcut** — when you must pre-position users, bookmark the target page and rename bookmarks to neutral labels to avoid priming users or revealing the full study scope.
- **Avoid long URLs and search shortcuts** — typing errors and search-result selection bias make bookmarks the least disruptive method for navigating to a specific page.

## Quotes

> Common mistake in usability testing is to define the study scope too narrowly. You usually gain invaluable lessons from broader tasks and having users approach the problem from scratch instead of taking them to an artificial starting point.

> Getting to something is a major part of using that thing. Quite often, navigation problems can be the biggest hindrance to the use of a feature.

> In real life, there's no silver bell that rings when users finally arrive at the right page.
