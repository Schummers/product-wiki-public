# Your Design System Needs an Enforcer

## Summary

Design systems promise consistency and efficiency but fail without enforcement. Small deviations compound quickly—each product manager requesting "small adjustments" to a carousel leads to dozens of variations, confused customers, and unmaintainable code. The enforcer role (not authoritarian but stewardship) ensures teams use the system, rolls innovations back into it, and facilitates compromises. Success requires executive backing, engineering support, strategic timing of reviews, clear contribution processes, and responsive judgment. Enforcers must distinguish legitimate needs from one-off exceptions: if three or more teams need a change, it belongs in the system; if one team does, it's an exception. The goal is not perfection but saying yes to the right things at the right time.

## Key Takeaways

- **The Enforcer Role Is Stewardship** — Enforcers ensure teams use the system, roll innovations back into it, and help compromises when needs conflict; this is not about saying no.
- **Small Deviations Compound Quickly** — One team customizes a carousel component, another adjusts a button; over time, dozens of variations emerge, confusing customers and breaking maintainability.
- **Three Core Functions** — Ensuring adoption, rolling innovations back, and facilitating compromises when different teams have different needs.
- **Success Requires Authority and Support** — Executive backing gives enforcers veto power; engineering support ensures designers and engineers collaborate; without this, enforcement becomes a suggestion.
- **"Three or More Teams" Rule** — If a change serves three or more teams, it belongs in the system; if it's one-off, it's an exception; prototype first when uncertain.
- **Flexibility Wins Adoption** — A perfect system nobody uses is worthless; a slightly messier system that solves real problems is valuable; teams trust systems that evolve with them.

## Quotes

> Here's what trips people up: they think enforcement is about saying no. It's not. It's about saying yes to the right things at the right time.

> Small deviations compound quickly. I once worked at an ecommerce company with a standard product-carousel component. It worked perfectly until every product manager started requesting "small" adjustments for their area.

> A perfect system that nobody uses is worthless. A slightly messier system that solves real problems is valuable.

## Concepts

- [[Design System]] — Effective design systems require active enforcement, not just documentation; enforcement ensures consistency and prevents a "Frankenstein's monster of competing patterns."
- [[Organizational Culture]] — System enforcement works only with organizational backing (executive authority, engineering support); it's a management and culture problem, not just design.
- [[Design Governance]] — Clear governance—ownership, contribution processes, review timing, decision criteria—prevents teams from working around the system.
- [[Component Consistency]] — Consistency benefits customers (learned interactions, reduced cognitive load) and engineers (maintainability); enforcement protects these benefits from erosion.
- [[Stakeholder Engagement]] — Enforcers balance stakeholder needs through judgment, regular reviews, and responsive decision-making; this requires diplomacy and credibility.
