# UX Research: Chapter 14: Communicating Insights

## Summary

This chapter addresses how to present research findings after analysis is complete. In his "Voice from the Streets" sidebar, Dan Brown frames the guiding question as "so what": insights have value only when they help a team make decisions. The chapter's own guidance is to create at least two separate presentations rather than one master presentation: an executive summary (5–10 slides highlighting activities and learnings) and a full report (detailed companion document with sections on introduction, research highlights, methods, users, logistics, findings, recommendations, and next steps).

Beyond presentations, the chapter surveys modes of presenting: video and audio (the most convincing evidence of what's working and not), participant quotes (anonymous, with demographic context), and research artifacts and diagrams (experience maps, personas, mind maps, user flows, swim lanes, mental models, severity scales, click maps, design specifications, change logs). The chapter emphasizes that artifacts should be used by teams going forward, not archived, and that different audiences (executives, team members, stakeholders) require different framing.

## Key Takeaways

- **Create separate presentations for different audiences** — Rather than one master presentation that tries to cover everything and becomes generic and long, create at least an executive summary (5–10 slides for executives and high-level stakeholders) and a full report (detailed document for the team). Different audiences need different levels of detail and framing.

- **The core question is "So what?"** — Insights have value when they translate to design decisions. Dan Brown's sidebar argues that creating an artifact at the tail end of research may not be necessary if findings translate immediately to design decisions (e.g., which information to prioritize, how to restructure a flow, which words to use). The deliverable is often the conversation, not the polished report.

- **Video and audio are the most convincing evidence** — Stakeholders find it hard to argue against research findings when they watch or hear user feedback directly. Highlight reels compiled from recordings serve both executive and full-report contexts.

- **Use participant quotes with context** — When recording is not possible, quotes (anonymous, with demographic or behavioral context) are equally valuable for showing what stakeholders observed.

- **Experience maps give perspective on different touchpoints** — Experience maps show how a product's impact unfolds over time, giving teams perspective on challenges at different phases; stakeholders find value in their objective representation of a product's impact on customers' lives. The chapter points to James Kalbach's *Mapping Experiences* (O'Reilly) for more on the method.

- **Personas make user segments easily digestible** — A persona captures key behaviors and qualities of a user segment, making it easier for teams to recall and discuss different types of users. The chapter points to *The Persona Lifecycle* by John Pruitt and Tamara Adlin (Elsevier) for further reading.

- **Mind maps highlight connections between data points** — Mind maps bring together piles of data points and visualize their relationships. By emphasizing different areas, teams can communicate different viewpoints on the same data.

- **User flows and swim lanes communicate complex workflows** — A user flow doesn't dictate the final design but informs the team of key considerations, mapping the actions of a single user; the chapter points to Marco Brambilla and Piero Fraternali's *Interaction Flow Modeling Language* (Elsevier) for more. Swim lanes extend this by mapping triggers and touchpoints across multiple actors, services, or systems, communicating complexities spanning multiple sources or time periods.

- **Mental models provide inventories of thoughts and actions** — These diagrams (sometimes spanning a conference room's length) show how users think and act in a defined context, informing how a product supports or hinders user goals. The chapter points to Indi Young's *Mental Models* (Rosenfeld Media) for more.

- **Severity scales focus teams on priority** — Rating issues using Nielsen Norman Group's Severity Ratings (0 = not a problem, 1 = cosmetic, 2 = minor, 3 = major, 4 = catastrophe) helps teams prioritize fixes and inform timelines; the chapter presents it as a common severity scale teams use, not the only one.

- **Use research artifacts with teams going forward** — Each insight should be distilled into actionable tasks for the product backlog, with artifacts referenced for rationale and context. Findings have both near-term benefits (immediate design decisions) and long-term benefits (informing product roadmaps and future research priorities).

- **Include stakeholder observations to build allies** — Capturing comments from stakeholders who observed sessions (even if biased) is valuable. Pointing to a stakeholder and saying "Remember when you saw the user struggle with feature X?" builds allies and reinforces that data is leading product development; the same quotes help counter uninformed comments from stakeholders who did not observe.

## Concepts

- [[Design Presentation]] — the chapter covers how to present research findings to different audiences through executive summaries and full reports, with guidance on structure, audience framing, and the choice between formal presentations and conversational discussions. The emphasis is on translating findings into actionable insights rather than presenting data for its own sake.

- [[Storytelling]] — the chapter treats reporting as a story to craft: used correctly, presentations tell a powerful story, and the medium changes with the stage of development, the method and the audience. Dan Brown's sidebar goes further, arguing that with the "leaning" of UX, stakeholders increasingly don't expect polished reports; his research often lives in a simple text file, and the discussion it seeds with the team is the deliverable.

- [[Data Visualization]] — the chapter catalogs visual artifacts for research: experience maps (for touchpoints over time), personas (for user behaviors and qualities), mind maps (for data point connections), user flows (for single-user actions), swim lanes (for multi-actor workflows), mental models (for user thought and action), severity scales (for issue prioritization), click maps (for user journey within a system), and change logs (for design iteration tracking).

- [[Persona]] — personas are presented as a tool for making user behavior and qualities digestible for teams and stakeholders, helping them recall and discuss different user segments and their needs.

- [[Journey Mapping]] — the chapter presents experience maps as a method for showing how users interact with a product across time and multiple touchpoints, giving stakeholders perspective on challenges and opportunities at different phases.
