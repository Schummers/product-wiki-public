# 13 QR-Code Usability Guidelines

## Summary

QR codes bridge physical and digital spaces with lower interaction cost than typing URLs, but their effectiveness depends on thoughtful design. Unlike links with labels, QR codes have no information scent—users cannot predict destinations. Thirteen guidelines address placement, context, size, deep-linking, mobile-friendliness, and security. Key principles include providing clear contextual information about what scanning yields, ensuring adequate size for reliable scanning (minimum 2cm×2cm), deep-linking to relevant content rather than homepages, and preventing security risks like phishing. QR codes work well for progressive disclosure, device authentication, and cross-channel transitions, but should not be used when users have less than 15 seconds to scan or as the primary method for frequent site revisits. Security concerns include phishing, malware, unauthorized payments, and fake codes overlaid on legitimate ones.

## Key Takeaways

- **Provide context, not just the code** — QR codes have no information scent; users need clear labels or URLs explaining what scanning will yield before they'll scan.
- **Size matters: 2cm×2cm minimum** — aim for larger codes when viewing distance increases; for every 10cm of distance, add 1cm to code dimensions for reliable scanning.
- **Deep-link to relevant content** — users expect codes to lead directly to related information, not generic homepages; app-download codes should go to App Store or Play Store.
- **Mobile-friendly destinations required** — assume all scans occur on mobile devices; linked pages must render well on small screens.
- **Use for authentication between devices** — QR codes efficiently enable users signed in on one device to authenticate on another, balancing convenience with security.
- **Three use cases dominate** — progressive disclosure (additional info in limited space), cross-channel transitions (physical to digital), and reducing print needs.
