# Virtual Tours: High Interaction Cost, Moderate Usefulness

## Summary

While virtual tours provide value for checking spatial details, they suffer from high interaction costs and technical limitations rooted in 1990s video-game interaction paradigms. Users often perceive virtual tours as secondary to traditional photo galleries and only interact with them later in decision-making when seeking detailed spatial information. Most virtual tours lack effective navigation, guidance, and meaningful contextual information. The most successful virtual tours limit interaction cost and provide expert guidance rather than freeform exploration, and virtual tours are best positioned as a tertiary information source after photos and descriptions.

## Key Takeaways

- **Secondary use case** — Users treat virtual tours as secondary or tertiary tools, preferring to start with photo galleries for quick visual scanning. Tours are used later when verifying spatial details and checking for undesirable aspects not visible in professional photos.
- **High interaction cost** — Movement through virtual spaces is slow and effortful, especially turning around, which can require 8+ swipes on mobile. Users often experience dizziness and disorientation. The interaction model has not progressed beyond 1990s video-game standards.
- **Initial delight fades quickly** — While visually impressive virtual tours elicit initial excitement, the wow factor dissipates quickly. Users close tours and move to other information sources, particularly for leisure destinations.
- **Poor wayfinding** — Users frequently cannot determine which specific room they are viewing or what rooms are nearby. Multiple navigation arrows in small spaces become confusing. Filmstrip-style navigation galleries are often ignored by users.
- **Lack of expert guidance** — Users desire traditional narrated videos or expert-led tours alongside virtual exploration. Expert information about room dimensions, appliance conditions, furniture fit, and other practical details would enhance tours significantly.
- **Opportunities for enhancement** — Virtual tours could be better than reality by providing room dimensions, appliance information, and expert insights by default, rather than requiring users to do the work through measuring tools or exploration.

## Quotes

> "I don't see a point of just the 3D tour without photos — [photos] are primary and [a 3D tour] is secondary"

> "The photos are like an advertisement to entice me to go in. If I take the tour, I'm investing a lot more time, so it's good for looking at all the nooks and crannies. The 3D tour is when I start to fall in love and want to see the details."

> "I do like that it allows me to move around freely in the home, so I can see the spatial relationships. This gives me a much better idea what it's actually like in, say, a bedroom, because seeing it in this 3D tour, the room looks much smaller than it did in the [still] photos."

## Concepts

- [[Virtual Reality]] — Modern virtual tour interfaces remain stuck in outdated interaction models from 1990s video games, with slow movement, limited control sensitivity, and navigation challenges that create interaction costs exceeding perceived benefits.
- [[Information Architecture]] — Virtual tours work best when integrated into information hierarchies with photos as primary touchpoint, narrated videos as secondary, and interactive tours as tertiary options for detailed spatial verification.
- [[Interaction Design]] — Successful virtual tours minimize interaction cost through guided, curated experiences rather than freeform exploration, and incorporate contextual expert information rather than requiring users to explore and discover details independently.
