# Product Management for UX People: CHAPTER 11: Chief Information Architect

## Summary

This chapter addresses the role of head of product as a "chief information architect" — someone responsible for maintaining coherence across a complex product or product family, especially as organizational pressures create fragmentation. Crumlish argues that the discipline of information architecture (mapping systems, revealing concepts, providing a coherent story) is essential to product leadership. The chapter then outlines the work of being a product leader: communicating like an executive, building product teams through hiring and enabling transitions, investing in team development through cross-training, and selling your team's services internally as a product. A product leader's job is to create the organizational conditions for coherent product thinking and to develop the next generation of product practitioners.

## Key Takeaways

- **Where the idea comes from** — The term "chief information architect" is Crumlish's own, arrived at after his product mentor Matte Scheinker asked him at AOL, where the title had vanished from the HR system, how many IAs a company needs; his answer was "at least one." The coherence question itself he quotes from information architect and consultant Jorge Arango, on Arango's *Informed Life* podcast in 2020, asking where in the product management framework there is a place for looking after coherence between things that belong to an ecosystem or family of products, with Kindle across dedicated reader, iOS and Mac as his example of its absence.

- **Chief information architect** — Someone needs to be responsible for the coherence of a product or product family at the highest level, mapping the problem/opportunity space and maintaining unity across platforms, device experiences, and organizational silos. Without this role, drift and decoherence are inevitable.

- **Decoherence comes from organizational structure, per Dawn Russell** — Dawn Russell, an information architect at CVS Health, argues in the chapter that decoherence is also born from org structure and internal funding models, giving as her example the flu shot offered through both the CVS pharmacy and MinuteClinic: same shot, same location, different lines of business, and so two ways to schedule it on one domain, with the onus on the customer. Crumlish adds that where everything constantly shifts it is almost impossible to impose coherence top-down, but a product leader can gradually bring a complex system into harmony.

- **Speaking executive language, per Harry Max** — The advice comes from Harry Max, a leadership consultant who has guided many product and UX leaders, and who points out that designers are often not all that great at communicating with executives: to be taken seriously and get a seat at the table you have to speak the language of business, talking about making money, saving money and avoiding unnecessary costs. Around it, product leaders must communicate in terms of business outcomes, money, brand, market share, hiring, and retention rather than design details; this requires precision, deep listening, and answering the questions executives are actually asking.

- **Team as internal product** — Applying product thinking to your own team—understanding your colleagues' needs, crafting deliverables and communications that solve their problems, overcommunicating, and building trust—is as important as external product work.

- **Partner with customer-facing teams** — Customer support, customer success, and community managers are invaluable sources of signal about what customers want and trend toward; siloing these teams away wastes crucial insights. The chapter's precedent is Craig Newmark, founder of Craigslist, who focused on customer support rather than making himself CEO and argued that a business only learns by channelling external feedback straight to the decision-making centre.

- **Enable transitions into product** — Identify people with product instincts from other roles (engineers, designers, marketers, support staff) and formally transition them with training, coaching, and mentorship.

- **A named practitioner's own day closes the chapter** — The chapter carries a "A Day in the Life of a VP of Product" box contributed by Benoît des Ligneris, Vice President of Product at Woolf, describing his own environment and practice rather than a generic one.

- **Cross-training builds organizational resilience** — Pairing junior or less-experienced product people with experienced practitioners to learn skills like analytics and experimentation distributes expertise and makes the team more flexible.

## Quotes

> Where within this \[product management\] framework is there a place for looking after the coherence between those things? Especially if they’re part of some kind of ecosystem or family of products. Eventually those things need to cohere at some level . . . .

> “If you want to be taken seriously and you want a seat at the table and you want to start dealing with executives, you have to start speaking the language of business. You’ve got to talk about making money. You’ve got to talk about saving money. You’ve got to talk about avoiding unnecessary costs.”

## Concepts

- [[Design Leadership]] — The head of product serves as a chief information architect, responsible for maintaining coherence and mapping meaning across a product or product family, preventing organizational fragmentation from producing incoherent experiences.

- [[Team Collaboration]] — Product leaders must apply user-centered design thinking to their own teams, treating colleagues as customers and overcommunicating the value and legibility of product work.

- [[Team Scaling]] — Building product teams requires identifying product instincts in existing staff, enabling formal transitions with training and mentorship, and implementing cross-training to develop expertise and organizational flexibility.
