# User Story Mapping: 9. The Card Is Just the Beginning

## Summary

The three Cs of story conversations (card, conversation, confirmation) are the beginning of a cycle that does not end at agreement. The chapter walks through the complete flow: after shared understanding and confirmation are reached, the team builds the software, and Patton's warning is that handing all the details off to someone else to build does not work, because the details that make the written record legible are in the head of whoever was in the conversation. Shared understanding is instead carried forward by retelling the story to whoever needs it next. The chapter also stresses testing with actual users, recognizing that the team's "vacation photos" (their shared picture) often differ from users' reality, and treating learning through iteration as the purpose of the process.

## Key Takeaways

- **Handing Off Doesn't Work** — Once the team has built shared understanding, it is tempting to hand off the details to others to build, but this fails because the shared picture exists in everyone's heads, not on paper. Even thorough documentation cannot capture what the team members were thinking and remembering. Continued storytelling and presence during builds is necessary.

- **Oral Tradition Over Documentation** — Instead of a formal handoff, storytelling from someone who was in the discovery conversation to those who were not is faster and more effective, especially when the listener can ask questions and mark up pictures to build her own vacation photos.

- **Keep the Group Small, and Not Everyone Needs to Be There** — Patton names as an anti-pattern the belief that everyone on the team must be in every conversation; effective discussion and decision making goes best with two to five people, "dinner conversation sized," with small groups deciding and continued conversations sharing the result. He says the same about user testing: everyone does not need to be in the room with users, and it would creep them out.
- **Test with Real Users** — The team's agreement about what to build is not a reliable prediction of user behavior. User testing reveals mismatches quickly and provides both motivation and direction for improvements.

- **Plan to Be Wrong** — Alistair Cockburn's principle that for every story you write, you need to put three into the backlog (write the story, fix the first card, fix the second) reflects the reality that learning is the purpose of building software. Don't assume getting it right the first time is the goal.

- **Validated Learning Over Working Software** — Kent Beck revised the Agile Manifesto to emphasize validated learning: working software is a means, but what matters is learning whether the solution truly serves the need. Stories can drive prototypes and user research, not just software builds.

## Quotes

> Handing off all the details about the story to someone else to build doesn't work. Don't do that.

> Effective discussion and decision making goes best with small groups of two to five. It's dinner conversation sized.

> Try using stories to drive the making of anything, whether it's software or not.

## Concepts

- [[Shared Understanding]] — Patton emphasizes that shared understanding must be maintained throughout the build process through continued storytelling and presence, not handed off as documentation, and that everyone's shared picture must be tested against user reality.

- [[Usability Testing]] — The chapter stresses regular testing with real users; Patton leaves the frequency open (he often waits until enough software is built to let a user accomplish something new) but says not to let more than a couple of weeks pass without watching a genuine user interact with the software, to see how actual behavior diverges from the team's expectations, and to build empathy across the team by witnessing user struggles.

- [[Validated Learning]] — Learning is the core purpose, not just shipping software. The team should plan to iterate and learn from each build, reflecting both on whether they got what they agreed to and on whether what they built is actually what users need.

- [[Product Discovery]] — After the build and user testing, the team writes new story cards capturing improvements and insights, feeding them back into the discovery cycle so that products evolve continuously rather than being finalized at release.
