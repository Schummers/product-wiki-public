# The Sketch Test: How to Test and Improve Your UX Deliverables and Other Documents

## Summary

The sketch test is a simple validation method for UX deliverables (wireframes, personas, journey maps, reports) that applies telephone-game principles. A colleague reads the deliverable and creates a sketch or summary of it; discrepancies reveal what the audience misunderstood or found unclear. The method resembles user testing but targets the deliverable's audience (designers, developers, product managers) rather than end users. It surfaces both perceptual problems (hard-to-see content) and comprehension problems (misunderstood meaning).

## Key Takeaways

- **Sketch tests reveal perceptual and comprehension issues** — content may be visually obscured (perceptual) or misinterpreted (comprehension); both limit deliverable effectiveness.
- **Recruit representative audience members** — test participants should match the deliverable's actual audience (developers for wireframes, project managers for reports); colleagues are appropriate here.
- **Participant observations matter more than direct feedback** — watch what they circle, underline, redraw, or struggle with; these behaviors reveal pain points better than verbal suggestions.
- **Encourage annotation and note-taking** — providing scratch paper and permission to mark the deliverable directly uncovers mental models and assumptions.
- **Avoid correcting participant misunderstandings during testing** — follow user research protocol; correcting biases the remaining data.
- **Use participant sketches to guide revision** — pay attention to different visual metaphors, intermediate inferences, and unfamiliar terminology they use; these hint at content gaps.
- **Document but also gather content feedback** — while the focus is format clarity, derivative content suggestions are acceptable and valuable for refinement.

## Quotes

> You give your deliverable to a colleague and ask her to create a short sketch or summary of your document. Observe what she sketches and writes. Then identify confusing or unclear elements, and iterate on the deliverable.

> It's ironic that UX professionals focus so much on understanding their products' end users, but don't extend the same sort of user centeredness to coworkers who are the target audience for their deliverables.
