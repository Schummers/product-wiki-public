# User Story Mapping: 15. Using Discovery for Validated Learning

## Summary

This chapter integrates design thinking — which Patton traces to IDEO and Stanford's d.school — and Lean Startup thinking, which he traces from Steve Blank to Eric Ries, into product discovery, arguing that most of what teams build fails to deliver the expected outcomes because they guess rather than validate their assumptions. Patton describes a short feedback loop approach: name risky assumptions, build small tests (not full prototypes), measure by observing customers, and rethink based on results. This validated learning cycle emphasizes speed and learning over perfection.

## Key Takeaways

- **Most of What We Build Doesn't Move the Needle** — Patton gives a 20 / 20 / 60 split — successful, genuinely harmful, and neither — while stating plainly that it is his own belief from his failures and what he observes, "not rooted in any formal scientific research or studies." The published research he cites is separate: Standish Group "Chaos" figures that 64 to 75 percent of features are rarely or never used, and, depending on the source, 75 to 90 percent of software startups failing. The strategy of guessing and pretending success doesn't work; teams must validate assumptions instead.

- **Design Thinking's Five Steps** — Design thinking is a way of working Patton attributes to IDEO and later described and taught by Stanford's d.school. Empathize (observe users directly, understand their context), define (distill shared understanding and focus on specific problems), ideate (deliberately generate multiple solutions), prototype (build simple models to explore ideas), and test (observe users actually using the solution, not performing imaginary use).

- **Design Processes Can Fail in Predictable Ways** — Common mistakes include starting without framing business needs, spending too much time researching without stopping, assuming design alone guarantees success, creating beautiful prototypes that don't work, or blaming people when outcomes don't match expectations.

- **Lean Startup: Build-Measure-Learn Loops** — Patton credits the underlying idea to Steve Blank, an advisor to Ries's company, whose *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* argued the first thing to develop is not a product but customers, and named that progressive validation a validated learning process; Eric Ries's contribution, in Patton's reading, was simplifying and productizing it into build-measure-learn and compressing the loop from weeks or months to days. Starting with guesses (informed by passion, experience, and insight), naming risky assumptions, building the smallest possible test (often not code), running tests with customers, and rethinking based on results. Failing fast is good news because it happens before large investments.

- **Minimum Viable Product Means Smallest Viable Test** — A minimum viable product is not a half-baked version of the final product; it is the smallest experiment needed to learn whether a core assumption is valid, whether through a design comic, a paper prototype, or a coded MVP. Patton's example is the nonprofit ITHAKA, whose JSTOR team tested its assumptions about off-campus student access with a design comic and 30-60 minute student interviews, and learned that some students did not have the problem they had assumed.

- **Stories and Story Maps Support Validated Learning** — During discovery, teams constantly tell stories about who users are and what they do. Story maps show how people work today and how they might use a solution. Stories break ideas into small pieces for quick agreement, and the whole conversation can happen in hours, not weeks, because the goal is learning, not planning detailed build work.

## Quotes

> The truth is that in the past I released lots of solutions I thought would be wildly successful, but they just weren't. They weren't dismal failures, either—they just didn't make much of a difference.

> It's actually not just guessing. It's a mixture of passion, experience, and insight—along with a fair dose of guessing—that gets the ball rolling.

> In a Lean Startup approach, failing to learn is frequently the biggest failure.

## Concepts

- [[Validated Learning]] — Patton endorses Eric Ries's build-measure-learn cycle as a way to test assumptions faster and cheaper than traditional design processes, emphasizing that initial guesses become valuable only after validation against real user behavior.

- [[Design Thinking]] — The five-step process (empathize, define, ideate, prototype, test) provides structure to discovery, but can fail if teams rush, assume success, or blame people rather than processes when outcomes don't match; Patton advocates for time-boxing and focus on specific problems.

- [[Minimum Viable Product]] — Patton reframes MVP not as a reduced-scope product but as the smallest possible experiment (design comic, paper prototype, or coded minimum) needed to validate a core assumption, allowing teams to learn quickly before building the full vision.

- [[Prototyping]] — Simple, low-fidelity prototypes (paper, comics, minimal code) help teams think through solutions and filter out ideas that won't work before building scalable software. The goal is learning, not perfection.

- [[Product Discovery]] — Discovery work using validated learning principles runs on shorter cycles (days to weeks) than traditional design, with teams constantly telling stories, building prototypes, and testing assumptions alongside the discovery team's multidisciplinary collaboration.
