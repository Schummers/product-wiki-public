# Quantitative User-Research Methodologies: An Overview

## Summary

Quantitative research methods allow UX professionals to put numbers on usability, compare designs statistically, estimate ROI for improvements, and tie UX to business goals. This overview covers nine quant methods: quantitative usability testing, web analytics, A/B testing, card sorting, tree testing, surveys, clustering qualitative data, desirability studies, and eyetracking. Each method has different cost, difficulty, data type, and context requirements. Choosing the right method requires starting with the research question, then considering budget and minimum sample size requirements.

## Key Takeaways

- **Method selection by research question** — general questions about usability trends use quant testing, analytics, or surveys; specific questions about design choices use A/B testing, card sorting, tree testing, or desirability studies.
- **Cost and difficulty vary widely** — web analytics and surveys are low-cost and low-difficulty; eyetracking and in-person testing are high-cost with high difficulty; remote methods reduce costs significantly.
- **Sample size requirements** — quantitative methods require larger sample sizes than qualitative testing (typically 20-30 users minimum) to reach statistical significance; results from 5 users should not inform design decisions.
- **Statistical significance requirement** — analysts must not collect metrics and make decisions without statistical analysis; minimum sample sizes exist for each method and must be met.
- **Behavioral vs. attitudinal data** — behavioral methods (usability testing, analytics, A/B testing) measure what people do; attitudinal methods (surveys, card sorting, desirability studies) measure what people say.

## Quotes

> Put a **number** on the usability of your product. Numbers are sometimes more persuasive than findings and videos from qual testing (particularly when you're trying to convince folks like executives).

> You can't just collect metrics and start making decisions without doing any statistical analysis.

> For each method discussed here, there are different recommended **minimum sample sizes**— the number of data points you'll likely need to collect in order to have reliable data and determine statistical significance.
