# Product Management for UX People: Frequently Asked Questions

## Summary

The opening chapter anticipates six key questions that UX professionals often ask when considering a transition to product management. These questions reveal common misconceptions and reveal tensions between UX and product disciplines: whether UX skills transfer to PM, whether PM work allows continuation of design practice, how authority structures differ, and how organizational design affects cross-functional relationships. Rather than providing definitive answers, the FAQ uses each question as an entry point to larger themes explored throughout the book.

## Key Takeaways

- **UX skills as foundation** — UX people "absolutely can" become effective product managers, though Crumlish adds "it's no guarantee": the best PMs he has worked with combined a sophisticated understanding of user experience research, strategy and design principles with an obsession for customer needs and a deep respect for UX practitioners.
- **PM and design are separate roles** — Product managers are not designers; the day-to-day work of the two roles differs significantly, though they share overlapping concerns and require deep respect for each other's expertise.
- **Authority through focus, not hierarchy** — Becoming a PM does not mean bossing engineers around: "not really," Crumlish answers, but you cannot succeed as a PM without learning to organize and focus the efforts of your engineering colleagues, and the book proposes using your UX "superpowers" to become your developers' best ally.
- **Growth hacking is "kinda" the enemy of UX** — Crumlish's answer is a qualified yes, not a no: the "cancerous 'growth for its own sake' ethos" driven by capitalism and venture capitalism does make short shrift of most UX ideals, but "growth" itself is not a dirty word, and every organism must learn to grow to thrive.
- **Organizational structure shapes collaboration** — Whether product and UX teams experience turf wars or healthy partnerships depends on clear role definitions and leadership articulation of responsibilities, not on the disciplines themselves.
- **Information architecture leadership** — Asked how many information architects a product leadership team needs, the answer is "at least one," a claim the FAQ defers to Chapter 11 rather than argues.

## Quotes

> Unlikely. Maybe in a small start-up where they need you to do two jobs at once, but product managers are not designers and the day-to-day work of the two roles differs significantly, as explained in Chapter 2, despite the large overlapping areas of shared concern.

> They absolutely can. It's no guarantee, but the best product managers I've worked with had a sophisticated understanding of user experience research, strategy, and design principles, an abiding obsession with the needs of customers and other users, and a deep respect for UX practitioners.

> Kinda. Certainly the cancerous "growth for its own sake" ethos driven by capitalism and its Silicon Valley derivative, venture capitalism, makes short shrift of most UX ideals, but "growth" itself is not a dirty word.

## Concepts

- [[UX Career]] — the FAQ frames the transition as real but not automatic: UX people can make good PMs with no guarantee, and continuing to do a lot of design work every day as a PM is "unlikely," maybe possible in a small start-up needing two jobs at once.
- [[Product Management]] — the FAQ establishes that PM requires distinct skills and decision-making authority, not simply applied UX expertise.
- [[Team Collaboration]] — cross-functional tensions are organizational design problems, not inherent conflicts; clarity about role responsibilities is the foundation for healthy relationships.
