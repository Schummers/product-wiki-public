# 6 Dimensions for Assessing Usability Data in Analysis

## Summary

This article addresses a critical flaw in usability test analysis: taking individual data points at face value without contextual assessment. The authors introduce a framework of six dimensions — authenticity, consistency, repetition, spontaneity, appropriateness, and confounds — to evaluate the reliability and relevance of each data point. No single comment or behavior tells the full story; researchers must examine what users say, what they do, study design factors, and recruitment strategy to draw trustworthy insights.

## Key Takeaways

- **Single data points are unreliable without context** — positive feedback may come from leading questions, participants may contradict themselves, and behavior may diverge from stated preferences; each data point must be assessed in context of other data points and study design information.
- **Authenticity filters out motivated responses** — comments or behaviors must be examined for naturalness; participants may try to please facilitators, feel compelled by framing, or alter behavior due to professional-participant status or conscious awareness of being observed.
- **Consistency between verbal and behavioral data matters** — when participants say a task was easy but struggled, made errors, or restarted, researchers should prioritize behavioral data; repeated comments or behaviors across sessions signal authentic sentiment or genuine usability problems.
- **Spontaneity versus priming determines genuine feedback** — actions and comments introduced without facilitator cueing (such as revealing study purpose or mentioning UI elements in questions) are more genuine than those elicited through priming or leading questions.
- **Appropriateness and confounds contextualize findings** — participant representativeness, task realism, and unintended influences (order effects, complex task instructions, fatigue) shape the validity of insights; AI cannot yet reliably analyze usability test data because it misses recordings, context, and study design factors.

## Concepts

- [[Usability Testing]] — emphasizes the need for rigorous, contextual analysis of test data across behavioral and verbal dimensions rather than accepting surface-level signals.
- [[Data Analysis]] — introduces a six-dimensional framework for evaluating data point reliability independent of sample size, applicable to both qualitative and quantitative research.
- [[Qualitative Research]] — stresses that qualitative data analysis requires understanding whether data is authentic, consistent, spontaneous, and representative rather than statistically generalizable.
- [[Research Validity]] — articulates how methodological factors (priming, leading questions, order effects) compromise data quality and must be considered during analysis.
- [[Participant Recruitment]] — highlights how misrecruitment (professional participants, coworkers) or unrepresentative sampling undermines appropriateness and limits insight generalizability.
- [[Study Design]] — focuses on how task wording, session structure, facilitation approach, and confounding variables influence participant behavior and must be factored into analysis.
