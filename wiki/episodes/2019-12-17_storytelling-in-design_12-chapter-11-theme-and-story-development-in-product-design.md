# Storytelling in Design: Chapter 11. Theme and Story Development in Product Design

## Summary

This chapter addresses theme and the red thread (through-line) in product design, drawing parallels to their function in traditional storytelling. Dahlström emphasizes that content, UX, and design must evolve together from the start of a project, guided by a clear theme that acts as a coherent through-line binding all decisions. She shows how product teams can apply storytelling approaches to develop a product story, distinguishing between idea, concept, premise, and theme, and integrating tools like content strategy, information architecture, and mental models.

## Key Takeaways

- **Content must be considered from the start, which is not the same as putting real copy in wireframes** — Dahlström's position, given as her own experience and against clients who insist otherwise: placing real copy in wireframes and amending it there causes more hassle and time wasted than value added, since what goes in is never the actual content but draft content. She is "all for using *Lorem ipsum* when used in conjunction with notes about the content that should go in there", and instead plans out the story each page and module should tell, an approximation of the amount of copy, and notes on the details it should cover. What matters is that content and design evolve hand in hand.
- **Theme is a complete sentence, not a word** — Citing Robert McKee, Dahlström defines theme not as a concept like "love and honor" but as a full, coherent sentence expressing the story's irreducible meaning. Theme implies function; it shapes strategic choices throughout the project.
- **The red thread binds the experience** — The "red thread" (*röd tråd* in Swedish, a phrase Dahlström traces to the Swedish children's book *Historien om Någon* by Åke Löfgren and Egon Möller-Nielsen, where a literal red thread runs through the pages, and further back to Ariadne's thread) is an invisible through-line that creates coherence, ensuring that everything in the product experience happens for a reason and is connected. Without it, "essentially anything goes, and the offering and experience can become a car crash."
- **Distinguish idea, concept, premise, and theme** — Journalist Larry Brooks argues the four are used interchangeably but differ, and Dahlström relays his worked example on Alice Sebold's *The Lovely Bones*: the idea is "to tell a story about what heaven is like"; the concept adds a "What if…?" qualifier — "What if a murder victim can't rest in heaven because her crime remains unsolved, and chooses to get involved to help her loved ones gain closure?"; the premise throws a character and the hero's quest into it — "What if a 14-year-old girl cannot rest in heaven, and realizes that her family cannot rest on earth, because her murder remains unsolved, so she intervenes to help uncover the truth and bring peace to those who loved her?"; and the theme is different in kind, raising what the story means to you and its relevance to life. An idea is a subset of a concept, and a concept a subset of a premise.
- **Tools that structure the product story** — Among a number of tools product teams can work with, Dahlström names three: sitemaps, card sorts, and content plans. Combined with methods from storytelling (index cards for the narrative, experience goals for theme definition, main plots and subplots for structure), they provide reference points and a framework for ensuring a theme runs through the product experience.
- **Mental models guide information organization** — Content should be structured not only by needs but by how users naturally organize information. Card sorting and the structure of each page and view should tell the story users expect, accounting for non-linear entry points and the narrative within each screen.

## Quotes

Author Robert McKee on theme, quoted by Dahlström:

> A true theme is not a word, but a sentence—one clear coherent sentence that expresses a story's irreducible meaning…it implies function: the controlling idea shapes the writer's strategic choices.

— Robert McKee

Producer Barri Evins on theme as a decision rule, quoted by Dahlström:

> Using theme as your 'decider', from big choices to small ones, is a powerful tool. It empowers you to infuse theme into every aspect of your story, creating a rich and resonant reading experience.

— Barri Evins

## Concepts

- [[Red Thread]] — the through-line that binds product design together; established by a clear theme and ensures that all content, features, and interactions are connected and purposeful.
- [[Content Strategy]] — must be developed in parallel with UX and design from the project's start, informed by the product's theme and ensuring the right content reaches users at the right time in a way that aligns with their mental models.
- [[Information Architecture]] — shaped by the product's theme and red thread; organizes content to reflect user mental models and expectations, ensuring the structure tells the story users expect regardless of their entry point.
- [[Narrative Structure]] — product teams should define the product story through plotting (Chapter 5) and theme, distinguishing between the idea, concept, premise, and theme, then building content strategy and information architecture to execute it coherently.
