# Storytelling in Design: Chapter 5. Defining and Structuring Experiences with Dramaturgy

## Summary

Chapter 5 introduces dramaturgy—which Dahlström defines with Merriam-Webster as "the art or technique of dramatic composition and theatrical representation"—as a framework for structuring product experiences. Dahlström explains how story structure impacts audience perception, using the Finding Nemo case from Pixar in a Box, Pixar's behind-the-scenes series on Khan Academy, as an example of how reordering information changes emotional response. She maps Aristotle's three-act structure to product life-cycle stages (awareness, consideration, purchase, post-purchase) and explores three major variations: Lavandier's modified three-act structure (before/during/after action), Field's Paradigm (four parts instead of three), and Frank Daniel's sequence paradigm (eight sequences that Daniel suggested keeping to 10–15 minutes). The chapter applies these narrative frameworks to UX design, showing how to break product experiences into sequences, scenes, and shots, and introduces plot points as the events and triggers that propel user experiences forward. It concludes with practical methods for defining product experience narratives: the two-page synopsis method and the index card method.

## Key Takeaways

- **Story structure serves the desired outcome; it is not right or wrong in itself** — Reordering Finding Nemo's flashbacks to the opening changed how the audience felt about Marlin, but the chapter immediately counters with Snape in *Harry Potter*, where withholding his feelings until the end was the point since Rowling wanted readers to dislike him first. The way you structure the story has to do with the desired outcome, for a film as for a product.

- **Three-act structure maps directly to product life cycles** — Aristotle's three acts (setup, confrontation, resolution) correspond to product stages: awareness (first encounter), consideration (research and learning), and purchase/post-purchase (decision and outcome).

- **Frank Daniel's eight-sequence paradigm originated from film reels and applies to product design** — Daniel suggested eight sequences of 10–15 minutes, each with its own beginning, middle, and end and its own conflict and resolution, though not all movies conform to a strict eight-sequence breakdown; after Daniel died in 1996 his disciple Paul Joseph Gulino described the approach in *Screenwriting: The Sequence Approach* (Continuum); this granular structure helps troubleshoot whether an experience has too few or too many sequences.

- **Sequences contain scenes, which contain shots** — In product design translation: acts are the beginning, middle, and end; sequences are life-cycle stages or key journeys; scenes are pages/views or main steps; shots are page elements or detailed journey steps.

- **Plot points are events and triggers that propel experiences forward** — Dahlström quotes screenwriter Robert Towne, that “A movie is really only four or five moments between two people; the rest of it exists to give those moments their impact and resonance,” and cites The Script Lab's naming of those moments as inciting incident, lock-in, midpoint, main culmination and third-act twist.
- **Plot points in product design** — These include triggers (notifications, CTAs), actions (user behaviors like adding to basket), barriers (sign-up requirements), system events (confirmations, errors), and delights (unexpected moments of pleasure); major plot points signal stage transitions.

- **Product experiences follow recognizable narrative patterns** — Ecommerce, SaaS, travel, social media, video on demand, and other categories have typical life-cycle structures and plot point patterns that serve as reference points and foundations for adaptation.

- **The two-page synopsis and index card methods translate screenplay structure to product design** — The synopsis method reaches Dahlström second-hand ("rumor has it" it comes from someone who studied with Daniel): title each sequence, then describe it. She says she personally starts by naming each life-cycle phase and writing one descriptive line for it, so the purpose of each stage is clear to her and everyone else, and that a sketch or a list of bullet points in an email will do just fine.

## Quotes

> Story structure is, in the words of Pixar story artist Kristen Lester, "the answer to the question, What do you want your audience to know and when?"

> Each stage, or sequence in script-writing terms, has a conflict and a resolution that becomes the start of the next stage; for example, "Decides to look into it" in Act 3 of awareness leads to the user starting to research in the consideration stage.

> Whether structure is used to write an actual story, define a product experience, or plan out a presentation, it is part of the backbone of good storytelling.

## Concepts

- [[Narrative Structure]] — Dahlström covers dramaturgy, Aristotle's three-act structure, and three major variations (Lavandier's, Field's Paradigm, Daniel's sequence paradigm); shows how story structure determines audience understanding and how these frameworks map to product life cycles and user journeys.

- [[Customer Journey]] — Maps awareness, consideration, purchase, and post-purchase stages to the three-act structure and demonstrates how to break these into sequences, scenes, and steps; includes an example from Dahlström's work on Sony Ericsson (now Sony Mobile) at the agency Dare, and typical patterns across ecommerce, SaaS, travel, and other categories.

- [[Scene Structure]] — Defines a scene as a single location, continuous time, and consistent cast of characters; explains how scenes make up sequences, and how in product design this translates to pages, views, screens, or main steps in a journey.

- [[User Flow]] — Discussed through the breakdown of life-cycle stages into mini-stories, sequences, and steps; shows how plot points and conflicts/resolutions structure the user's progression through an experience.

- [[Experience Map]] — Demonstrates mapping life-cycle stages and plot points to visualize and understand the narrative structure of product experiences at a high level.

- [[Shared Understanding]] — On the Sony Ericsson project at the agency Dare, defining the product life cycle upfront gave the team a basic structure and story arc that kept everyone on the same page about what mattered to the users and to the business.
