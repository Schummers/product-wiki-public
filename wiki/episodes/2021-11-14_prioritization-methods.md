# 5 Prioritization Methods in UX Roadmapping

## Summary

Prioritizing work into a UX roadmap requires frameworks that base decisions on objective criteria rather than subjective opinions. The article outlines five evidence-based prioritization methods suited to different team contexts and goals: impact-effort matrices, feasibility-desirability-viability scorecards, the RICE method, MoSCoW analysis, and the Kano model. Each method uses distinct criteria and processes to rank features, problems, or initiatives. The article emphasizes that no method is universally better; teams should select approaches matching their culture, expertise, and project constraints, then adapt the method to their needs.

## Key Takeaways

- **Impact-effort matrix suits quick collaboration** — Plots items by relative user value (impact) and implementation complexity (effort), assigning items to quick wins, big bets, money pits, and fill-ins. Works well for democratic, team-wide prioritization.
- **Feasibility-desirability-viability scorecards are customizable** — Scores items across three criteria and sums them for ranking. Highly adaptable; teams can add or replace criteria based on organizational priorities.
- **RICE method works for technical teams** — Multiplies reach × impact × confidence and divides by effort to yield a prioritization score. Quantitative and formula-based, ideal for data-comfortable teams with many items to rank.
- **MoSCoW clusters items into categories** — Groups items into Must Have, Should Have, Could Have, and Will Not Have based on weighted voting and discussion. Works best with clearly defined time horizons.
- **Kano model forces user-centric thinking** — Plots items by functionality and customer satisfaction to categorize them as Attractive, Performance, Indifferent, or Must-be. Useful for teams struggling to prioritize user needs over business pressures.

## Quotes

> These prioritization methods can be used to prioritize a variety of "items," ranging from research questions, user segments, and features to ideas, and tasks.

> One method is not better than another. Consider your project's context, team culture, and success criteria when choosing a prioritization approach.

> The best prioritization methods are ones that everyone on your team, including stakeholders, buy into.
