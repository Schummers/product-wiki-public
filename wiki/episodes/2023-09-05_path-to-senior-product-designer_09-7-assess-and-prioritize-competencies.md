# The Path to Senior Product Designer: 7. Assess and Prioritize Competencies

## Summary

Part III of this book guides designers through building a growth plan. This chapter focuses on the first two critical steps: assessing where you currently stand on each competency, and prioritizing which ones to develop. The chapter emphasizes that self-assessment alone is insufficient; external perspective from managers, peers, or ex-colleagues helps avoid the Dunning–Kruger bias, which causes those with low competence to overestimate their abilities. The author then provides a framework for prioritizing competencies based on three factors: industry requirements, future career goals (especially if planning a job change or business launch), and external opinions. Using this method, designers can create a precise growth strategy that positions them for promotion within their current company or beyond.

## Key Takeaways

- **Dunning–Kruger effect** — A cognitive bias where people with low ability overestimate their competence level. The gap between self-evaluation and actual skill shrinks as competence increases, so seeking external feedback helps surface blind spots.
- **360-degree feedback as foundation** — Assessment should ideally combine feedback from your manager and peers to give you an objective view. If unavailable from your employer, ask colleagues or ex-managers who know your work well.
- **Majority rule for promotion** — Companies typically do not require every single competency at a given level for promotion, but most of them. Long-term demonstration matters: you must have performed most competencies at the target level consistently.
- **Prioritization framework** — Fine-tune your focus by weighting three factors: industry requirements (a score out of five the author assigned to each competency from his own analysis of company documents), your specific career goal (especially relevant if changing jobs soon), and external opinions from people who know your work.
- **Competitive advantage in interviews** — By completing this self-assessment and growth plan, you can confidently explain your weaknesses and your approach to improving them, which is exactly what interviewers want to hear.

## Quotes

> "In order to get promoted, you need to have already performed most competencies at that level for a while. For instance, you would have had to consistently demonstrate most competencies at the senior level in order to get promoted from mid-level to senior level."

> "This is especially relevant if you're planning to change jobs soon as it ensures your growth plan will be relevant to the industry and not to the specific company you're about to leave. The closer you are to quitting, the higher the weight should be."

> "Now you have a great answer when asked about your weaknesses. You can demonstrate that you are not only aware of them but that, if you follow the framework in this book, you have a plan for improving them."

## Concepts

- [[Design Competencies]] — The chapter provides a structured approach to assessing where you stand on each competency: using external perspective, evaluating yourself honestly against competency levels, and recognizing that companies expect most (not all) competencies at a given level for promotion.
- [[Career Growth Plan]] — This chapter frames competency assessment as the foundation for a growth plan; the prioritization exercise helps you choose which competencies to develop and in what order.
- [[Performance Review]] — The chapter discusses 360-degree feedback, manager evaluation, and peer feedback as mechanisms for objective assessment of your competencies and avoiding self-bias.
- [[Promotion]] — Promotion readiness depends on demonstrating most competencies at the target level consistently over time; this chapter provides the self-assessment method to determine gaps.
- [[UX Career]] — Understanding competencies at each level and assessing yourself against them is foundational to planning a UX career progression, from entry-level through senior roles.
