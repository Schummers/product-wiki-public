# Remote Contextual Inquiry: Lessons Learned

## Summary

Contextual inquiry is a field research method that combines observation and semi-structured interviews to understand users' work practices and behaviors in their natural environments. While traditionally conducted in person, remote tools enable contextual inquiry in a modified form: the user remains in their natural context (desktop, home office) while the researcher observes via video call and screen sharing. This approach addresses traditional field study challenges (access, travel costs, safety) while maintaining many benefits of in-situ research, revealing natural distractions, workarounds, and interruptions.

Remote contextual inquiry works best for computer-based, desk-based knowledge work viewable on a single screen-shareable display. The method requires proper setup, including webcams, microphones, screen-sharing platforms, and large monitors. Ten lessons learned include: clearly communicate technical requirements upfront, stress interest in the user's natural environment to prevent artificial tidiness, practice technical setup with participants beforehand, adapt session length to 2-3 hours for virtual attention spans, turn off the researcher's camera during observation to aid participant flow, record sessions for deeper analysis and follow-up, watch for environmental cues (distractions, off-screen behavior), use active inquiry to probe points of interest, improvise with guided tasks if flow is difficult, and invite participants to follow-up sessions for longer workflows.

## Key Takeaways

- **Remote contextual inquiry is possible and reveals valuable in-situ insights** — as long as the user is working in their typical context, researchers can observe remotely and capture natural distractions and workarounds.
- **Computer-based, desk-bound work is the best fit** — remote contextual inquiry requires work visible on a single screen-shared display with minimal physical movement; knowledge workers with computer-based tasks are ideal.
- **Set clear technical requirements and practice beforehand** — communicate screensharing needs, firewall restrictions, and recording permissions upfront; practice technical setup in a 10-minute video call before the session.
- **Limit sessions to 2-3 hours and plan multiple sessions if needed** — remote sessions require more attention than in-person sessions; break into observation and discussion for easier participant flow; multiple shorter sessions capture more workflows.
- **Use active inquiry during observation** — remote settings make it harder to recall details; probe on points of interest in real time; encourage thinking aloud but don't force it unnaturally.

## Quotes

> "Remote contextual inquiry almost sounds like an oxymoron. If, by definition, contextual inquiry takes place within the natural environment of the user, can you conduct a contextual inquiry session remotely, without actually be physically with the user? The short answer: Yes, and with great success!"

> "Stress your desire to witness the user's typical environment. Otherwise, well-meaning participants may try to move their work to a quiet, less-distracting place in an attempt to be helpful."

> "turning off the camera can help the user to slip into a better state of flow. Your constant video feed may be distracting, as users attempt to watch for feedback or read your facial expressions."
