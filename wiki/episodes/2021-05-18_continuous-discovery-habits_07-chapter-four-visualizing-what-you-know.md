# Continuous Discovery Habits: Chapter Four: Visualizing What You Know

## Summary

Part 2 of Continuous Discovery Habits begins with a focus on discovering and structuring opportunities. This chapter teaches teams how to create experience maps—visual representations of what they currently know about their customers' experiences—before diving into customer interviews and opportunity mapping.

Drawing on cognitive science showing that external representations create shared understanding, the chapter argues that product trios should start by individually mapping their current knowledge using visual representations rather than words. This individual exercise prevents groupthink and surfaces the diverse perspectives each team member brings (the designer's view of customer confusion, the engineer's understanding of process interdependencies, the product manager's grasp of known pain points). The trio then merges individual perspectives into a shared experience map that becomes the starting point for discovery work. The map evolves continuously as the team learns more about customer needs, pain points, and desires.

## Key Takeaways

- **Start individually before merging perspectives** — Each product-trio member should develop their own experience map individually before combining perspectives, preventing groupthink and ensuring diverse knowledge is captured and surfaced.
- **Scope constrains what you capture** — Define the scope of your experience map using your desired outcome; narrow scope for optimization outcomes (e.g., "how do customers complete our application?"), broader scope for open-ended outcomes (e.g., "how do consumers entertain themselves with video?").
- **Draw, don't verbalize** — Use visual representations (boxes, arrows, stick figures) rather than words to create experience maps; drawing forces concrete thinking and reveals gaps that discussion might obscure.
- **Capture context at each step** — Map what customers are thinking, feeling, and doing; include where they get stuck, go wrong, course-correct, or abandon the process, not just the happy path.
- **Treat maps as evolving first drafts** — Experience maps contain hunches, not truth; they are starting points for customer interviews and will evolve week-to-week as the team learns more about customer context.

## Quotes

> "Whether actual or virtual, an external representation creates common ground…"

> "If we give each other time to explain ourselves using words and pictures, we build shared understanding."

> "Language is vague. It's easy for two people to *think* they are in agreement over the course of a conversation, but, still, each might walk away with a different perspective. Drawing is more specific."

## Concepts

- [[Experience Map]] — This chapter introduces experience maps as the foundational artifact: a visual representation of what teams currently know about how customers experience a process, revealing gaps in team understanding and serving as a guide for discovery interviews.
- [[Product Discovery]] — The systematic process begins with taking inventory of what teams already know before diving into customer discovery work; this chapter frames discovery as structured exploration starting from known unknowns.
- [[Product Trio]] — The chapter emphasizes that product trios (product manager, designer, engineer) bring different knowledge and must work individually first, then merge perspectives to avoid decisions made only by the loudest voice or most senior person.
- [[Continuous Interviewing]] — Experience maps guide the customer interviews that follow; they become starting points that evolve as teams continuously learn from customers, requiring ongoing refinement rather than one-time creation.
