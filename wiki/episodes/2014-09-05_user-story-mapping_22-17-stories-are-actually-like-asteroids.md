# User Story Mapping: 17. Stories Are Actually Like Asteroids

## Summary

This chapter uses the metaphor of Asteroids, which Patton calls an early video game and, later in the chapter, the old Atari game, to caution against breaking all stories down too early in the process. Patton argues that premature story decomposition fills the backlog with tiny stories that lose the big picture and become difficult to prioritize. Instead, teams should break stories progressively and just-in-time at four specific stages: opportunity discussion, discovery, release planning, and development cycle planning. The chapter also shows how to reassemble broken stories by bundling small items under a single header card, making backlogs more manageable.

## Key Takeaways

- **The Asteroids Metaphor: Break Stories Progressively, Not All at Once** — In the game, shooting all big asteroids early creates a screen full of small asteroids flying in different directions, causing quick defeat. Similarly, breaking all stories small too early fills the backlog with complexity and loss of context. Break stories progressively and just-in-time as you move through four stages: opportunities, discovery, release planning, and development cycle planning.

- **Four Stages of Story Breaking with Purpose** — (1) For opportunities: discuss who, what problems, and business alignment; split only bloated opportunities. (2) During discovery: discuss user needs and feasibility deeply; do most rock breaking here with learning in mind. (3) When planning releases: identify risks (user adoption, technical feasibility) and break stories to learn early. (4) During development cycle planning: finalize decisions and break stories to satisfy individual acceptance criteria.

- **Reassemble Small Stories to Manage Backlogs** — Backlogs filled with hundreds of tiny stories are unmanageable. Print story cards, get the team to physically cluster similar stories in silence, then write distilled headers that bundle them together. These new header cards become your working backlog, with original stories as supporting details.

- **Don't Overdo Story Mapping** — Map only what is needed to tell the story about your feature; do not create elaborate maps of the entire system. A good rule: if you don't need to discuss it, don't map it.

- **Don't Sweat the Small Stuff** — Small bugs and obvious improvements discovered after release do not require opportunity discussions or deep discovery. Get them into the current release backlog and workshop them as early as possible with the team. Patton's example is his friend Sherif's team at Atlassian, who mark a tick on the wall for every small fix so as not to let their users die "a death by a thousand paper cuts"; about 47 of them were headed into the next release.

## Quotes

> A really bad product backlog management strategy is to break down all the big stories so they're small enough to fit into the next development cycle. Your backlog will fill with lots of small stories flying every which way, and you'll die. Well, you won't actually die, but you'll be buried alive in a lot of needless complexity.

> If this were Asteroids, you'd have lost. But since it's not, try bundling up your small stories into bigger stories:

> We sometimes forget that another name for software development is knowledge work. When we forget that and instead fixate on documents and process, it turns into something dry and secretarial.

## Concepts

- [[Story Slicing]] — Stories should be broken progressively at four distinct stages (opportunity, discovery, release, cycle planning), each with different purposes. Breaking all stories small too early creates unmanageable backlogs; breaking on schedule keeps context and purpose aligned.

- [[Product Backlog]] — Large backlogs become unmanageable when filled with tiny stories. Patton recommends bundling small items under distilled headers, treating the bundled cards as the working backlog and original stories as supporting details, which makes prioritization and context easier.

- [[Prioritization]] — When a backlog is filled with hundreds of small stories, meaningful prioritization is impossible. Bundling stories and reassembling broken rocks makes prioritization conversations feasible and preserves the connection between small work items and larger goals.
