# Laws of UX: 3. Miller's Law

## Summary

Miller's Law states that the average person can retain approximately seven items (plus or minus two) in working memory. This principle emerged from George Miller's 1956 psychological research but is widely misunderstood in design practice. The chapter clarifies that Miller's work centered on chunking—grouping information into meaningful units—rather than imposing a rigid limit of seven items on interface elements. It establishes cognitive load theory as the real design value: organizing content to match how human brains process information.

## Key Takeaways

- **Working Memory Capacity** — The average person can hold approximately 7 ± 2 items in short-term memory, though this capacity varies per individual based on familiarity with content, context, and complexity. The chapter cites research putting the average limit closer to four items, and other theories that argue against measuring capacity as a fixed number of elements at all.

- **Chunking Over Count** — Miller's actual contribution was demonstrating that chunking (grouping related information) helps memorization more than the number of items. Formatting information into chunks aids memory; a formatted phone number is easier to process and memorize than the same digits in an unbroken string.

- **Cognitive Load Theory** — The amount of mental resources required to understand and interact with an interface. When incoming information exceeds working memory capacity, users struggle, miss details, and may abandon tasks. Chunking reduces cognitive load by organizing content into digestible groups.

- **Seven-Item Navigation Myth** — Navigation menus do not require memorization since choices remain visible. Nike.com's navigation exceeds seven items yet remains scannable through clear categorization, whitespace, and visual grouping—proving the "magical number seven" is not a design rule.

- **Design Chunking Methods** — Organize content using visual hierarchy, color, scale, dividers, and spacing to create distinct groups. This applies to dense information layouts, ecommerce product groupings, and toolbars in editing applications.

- **Information Hierarchy** — Effective chunking helps users scan, identify relevant information aligned with their goals, and process content faster. Wall-of-text content with no hierarchy is harder to parse than content structured with headings, sections, and whitespace.

## Quotes

> The average person can keep only 7 (± 2) items in their working memory.

> Miller's findings were centered around the limitations of short-term memory and how it can be optimized by organizing bits of information into meaningful chunks.

> Miller's law teaches us to use chunking to organize content into smaller clusters to help users process, understand, and memorize easily.

## Concepts

- [[Miller's Law]] — principle that working memory capacity is limited and optimized through chunking information into meaningful units; grouping related information, objects, and actions together using visual design to make content easier to comprehend and process; the temporary buffer space in human cognition for storing information relevant to the current task.
- [[Cognitive Load]] — the mental effort required to understand and interact with an interface; design should minimize unnecessary cognitive load.
- [[Information Architecture]] — the organization and structure of content, including hierarchy and relationships between information groups; using spacing, color, scale, headings, and dividers to visually separate and organize information into distinct, scannable sections.
