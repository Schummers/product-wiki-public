# Stop Shaming Your Users for Micro Conversions

## Summary

Manipulinks (also called confirmshaming) are deceptively worded rejection options in modals designed to make users feel bad for declining offers (e.g., "No thanks, I hate saving money"). While A/B tests may show increased micro conversions from this tactic, the short-term gain comes at significant long-term cost: disrespecting users damages brand perception, trust, and credibility. The article argues that deliberate hostile design violates basic customer respect and that honest, straightforward offers resonate better with users than manipulative tricks.

## Key Takeaways

- **Manipulinks deliberately cause negative emotions** — Rejection link text is phrased as undesirable self-descriptions to make users pause before declining; this is intentional hostile design, worse than unintentional poor UX.
- **A/B conversion gains are misleading** — Higher micro conversions from manipulinks may come less from cleverness than from dishonesty (not clarifying that email signup follows, for example).
- **Disrespect drives long-term losses** — Short-term newsletter signup increases come at the expense of negative brand perception, lower NPS scores, reduced credibility, and lost user trust.
- **Three levels of bad emotional design** — Mistakes (unthinking design), degradation (pleasant becoming unpleasant through repetition), and deliberate hostility (intentional harm); manipulinks are the worst category.
- **Honest offers outperform manipulation** — User research shows preference for direct, polite modals (X button, "No thanks" option) over condescending tones and clever rejection language.
- **Rudeness in writing is still rudeness** — If a behavior would be rude in person (a waiter asking if you prefer bland food), it remains rude in digital copy.
- **Users aren't stupid** — Treating users like they can be tricked or shamed into decisions offends them and demonstrates the company views them as adversaries rather than customers.

## Quotes

> Manipulinks represent a new spin on an old pattern. For years, traditional popup windows have been using large eye-catching acceptance buttons meant to lead users toward opting into whatever was offered. However, these old-school popups were a little more polite when it came to letting users opt out.

> Your users aren't stupid, and if you treat them like they are, they'll be offended.

> If it's rude to say it in person, it's rude to say it in copy.
