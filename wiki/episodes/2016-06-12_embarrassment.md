# Computer-Assisted Embarrassment

## Summary

As computing becomes ubiquitous in social contexts, systems fail not only in isolation but in front of others, embarrassing their users. Computer-assisted embarrassment occurs when system flaws and faulty interactions reflect badly on their users and embarrass them in social situations. This represents an expansion of UX concerns beyond the isolated user to include social users (interacting with systems in presence of others) and communities of users. Failures include communication disasters, privacy leaks, terrible timing, aggressive helpfulness, one-size-fits-all alerts, and systems excluding entire demographics. Research with diverse populations and in realistic contexts (field studies, diary studies over time) is essential to prevent social defects that damage user dignity and company reputation.

## Key Takeaways

- **System harm extends to social context** — Systems should not harm users in any way, including embarrassing them or making them feel bad in front of others; ubiquitous devices expose users to computer-assisted embarrassment in previously safe contexts.
- **Communication disasters** — Messaging systems easily include unintended recipients or send from wrong addresses; group messages intended as private can broadcast to entire groups causing embarrassment for everyone.
- **Privacy leaks and default problems** — Systems expose, share, or fail to delete private information; wireless mics/cameras thought to be off may transmit private moments; default settings can cause unintended public exposure.
- **Timing failures** — Alarms/appointments may default to wrong times (2am instead of 2pm); unwanted sound effects intrude into recordings and meetings; personal messages pop up during formal presentations; autocorrect causes public embarrassment.
- **Algorithmic cruelty** — Algorithms not specific enough can cause severe embarrassment; example: LinkedIn combined a Jewish socialist's photo with news of a white supremacist in automated email to wrong guy's network because they shared a name.
- **Exclusion-based embarrassment** — Systems designed for only one demographic (gender, ethnicity, ability) fail for others; image search not recognizing black people, voice recognition not supporting dialects, devices requiring two hands all exclude and embarrass non-target users.
- **Context determines appropriateness** — Same systems require different behaviors in different contexts (dinner, offices, church services, sporting events); systems without contextual awareness cause social awkwardness through mismatched behaviors.
- **Testing must include social contexts** — Lab testing with one user at a time misses social defects; field studies, diary studies over time, and testing with groups and in realistic contexts reveal problems invisible in isolation.

## Quotes

> Computer systems should not harm humans in any way — and that includes not embarrassing people or making them feel bad.

> By making smart devices ubiquitous, we've exposed ourselves to computer-assisted embarrassment.

> Mapping out unintended and potentially harmful user consequences (and how to avoid and mitigate them) should be part of every system's design plan.
