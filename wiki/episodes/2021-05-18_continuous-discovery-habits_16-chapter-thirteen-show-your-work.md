# Continuous Discovery Habits: Chapter Thirteen: Show Your Work

## Summary

Part 2 of *Continuous Discovery Habits*, this chapter addresses the critical challenge of bringing stakeholders along on the discovery journey. Torres uses the Airship case study—where a product team discovered that their competitors' journey builders were too complex and prototyped a better solution, only to face sales resistance—to illustrate why presenting conclusions alone (roadmaps, backlogs) fails to gain buy-in. The chapter teaches how using visual artifacts (opportunity solution trees, experience maps, story maps) to show the reasoning behind decisions leads to co-creation rather than opinion battles, particularly important for avoiding the HiPPO (Highest Paid Person's Opinion) syndrome.

## Key Takeaways

- **Show reasoning, not conclusions** — presenting roadmaps and backlogs (conclusions) invites stakeholders to share their own opinions; showing the discovery journey (opportunity mapping, interview insights, assumption testing) invites them to evaluate your thinking.
- **Start at the top of the opportunity solution tree** — when meeting with stakeholders, begin by confirming the desired outcome, then walk through how you mapped the opportunity space, which opportunities you prioritized and why, before ever mentioning solutions.
- **Tailor detail to the stakeholder** — your boss might want week-over-week discovery details; your CEO needs only outcome, top two or three opportunities, the chosen one, and one or two supporting tests.
- **Treat solutions as diverse ideas to evaluate, not preferences to defend** — when stakeholders suggest ideas, rather than shooting them down, use story mapping and assumption listing to help them reach their own conclusions about whether the idea supports your target opportunity.
- **Avoid ideological battles** — if asked to deviate from discovery process, don't argue the philosophy; instead focus on the decision at hand and look for future opportunities to show the benefit of the approach.
- **Continuously manage stakeholders** — sharing work along the way (week to week or month to month, depending on stakeholder) rather than only at the end builds alignment and prevents surprises.

## Quotes

> The more leaders can understand where teams are, the more they will step back and let teams execute.

> When we show our work, we are inviting our stakeholders to co-create with us. Instead of sharing our conclusions and inviting them to share their preferences, we are sharing our work and inviting them to assess our thinking and to add their own.

> If your stakeholders are more senior to you, odds are their opinion is going to win. This is why we have the dreaded HiPPO acronym (the Highest Paid Person's Opinions) and the saying "The HiPPO always wins."

## Concepts

- [[Stakeholder Engagement]] — Bringing stakeholders along is critical to success; experience maps, story maps, and opportunity trees serve as language for discussing customer needs and solution trade-offs without anchoring to solution preferences. Rather than presenting conclusions (which invites opinion battles), showing work invites stakeholders to co-create and avoid the hierarchy trap, ensuring good discovery does not go unused.
- [[Decision Making]] — showing the decision tree (outcome, opportunities, prioritization, solutions considered) rather than the final decision creates a collaborative evaluation process.
- [[Opportunity Solution Tree]] — using the same visual artifact both for your own discovery work and for communicating with stakeholders ensures alignment and reduces opinion-based disagreements.
