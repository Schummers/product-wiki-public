# Laws of UX: 1. Jakob's Law

## Summary

Jakob's Law states that users spend most of their time on other sites and prefer new sites to work like the ones they already know. This chapter explains how familiarity reduces cognitive load by allowing users to transfer mental models from one product to another, letting them focus on their goals rather than learning new interface patterns. The chapter explores the psychological concept of mental models—internal representations of how systems work—and demonstrates how designers can leverage existing mental models through familiar conventions in navigation, layout, and interaction patterns. The chapter includes examples ranging from Snapchat's failed 2018 redesign, which violated user expectations, to Google's gradual redesign approach and ecommerce sites like Etsy that maintain familiar purchasing patterns. Yablonski stresses that Jakob's Law does not mandate that all designs be identical, but rather that designers should begin with common patterns and depart from them only when innovation clearly improves the core user experience.

## Key Takeaways

- **Mental models transfer between products** — Users develop expectations from their cumulative experience with other products and apply those mental models to new interfaces; when designs align with these existing models, users can immediately be productive.
- **Cognitive load reduction through familiarity** — The less mental energy users spend learning an interface, the more they can dedicate to achieving their objectives; eliminating extraneous friction by following familiar conventions achieves this.
- **Conventions as mental model alignment** — Designers can reduce friction by leveraging common design patterns in page structure, workflows, navigation, and element placement; this allows users to focus on content and goals rather than how the interface works.
- **Mismatch consequences demonstrated by Snapchat 2018** — Snapchat's major redesign that combined stories and messaging in unfamiliar ways caused massive user backlash, migration to competitors, dropped ad revenue, and shrinking user count—a direct result of failing to align with users' established mental models.
- **Graduated change preserves mental models** — Google's approach of allowing users to opt into redesigned versions of products (YouTube, Gmail, Calendar) let users acclimate gradually, reducing the mental model mismatch that would otherwise cause rejection.
- **Innovation requires rigorous testing** — While breaking conventions can differentiate products, designers must first weigh user needs, context and technical constraints, be able to make a compelling argument that the departure improves the core user experience, and then test with actual users to ensure the novel approach is understood.

## Quotes

> Users will transfer expectations they have built around one familiar product to another that appears similar.

> Familiarity helps the people interacting with a digital product or service know immediately how to use it, from interacting with the navigation to finding the content they need to processing the layout and visual cues on the page in order to understand the choices available to them.

> Jakob's law isn't advocating for sameness in the sense that every product and experience should be identical. Instead, it is a guiding principle that reminds designers that people leverage previous experience to help them in understanding new experiences.

## Concepts

- [[Jakob's Law]] — The principle that users expect new products to work like the familiar ones they already know; users spend most time on other sites and transfer those expectations to new interfaces, preferring design consistency that leverages their existing mental models.
- [[Mental Model]] — A user's internal representation of how a system works, built through experience with similar systems; designers can improve user experience by aligning their designs with these mental models so users can apply previous knowledge without needing to learn new interaction patterns.
- [[Design Consistency]] — The practice of maintaining familiar patterns and conventions across interfaces in areas such as page structure, workflows, navigation, and element placement; consistency allows users to apply knowledge from previous experiences and reduces cognitive load.
- [[User Expectations]] — The anticipated behaviors and layouts users develop from cumulative experience with other products; when designs violate these expectations through unfamiliar conventions, users become confused and frustrated, as demonstrated by Snapchat's 2018 redesign failure.
- [[User Friction]] — The mental effort and time required for users to accomplish tasks; designers reduce extraneous friction by following familiar conventions, allowing users to stay focused on their goals rather than learning how the interface works.
