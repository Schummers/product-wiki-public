# Footers 101: Design Patterns and When to Use Each

## Summary

This article examines footer design patterns and their role in supporting user tasks despite receiving less attention than above-the-fold content. The article identifies multiple footer functions including utility links, doormat navigation, secondary task navigation, site maps, testimonials, brand information, and customer engagement. The research shows users intentionally scroll to footers to find specific content and use footers as a safety net when they cannot find information in main navigation.

## Key Takeaways

- **Users intentionally visit footers** — Footers serve as either a second chance to convince users or a last resort for hard-to-find content; users may also use footers for navigation rather than scrolling back to top.
- **Always include utility links** — Contact information, customer service, privacy policy, and terms of use should appear in every footer, as users expect to find them there.
- **Use doormat navigation for long pages** — Repeating global navigation in the footer helps users navigate between sections without scrolling back to the top, especially on long pages.
- **Avoid excessive information hierarchy** — Footers should display only first and second-level categories, not entire site maps; if everything is important, nothing is important.
- **Use clear, conventional link names** — Footers should use direct terms like "Contact Us" rather than vague labels like "Resources" or "Help" that don't clearly communicate what information users will find.
- **Make footers visible and readable** — Use legible font sizes and adequate contrast; avoid hiding footers with animations or accordions that users expect to be visible by default.

## Quotes

> A footer is the place users go when users they're lost. If we want to leave a lasting good impression, it's critical that we don't neglect the bottom of the page.

> Thus, except for overly bloated footers that could delay page download or rendering, a footer is a cost-free addition to the user experience. It can help, but it can't hurt.

> After all, even the most mundane, utilitarian sections of an interface can make the biggest impact on a user's experience.
