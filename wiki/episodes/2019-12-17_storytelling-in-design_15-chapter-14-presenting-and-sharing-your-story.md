# Storytelling in Design: Chapter 14. Presenting and Sharing Your Story

## Summary

Chapter 14 focuses on how to present design work effectively by using storytelling techniques. Dahlström argues that storytelling is not separate from presenting—telling something is storytelling—and that it enables you to create an experience for your audience (team members, stakeholders, clients) rather than just presenting facts and rationale. The chapter covers four main applications of storytelling in work presentations: achieving a desired outcome through purposeful stories with clear calls to action, getting buy-in from team members and stakeholders, inspiring others through the "pull of possibility" that Dahlström builds on Nancy Duarte's analysis of great talks, including Duarte's readings of Martin Luther King Jr. and of Steve Jobs, and telling compelling stories behind data through visualization and KPIs.

Dahlström then presents five practical steps adapted from traditional storytelling: identifying the "who" (audience) and "why" (desired outcome) of your presentation, defining your story with a clear narrative structure and plot points, delivering verbally with attention to emotional response and pacing, presenting visually with information hierarchy and clarity, and adapting both what and how you present based on your audience's knowledge and priorities. Throughout, the chapter emphasizes that being a storyteller requires understanding your audience, listening, and being able to adapt in real time—treating presentations themselves as experiences to be designed.

## Key Takeaways

- **Purposeful storytelling with clear call to action** — stories in work presentations need a specific desired outcome, and the audience must understand what action you want them to take, whether it is buy-in, approval, or a decision.

- **Understanding why buy-in is lacking** — when presenting to stakeholders or team members, diagnose whether resistance stems from lack of understanding the value, preference for other disciplines, or budget constraints, then tailor your story accordingly.

- **Structuring presentations with narrative elements** — sketch a clear outline with plot points and desired emotional response for each section; less is usually more in terms of slides and pages.

- **Data storytelling requires context to avoid vanity metrics** — quantitative and qualitative data are both valuable, but single metrics without context can mislead; set KPIs tied to actual experience goals and visualize data to aid understanding.

- **Adapting presentation to audience knowledge and priorities** — deliver different levels of detail and emphasis based on what the audience already knows, what they care about, and what will make them look good to their own stakeholders.

## Concepts

- [[Storytelling]] — this chapter applies storytelling specifically to presenting work and achieving desired outcomes with an audience; Dahlström covers storytelling as a tool for getting buy-in, inspiration, and moving audiences emotionally, distinct from its role in product design itself.

- [[Design Presentation]] — this chapter is entirely focused on how to present design work effectively using storytelling techniques—the verbal, visual, and narrative elements, understanding your audience, and adapting your approach.

- [[Narrative Structure]] — Dahlström reuses the methods the book applied to product experiences (the two-page synopsis and the index card method from Chapter 5) to structure a presentation, sketching an outline with plot points and a final act where everything comes together, and mapping a desired emotional response onto it as in Chapter 9.

- [[Stakeholder Engagement]] — the chapter extensively covers how to get buy-in from internal stakeholders and clients by understanding why buy-in is lacking, identifying who has decision-making power, understanding what matters to them, and adapting your story to resonate with their priorities.

- [[Experience Shape]] — Dahlström emphasizes designing the emotional arc of a presentation, mapping desired emotional response (low, medium, high) to each slide or section to create an engaging experience that guides the audience through the narrative.
