# Laws of UX: 8. Von Restorff Effect

## Summary

Chapter 8 explores the von Restorff effect, named after German psychiatrist Hedwig von Restorff's 1933 study, which found that distinctly different items from a set of similar ones are more memorable. In digital design, this principle manifests as the power of visual contrast to direct user attention toward key information and actions. However, the chapter emphasizes that contrast, while powerful, must be used with restraint to avoid overwhelming users, creating banner blindness, or excluding those with visual impairments or motion sensitivities.

## Key Takeaways

- **Isolation Improves Memory** — Von Restorff's 1933 study showed participants best remembered categorically similar items that were distinctly different. This principle extends to digital interfaces where visual isolation improves recognition and recall.

- **Selective Attention Shapes Perception** — Humans filter vast sensory information through selective attention, a cognitive capacity with limited resources. Banner blindness exemplifies this: users ignore elements they perceive as advertisements, and even legitimate content may be ignored if it remotely resembles an ad or sits close to one.

- **Multiple Contrast Tools** — Visual contrast can be created through color, shape, size, position, and motion. Examples include Material Design's floating action button (FAB), Notion's pricing table emphasis on "Plus" plan through color and shape, and notification badges.

- **Change Blindness Risk** — Users fail to notice significant changes when visual cues are weak or attention is focused elsewhere. If a change requires user awareness, designers must intentionally draw attention through appropriate contrast.

- **Color Contrast Standards** — WCAG requires at least 4.5:1 color contrast ratio between text and background for standard text, and 3:1 for larger (18pt+) or bold (14pt+) text. People with color vision deficiency or low vision need sufficient contrast.

- **Restraint and Accessibility** — Too much contrast creates visual noise, diluting emphasis and potentially causing users to tune out important information. Motion-based contrast must consider vestibular disorders; color-based contrast must not be the only differentiation for color-blind users.

## Quotes

> When multiple similar objects are present, the one that differs from the rest is most likely to be remembered.

> Our ability to focus on the things around us is limited in terms of capacity and duration, so we focus on relevant information to the detriment of nonrelevant information.

> The only thing worse than no contrast is way too much of it, which not only can dilute the power of the elements or content that you intended to stand out but can also visually overwhelm people.

## Concepts

- [[Von Restorff Effect]] — The principle that distinctly different items from a similar set are more memorable, applied in digital design through visual contrast to guide attention to key information and actions.

- [[Cognitive Psychology]] — Limited cognitive capacity that enables users to filter vast sensory information and focus on relevant details while ignoring distractions; drives phenomena like banner blindness and change blindness.

- [[Visual Design]] — Strategic use of contrast through color, shape, size, position, and motion to create visual hierarchy and direct user focus toward important elements and actions.

- [[Visual Hierarchy]] — Visual differentiation achieved through multiple properties; powerful when used sparingly but problematic when overused, creating noise, banner-blindness effects, or user overwhelm.

- [[Accessibility]] — Inclusive design requires contrast beyond color alone (patterns, strokes, shape), sufficient color contrast ratios (4.5:1 for text), and careful motion use to avoid excluding users with visual impairments or vestibular disorders.

- [[Eye Tracking]] — A research technique measuring where users look and how they interact with interfaces, providing objective data about where users look, how they navigate, what they ignore, and their emotional responses; the chapter recommends pairing it with other research methods because of its limitations.
