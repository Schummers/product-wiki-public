# Writing Tasks for Quantitative and Qualitative Usability Studies

## Summary

This article explains how to write effective test tasks, emphasizing that task requirements differ dramatically between quantitative and qualitative research. Quantitative studies seek rigor and metrics (time, success/failure) through tightly controlled conditions; qualitative studies seek insights and understanding through flexible exploration. Quantitative tasks must have one single interpretation, one success criterion, and enough specificity that all participants do essentially the same thing; ambiguity and open-endedness are design failures in quant studies. Qualitative tasks can be open-ended and flexible, evolving mid-study if better understanding emerges; flexibility is a feature, not a failure. Both require realistic tasks based on user research, avoidance of priming language, emotional neutrality, and pilot testing. The article provides specific examples of bad and better tasks for each methodology, demonstrating how phrasing choices affect whether results will be reliable.

## Key Takeaways

- **Quantitative Tasks Require Specificity** — Quant tasks must be narrow, focused, and specific enough that all participants interpret them identically; ambiguity causes variability in what participants actually do, making data meaningless.
- **Qualitative Tasks Allow Flexibility** — Qual tasks can be open-ended, and changing them mid-study is acceptable if better understanding emerges; flexibility to explore unexpected insights is appropriate and valuable.
- **One Success Criterion Per Task** — Quant studies need clear, singular endpoints to measure success and time-on-task; multiple success criteria cause ambiguity about whether partial success counts, contaminating metrics.
- **Avoid Priming and Clues** — Both quant and qual tasks should avoid providing hints about solutions; using exact UI labels primes participants to search for those terms rather than discovering information naturally.
- **Realistic Tasks Based on Research** — Tasks should reflect what actual users do with the product; inspiration comes from user interviews, search logs, analytics, and customer-service calls, not from designer assumptions.
- **Pilot Testing Is Critical** — Testing task wording with 1-2 representative users before the main study is essential; pilot testing often reveals ambiguities, missing details, or unrealistic scenarios that formal testing would have missed.
- **Quantitative Tasks Require Dummy Data** — Providing fake login credentials ensures all participants enter identical information, minimizing variability and preventing hesitation from users unwilling to share personal data.

## Quotes

> "a task that would be perfect for a qualitative study wouldn't work at all for a quantitative study, and vice versa."

> "Tasks must be as realistic as possible. You don't want to force participants to do something they wouldn't do in real life, or you'll measure something that's irrelevant to your project."

> "It's important that each participant is doing the exact same task in quantitative studies."

## Concepts

- [[Usability Testing]] — Research method where users attempt tasks on an interface; requires different task design depending on whether the goal is to collect metrics (quant) or understand user thinking (qual).
- [[Task Design]] — The craft of creating instructions for what users should do in research; requires understanding research goals and methodologies; mistakes in task design undermine research validity.
- [[Quantitative Research]] — Research that measures and compares variables to establish statistical patterns; requires controlled conditions, large samples, and specificity to ensure data reliability.
- [[Qualitative Research]] — Research that explores how and why things happen; values depth of understanding over breadth; flexible enough to pursue unexpected insights mid-study.
- [[Research Methods]] — The systematic approach and methods used to gather and analyze data; choice of methodology affects every aspect of task design, from specificity to flexibility.
