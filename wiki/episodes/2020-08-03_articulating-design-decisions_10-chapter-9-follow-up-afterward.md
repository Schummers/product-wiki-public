# Articulating Design Decisions: Chapter 9. Follow Up Afterward

## Summary

Chapter 9 focuses on the critical work that happens after a design review meeting ends. Greever argues that the time immediately following a meeting is nearly as important as the meeting itself, and that the designer's role extends well beyond the conference room. The chapter covers strategies for debriefing with stakeholders, documenting decisions clearly, filtering feedback appropriately, and making decisive calls when ambiguity remains.

## Key Takeaways

- **The meeting after the meeting** — The most productive conversations often happen in the hallway after the formal meeting ends, when people are willing to share unpopular opinions or offer support in ways they wouldn't in the group setting.
- **Follow up quickly** — Sending a thorough written follow-up within an hour or at most within a day creates a record, demonstrates urgency and respect, and prevents people from changing their minds about decisions already made.
- **Filter judiciously** — Not every comment in a meeting deserves to be documented or actioned; use judgment to assess whether feedback aligns with project objectives and whether the speaker is influential or likely to raise it again.
- **Seek one-on-ones** — Private conversations with key stakeholders and influencers after the meeting provide opportunities to gather additional insights, build coalition support, and connect with people across departments.
- **Make decisions when ambiguous** — When a meeting ends without clear resolution, the designer should make an explicit decision themselves and communicate it, which often prompts stakeholders to speak up with real opinions rather than remaining silent.
- **Create visibility and momentum** — Follow-up notes that recap decisions, assign specific tasks with names and dates, and outline next steps demonstrate decisiveness and keep the project moving forward.

## Quotes

> As soon as possible (preferably within an hour or at least within a day) send a follow-up to the entire team. It doesn't need to be written perfectly; the purpose is more functional than poetic.

> This time immediately following the meeting is your best defense against making a decision that could ultimately spell disaster for the user experience of the project.

> It's better to do something (even if it's wrong) and give your team the opportunity to speak out for or against your choice rather than deal with stale decisions and a stagnant design process.

## Concepts

- [[Stakeholder Engagement]] — the chapter emphasizes that designers must actively manage the period immediately after a meeting, including informal conversations and written documentation, to capture decisions and maintain momentum; one-on-one conversations with key stakeholders after the meeting reveal hidden support and concerns, and allow the designer to build coalitions for future decisions.
- [[Design Rationale]] — written follow-up notes serve as a record of reasoning, allowing the designer to avoid revisiting settled decisions and to reference the logic when changes are later questioned.
- [[Team Collaboration]] — assigning specific tasks to team members in the follow-up (including non-designers) distributes work and makes visible who owns what.
- [[Active Listening]] — filtering feedback requires the designer to assess which comments reflect genuine stakeholder needs versus casual brainstorming or tangential opinions, informed by understanding stakeholder intent and influence.
