# Articulating Design Decisions: Chapter 7. Choose a Message

## Summary

Chapter 7 catalogs common, reusable messages that justify design decisions across different types of projects and stakeholder concerns. Rather than reinventing justifications each time, Greever organizes responses into four categories—Business, Design, Research, and Limitations—providing templates and examples for each. These messages serve as pre-built responses that designers can adapt to their specific context, reducing the cognitive load of articulating decisions on the fly and ensuring consistency across meetings.

## Key Takeaways

- **Four message categories** — Business (goal, use case, branding), Design (common pattern, attention, flow), Research (data, testing, external research), and Limitations (resources, technology, standards) cover most design justifications; having templates for each reduces decision fatigue.
- **Connect to business goals** — "helps achieve a goal" is one of the most persuasive messages; frame design decisions as one step in a larger strategy using the pattern "[design] will affect [goal] because [reason]."
- **Use data, but carefully** — existing data helps make informed guesses, while reflective data (before/after metrics) proves effectiveness; but data interpretation involves assumptions, so avoid overinterpreting and always show your reasoning.
- **Stories beat statistics** — when research is used to justify decisions, user testing stories with real observations and video clips are often more compelling than spreadsheets; they create empathy and demonstrate that users actually informed the work.
- **Pattern libraries save time** — using established design patterns is both cheaper and easier to defend than custom solutions; help stakeholders understand that consistency in patterns sets user expectations and reduces implementation cost.
- **Know your limits upfront** — resource, technology, and standards constraints often emerge during implementation; naming these explicitly and early prevents frustration and helps stakeholders understand trade-offs rather than feeling surprised by cutbacks.

## Quotes

> "Using data to support your design decisions is the golden ticket to getting support because it is the most scientific way of demonstrating that your designs are having the intended effect."

> "It's not easy, but the good news is that this effort pays off in huge ways, because almost everyone is convinced by data."

> "We can't always do what our clients want, simply because of other factors that are beyond our control or because we're trying to adhere to standards in design and programming."

## Concepts

- [[Design Rationale]] — the chapter's message categories provide strategic rationales for specific design moves; a designer must be able to articulate which category applies and use its language to justify their approach.
- [[Design Presentation]] — effective messaging requires choosing the right argument type for the audience; a business stakeholder may respond better to financial arguments, while a research-oriented one wants data or testing evidence.
- [[Storytelling]] — user testing observations, anecdotes, and video clips are powerful justification tools because they ground abstract design principles in human experience; stories are remembered and retold better than statistics.
- [[Documentation]] — maintaining a file of research references, quotes, and data enables designers to back up claims when asked and shows stakeholders that decisions are intentional, not arbitrary.
