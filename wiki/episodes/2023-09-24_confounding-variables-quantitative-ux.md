# Confounding Variables in Quantitative Studies

## Summary

Confounding variables are unmeasured variables that can unintentionally affect the outcome of a research study, potentially invalidating conclusions. This article explains what confounding variables are, how they arise, why they matter for UX research, and provides best practices for avoiding or controlling them. The author uses a realistic example of a within-subjects usability study where design testing was split between morning and afternoon sessions, introducing multiple confounding variables that could explain differences in task-completion times.

## Key Takeaways

- **Definition** — A confounding variable is an unmeasured variable that may unintentionally affect both independent variables (study conditions) and dependent variables (measured outcomes), causing unpredictable results.
- **Common Sources** — Age effects, seasonal effects, competitive market shifts, existing product experience, and preexisting opinions about products can all act as confounding variables if not controlled.
- **Impact on Internal Validity** — When a study has significant confounding variables, its conclusions may be wrong, leading to serious loss of time and money if organizations base decisions on faulty data.
- **Within-Subjects Design Solution** — Randomly assign or counterbalance the order in which participants encounter different conditions to reduce confounding effects from learning and fatigue.
- **Measurement and Control** — Carefully measure potential confounding variables so they can be controlled for later in statistical analysis, or deliberately structure studies to avoid them.
- **When to Use Qualitative Instead** — If confounding variables are too difficult to control in a quantitative study, consider conducting qualitative research instead for exploratory work.

## Concepts

- [[Research Methods]] — Proper study design requires identifying and controlling for confounding variables to ensure conclusions are valid and reliable.
- [[Quantitative Research]] — Confounding variables can systematically bias quantitative study results and invalidate the internal validity of findings.
- [[Study Design]] — Randomization, counterbalancing, and consistent testing environments are essential techniques to mitigate confounding variable effects.
