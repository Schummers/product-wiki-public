# Anchors OK? Re-Assessing In-Page Links

## Summary

In-page links (anchor or jump links) lead users to content on the same page rather than to another page. Historically discouraged due to violating the mental model of links as page navigators, they have become more valuable with responsive design and small screens where content expands across multiple scrolls. The article reassesses when in-page links are beneficial, providing guidelines for responsible implementation including content length assessment, clear headings, explicit labeling, heading confirmation on jump, and strategic sticky placement.

## Key Takeaways

- **In-page links violate mental models** — Users expect links to lead to other pages; jumping within the same page disorients users, creating the core usability concern.
- **Mobile screens increase in-page link value** — As screen size decreases, content expands vertically; in-page links become more valuable for helping users navigate longer scrolling pages without excessive effort.
- **Use for meaningful content only** — In-page links help when content is lengthy and sectioned; first consider if content could be shortened, reorganized, or split across pages instead.
- **Label in-page links explicitly** — Use visual indication or labels like "On this page" or "In this article" to differentiate from cross-page links; critical for screen-reader and screen-magnifier users.
- **Match link text to headings** — Ensure in-page link text matches the heading it targets to reassure users they've reached the correct content; acts as a signpost for orientation.
- **Scroll content near top** — When jumping to a section, scroll it close to the screen top for readability; show small whitespace at screen top to help users notice headings not obscured by sticky elements.
- **Consider sticky placement** — On larger screens, sticky sidebar navigation can remain visible as users scroll; helps when a static top table of contents quickly scrolls out of view.
- **Show current location** — If in-page links stay sticky while scrolling, indicate current section with visual markers to help users orient themselves on the page.

## Quotes

> they can help users find information, particularly on longer, scrolling, mobile pages

> before adding in-page links to make a longer piece of content more manageable, first consider if the content could be shortened, reorganized or rewritten. Thoughtful editing may pare down unnecessary details, shorten the content, and may make in-page links unnecessary.

> Headings are only helpful if the user sees them. In-page links help address that issue. They can appear at the top of page, acting as a table of contents to the page.
