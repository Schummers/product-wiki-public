# User Story Mapping: 3. Plan to Learn Faster

## Summary

Chapter 3 follows Eric, a product owner at Liquidnet, through the early stages of discovering and validating a new product opportunity. Rather than immediately building a full solution based on initial ideas, Eric applies a learning-driven strategy: he discusses the opportunity with leadership, validates that customers have the problem he's trying to solve, sketches prototypes to envision solutions without building production software, and then releases increasingly complete versions to development partners while measuring how they actually use the product.

Patton names this a validated learning strategy, drawn from Lean Startup thinking and Eric Ries's build-measure-learn loop, and borrows his definition of discovery from Marty Cagan. The core insight is that the real outcome a product owner seeks isn't a prototype or a minimal viable product—it's customers who choose to use the product every day. Eric's strategy reveals that the path to this outcome requires treating each release as an experiment designed to learn something specific, and building only what's necessary to learn it, before committing to larger bets.

## Key Takeaways

- **Start with opportunity framing** — Before creating a backlog, discuss the big idea with leadership and ensure shared understanding of who the customers are, who the users are, what problems the product solves, and why the organization is building it.

- **Validate the problem exists** — Talk directly to customers and users to confirm they actually have the problems being solved and are interested in a solution, rather than assuming the initial idea is correct.

- **Sketch and prototype without building software** — Use simple prototypes (wireframes, interactive mockups in tools like Axure) to envision solutions and test with users, because surprises and bad news at this stage are cheap to address.

- **Build less than minimal first** — Release a product that is less than viable to a small group of development partners who care about solving their problem, rather than attempting to build a complete solution upfront.

- **Measure real usage, not stated preferences** — Track metrics on how people actually use the product alongside qualitative feedback, because people may say they like something but never use it; actual usage is the real outcome to optimize for.

- **Release something usable at every step, per Henrik Kniberg** — Patton reproduces a visualization by his friend Henrik Kniberg contrasting a broken release strategy, where each release yields something unusable until the last, with one where each release (skateboard, bicycle, motorcycle) delivers something the person can actually use and the team can learn from.
- **Iterate until viable with development partners** — Gradually grow and improve the product with feedback until development partners start recommending it to others, signaling that minimum viable has been achieved and the product is safe to market broadly.

## Quotes

> Your first story discussion is for framing the opportunity.

> Validate that the problems you're solving really exist.

> Treat every release as an experiment and be mindful of what you want to learn.

## Concepts

- [[Validated Learning]] — Patton names what Eric the product owner did a validated learning strategy, one of the core concepts of Lean Startup thinking, and says it is the heart of the build-measure-learn loop described by Eric Ries; each release is a minimum viable product experiment designed to learn something specific.

- [[Product Discovery]] — Patton says he borrows his definition of discovery from Marty Cagan and widens it to include Lean Startup, Lean UX and Design Thinking practice; the goal is to learn as fast as possible whether you are building the right thing, by talking to customers, validating problems, prototyping solutions, and iterating with real users.

- [[Prototyping]] — High-fidelity prototypes in tools like Axure or PowerPoint are used to envision solutions before committing to building software.

- [[Minimum Viable Product]] — Reframed as "minimum viable product experiment" (MVPe): the smallest thing that can be built to learn something; Eric builds less than viable to start and iterates toward viability.

- [[Release Planning]] — The story map organizes releases as slices, with the top slice being current work, and lower slices representing future releases; each release has explicit learning goals.

- [[Assumption Testing]] — The strategy validates assumptions about customer problems, solution ideas, and what makes a product viable by releasing incrementally to development partners and measuring results.
