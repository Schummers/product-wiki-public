# Compensatory vs Noncompensatory: 2 Decision-Making Strategies

## Summary

This article explains two distinct decision-making strategies people use when choosing among alternatives. The number of options available is the primary factor determining which strategy users employ. Noncompensatory strategy is used when many alternatives exist, allowing rapid elimination of options that don't meet key criteria. Compensatory strategy is used with few alternatives, involving thorough comparison of pros and cons. The article argues interfaces should support both strategies at different stages of the decision process.

## Key Takeaways

- **Noncompensatory for many options** — When faced with many alternatives, users eliminate options that don't meet nonnegotiable criteria, rather than comprehensively comparing all alternatives, allowing quick narrowing of choices.
- **Compensatory for few options** — When evaluating 5-7 alternatives, users engage in thorough comparison of attributes, allowing positive aspects to outweigh negative ones in their decision.
- **Filters support noncompensatory strategy** — Product-listing pages should provide relevant filters and faceted navigation to help users eliminate options that don't match their key criteria.
- **Comparison tools support compensatory strategy** — When users have narrowed options to a small set, side-by-side comparison tables help them evaluate pros and cons and make optimal decisions.
- **Sequential strategy use** — Users often employ both strategies in sequence: first using filters to eliminate poor matches from many options, then using comparison tools to evaluate the remaining few.

## Quotes

> Noncompensatory decision making is used when there are **many alternatives** to choose from; it allows people to quickly narrow down the number of options to one or a few.

> Understanding how people make choices allows us to design tools that support users' decision-making strategies and increase usability.

> As illustrated in the earlier Lowes example, commonly people first narrow down a large set of alternatives using a noncompensatory strategy such as filtering, then, once they are left with a smaller number of results, they compare the negative and positive attributes of individual items.
