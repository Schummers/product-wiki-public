# Storytelling in Design: Chapter 6. Using Character Development in Product Design

## Summary

Chapter 6 translates character development from traditional storytelling to product design, arguing against the reluctance to create detailed personas. Dahlström advocates moving beyond demographic segmentation toward data-driven archetypes based on behavior and insight. She identifies the many "characters" and "actors" in a product experience: the protagonist user, other users (peers, contributors, influencers, promoters, detractors), friends/family, the system, the brand, bots and voice UIs, AI, devices, touch points and drivers, and antagonists (internal, external direct, and external indirect). The chapter distinguishes characters (personified players), actors (non-personified systems), and props (supporting elements). Drawing on approaches from screenwriting, literature, acting, animation, and game design, she presents character development methods: character hierarchy mapping, character questionnaires, and character arc definition using the goal-lie-truth framework, which Dahlström takes from a Reedsy guide to positive character arcs and notes has some resemblance to Pixar's wants versus needs. The chapter emphasizes that emotional connection, not surface traits, is what makes audiences care about characters.

## Key Takeaways

- **Personas and detailed user understanding remain essential, not superfluous** — Dahlström directly challenges the reluctance to invest in detailed personas, following UX researcher Meg Dickey-Kurdziolek's principle that being user-centered means deliberately seeking stories, data, and rationale behind users' motivations; research often reveals surprises that contradict assumed knowledge, and data-driven archetypes provide far more value than broad demographic classifications.

- **Many characters and actors play roles in product experiences beyond the primary user** — Identify other users, friends/family, the system, the brand, bots, AI, devices, touch points/drivers, and antagonists; each affects the experience narrative, and their importance varies by product type.

- **Characters are personified players that interact and influence; actors are non-personified systems; props are supporting elements** — A character should be present for scenes to work as intended; actors are systems the product depends on (e.g., payment gateways); props further the action but don't drive narrative.

- **Emotional connection, not surface traits, is what makes characters matter** — Drawing from screenwriter John Truby, what makes an audience care is the character's weakness (their deep personal problem) and their goal, not demographics or appearance; Martha Alderson notes that emotions are universal, relatable, and humanizing—they always tell the truth, while thoughts and dialogue can lie.

- **Character hierarchy clarifies role and importance** — Rank characters as main, supporting, subplot, or one-string to ensure narrative focus matches importance; similar patterns exist in successful movies by genre.

- **The Stanislavski method and "Magic If" build empathy and understanding** — Asking "What would I do if I found myself in this user's situation?" helps teams imagine users' thoughts, emotions, and reactions; assigning personas to team members ensures their needs are considered throughout design.

- **Character development explores wants, needs, obstacles, stakes, and arcs** — A character's want often drives surface entertainment (what they say they want) while their need drives emotional resonance (what they truly require); obstacles and stakes define the journey; a positive character arc shows transformation through goal-lie-truth (what they pursue, what misconception blocks them, what truth they discover), and Dahlström notes an arc can also be negative.

- **Character definition, development, and growth are ambiguous terms Dahlström fixes for this book** — She notes the three overlap in common usage and sets them as conventions for the rest of the book: definition identifies and initially describes characters; development creates depth and personality through research and tools like questionnaires; growth tracks transformation throughout the story.

## Quotes

Freelance UX researcher Meg Dickey-Kurdziolek, quoted by Dahlström:

> Being a user-centered designer means that you deliberately seek out the stories, data, and rationale behind your users' motivations.

Director and cinematographer Samo Zakkir, quoted by Dahlström:

> If you've done your homework, really enveloped yourself within the character iceberg, and you know your characters intimately, the rest is easy. The character tells you. All you have to do is listen.

> If you don't understand your users, you will never build great products.

## Concepts

- [[Character Development]] — The core topic; covers definition (identifying and initially describing), development (creating depth and personality), and growth (transformation throughout the story); includes character arcs (goal-lie-truth), wants versus needs, obstacles, stakes, and character hierarchy; applies tools from screenwriting (questionnaires), acting (Stanislavski method), literature, animation, and games.

- [[Persona]] — Foundational throughout the chapter; emphasizes data-driven personas as characters rather than demographic stereotypes; cites Alan Cooper, inventor of design personas, on looking at personas as individual users and digging deep into what matters to them; advocates detailed, research-backed personas with backstory, beliefs, motivations, weaknesses, and relationships to ensure users become the heroes of experiences.

- [[Empathy]] — Discussed as emotional connection to characters, achieved through research into context, culture, and motivation; the chapter cites IDEO's *Human-Centred Design Toolkit*, which defines empathy as a “deep understanding of the problems and realities of the people you are designing for,” and names empathy maps as one tool for building it; Stanislavski's "Magic If" helps teams imagine users' perspectives; deep understanding of users' problems, realities, and emotional states drives user-centered design.

- [[User Engagement]] — Indirectly: Dahlström argues that emotional connection to characters is what makes an audience care enough to keep watching or turning the page, and transposes that to caring about the people we design for rather than about features.

- [[Shared Understanding]] — Character hierarchy, detailed personas, and clear definitions of who plays what role ensure the team and client align on the product's narrative and the people being designed for.
