# Sneaking: The Deceptive UX Pattern You Never Saw Coming

## Summary

Connor Chan examines "sneaking," a category of deceptive design patterns that trick users into agreeing to things they did not intend. Sneaking encompasses three practices: forced continuity (tricking users into ongoing subscription payments), hidden costs (revealing fees only at checkout), and sneak into basket (automatically adding items without consent). The article documents real-world examples from Spotify, Ticketmaster, Airbnb, GoDaddy, and LinkedIn, contrasting them with ethical alternatives from companies like Apple and United Airlines. While sneaking may drive short-term business gains, it sacrifices transparency and damages long-term customer loyalty and trust.

## Key Takeaways

- **Forced continuity hides subscription obligations** — highlighting free trials while burying ongoing payment terms in small print, or delaying billing notifications, tricks users into forgetting they have active subscriptions.
- **Hidden costs exploit checkout psychology** — showing lower initial prices and revealing true costs only at checkout exploits users' limited attention and desire to minimize interaction cost, creating frustration and distrust.
- **Sneak into basket takes advantage of limited attention** — automatically adding items to carts or prefilling consent checkboxes assumes users won't notice or lack patience to find and deselect them.
- **Deceptive patterns damage brand loyalty** — while these patterns may increase immediate sales or subscriptions, they harm long-term customer relationships and brand reputation when users discover the deception.
- **Transparency builds trust** — companies that clearly communicate prices upfront and send regular notifications about subscriptions (like Apple) build stronger customer relationships than those using sneaking.
- **Regulation is increasing** — laws like GDPR prohibit prefilled checkboxes and require explicit user consent, establishing that ethical transparency is becoming a legal requirement, not just a best practice.

## Quotes

> Sneaking is a deceptive practice that tricks customers into agreeing to something they did not intend to.

> Sneaking exists because it can drive immediate business growth by increasing sales or subscriptions. However, it does so by misleading users, sacrificing transparency, and damaging long-term customer loyalty.

> To promote ethical design, default choices should be noncommittal. Designers can create a transparent and user-friendly experience by allowing people to make personal decisions.

## Concepts

- [[Deceptive Patterns]] — Interface designs and interactions deliberately crafted to mislead users into unintended actions through dark patterns like sneaking, forced continuity, and hidden costs; these deliberately exploit user behavior and cognitive biases to serve business interests at the expense of user interests.
- [[User Trust]] — the confidence and willingness of users to engage with a product or company, which is damaged by deceptive practices and built by transparency and ethical design.
