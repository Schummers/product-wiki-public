# Solving Product Design Exercises: How to present your solution

## Summary

Presentation is a critical component of design exercises because it must convey not just the final solution but all the thinking that led to it. Dashinsky provides distinct guidance for whiteboarding sessions versus take-home exercises. In whiteboarding, he recommends explicitly walking through the framework steps and encouraging the interviewer to participate, treating their input as helpful rather than a liability. For take-home exercises, he advises pairing a clickable prototype with a written presentation covering the task, problem definition, audience, context, selected idea, visual solutions, and success metrics. The chapter concludes with advanced tips on scope, blind spots, competitive analysis, and ecosystem integration for big tech companies.

## Key Takeaways

- **Whiteboarding: narrate the process** — State each step you're taking and write them on the board as a prompt; if stuck, explain your thinking aloud; interviewer input is normal and acceptable, not a sign of failure.
- **Take-home: pair prototype with explanation** — Deliver a clickable prototype alongside a written presentation that covers the problem, audience, context, chosen idea (with one or two sentence summary), visual decisions, and success metrics.
- **Show scope and constraints** — Explicitly state what your solution addresses and what it does not, plus what you would do if given more time; this demonstrates clear thinking within constraints.
- **Address assumptions and blind spots** — For critical assumptions, propose how you would validate them (quick user research, surveys, or testing services); this shows rigor and awareness of the limits of your solution.
- **Connect to competitive landscape** — Position your solution relative to competitors to show whether it creates differentiation or catches up; link to company ecosystem when interviewing with large tech firms (e.g., how a solution could integrate with other products or services).

## Quotes

> The presentation should include all of the thinking that led to the solution.

> If that's the case and you feel stuck on one step, your approach should be to explain the step.

> It doesn't automatically decrease your chances of getting hired.

## Concepts

- [[Design Exercise]] — The communication and persuasion component where candidates articulate their entire thought process, framework application, and design decisions to interviewers or hiring committees.
- [[Design Presentation]] — Techniques for presenting design work that emphasize the reasoning behind choices, including narration of process (whiteboarding), prototype plus explanation (take-home), and strategic framing of scope and competitive positioning.
- [[Portfolio]] — For take-home exercises, the presentation becomes a portfolio piece; successful presentation includes prototype, written explanation, scope definition, assumption validation approach, and competitive analysis.
- [[Problem Framing]] — Clearly stating the problem, target audience, and context at the start of a presentation ensures the solution is understood as a response to a specific, bounded challenge.
