# Continuous Discovery Habits: Chapter Nine: Identifying Hidden Assumptions

## Summary

Before testing ideas, teams must first surface and enumerate the hidden assumptions embedded in each solution. This chapter explains how confirmation bias and escalation of commitment can lead teams to invest heavily in the wrong ideas without realizing it, then teaches five methods for surfacing assumptions: story mapping (tracing each step a user must take), pre-mortems (imagining failure to uncover risks), walking the opportunity solution tree backward (connecting solutions to outcomes), exploring potential harm (ethical assumptions), and assumption mapping (prioritizing which assumptions are most risky). The chapter emphasizes that assumptions fall into five categories—desirability, viability, feasibility, usability, and ethical—and that teams typically have blind spots in some categories. Assumption mapping, developed by David Bland, plots each assumption on two axes: how much evidence exists and how important it is to success. The highest-risk assumptions (top-right quadrant, high importance and weak evidence) are the "leap of faith" assumptions that must be tested first.

## Key Takeaways

- **Identify five types of assumptions: desirability, viability, feasibility, usability, ethical** — Every idea embeds dozens of assumptions across these categories; teams often have blind spots, particularly in ethical assumptions, so use multiple discovery methods to catch all categories.
- **Story map your ideas to surface hidden assumptions** — Map what users must do to get value from each solution, step by step, then generate assumptions for each step: What must be true for this step to work? What desirability, feasibility, and usability bets are we making?
- **Use pre-mortems and opportunity-tree analysis to uncover viability and ethical risks** — Imagine your solution fails and work backward to guess why; connect your solution back through opportunities to outcomes to expose viability assumptions; ask directly, "What harm could this cause?"
- **Assumption map to find your riskiest bets** — Plot each assumption by importance to success and evidence strength; test your "leap of faith" assumptions (top-right: high importance, weak evidence) first, in order of risk.
- **Prioritize testing, don't test everything** — Most assumptions will be harmless if your discovery work was solid; focus testing on the 2-3 riskiest assumptions per idea, not every one of dozens.

## Quotes

> "We loosely define an iteration in discovery as trying out at least one new idea or approach…To set your expectations, teams competent in modern discovery techniques can generally test on the order of 10–20 iterations per week."

> "Just assume that you are being overconfident, and give yourself a healthy margin of error."

> "Every product introduces some potential for harm. As product trios, we don't always need to mitigate every potential area for harm, but we should be aware of the impact our products and services are having and ask how we can reduce the potential harm."

## Concepts

- [[Assumption Mapping]] — A two-dimensional prioritization technique that plots assumptions by importance to solution success (y-axis) and strength of existing evidence (x-axis); the top-right quadrant identifies "leap of faith" assumptions with high importance and weak evidence that must be tested first.
- [[Assumption Testing]] — The practice of quickly testing critical assumptions through iterations rather than building full prototypes; teams test dozens of assumptions per week by systematically validating or refuting the beliefs embedded in each idea.
- [[Story Mapping]] — A technique for clarifying what an idea actually means by mapping the steps each user (or actor) must take to get value from the solution; story maps force teams to be specific and reveal dozens of hidden assumptions about what users will do, understand, and want.
