# Solving Product Design Exercises: 3.3. A dashboard for freelancers

## Summary

This chapter applies the seven-step design framework to a business management dashboard for freelancers. Dashinsky frames the problem as helping freelancers spend less time on administration so they can focus on billable work, then designs a dashboard that answers three core questions: "How is my business doing financially?", "How should I plan my future work?", and "What can I do now to move my business forward?" The solution uses a master-detail layout with sidebar navigation organized around the freelancer's business workflow cycle (proposal, contract, time tracking, expenses, invoicing, payment), supported by content areas displaying financial status, project timeline, and actionable next steps.

## Key Takeaways

- **The freelancer's core needs** — Freelancers need to know their financial health (outstanding payments, expenses, profit trends), see project status at a glance (where each project stands in the proposal-to-payment cycle), and get suggested next actions to unblock progress without hunting for what to do next.
- **Master-detail with workflow-aligned navigation** — Navigation follows the actual freelancer business cycle rather than generic categories: proposal, contract, time tracking, expenses, invoicing, and payment become primary navigation items, enabling quick access to any step across all clients and projects.
- **Financial status with trend comparison** — Display current month's key metrics (outstanding, paid, expenses) alongside comparison to prior month and projection for next month using a three-metric graph (Paid, Expenses, Profit), answering both "How am I doing?" and "Am I getting better?"
- **Timeline visualization for project status** — Place each project on a chronological timeline from proposal through payment so freelancers see at a glance where each project stands and what action is due next, replacing scattered status indicators.
- **Audience-specific guidance paths** — New freelancers need onboarding on business setup and first invoicing; experienced freelancers need import tools for prior data; retainer contractors (a small minority) have flat revenue and may not benefit from this design, and could receive specialized treatment later.
- **Mobile reorganization strategy** — Minimize navigation to a hamburger menu for discovery, but create a separate "Up Next" tab at the top to make action items visible and prominent, capturing the mobile user's attention with high-priority updates.

## Quotes

> The global workforce is becoming more independent, so the market of freelancers is going to grow constantly in the next few years. People dream about the independence and flexibility freelancing provides, but they don't always realise how much administration it requires.

> I believe that freelancers would need to access each of these aspects often across their clients or projects (e.g. to see all the payments or proposals issued recently). To make them easily accessible from anywhere in the app, I'd use them as part of primary navigation.

> Since these steps are chronological, we could place each project on a timeline which will provide an overview of where they stand and what the freelancer should do next.

## Concepts

- [[Design Exercise]] — The chapter walks through all seven steps of the design framework: understanding the goal of reducing admin burden, defining diverse freelancer personas, mapping context and needs, brainstorming web and mobile solutions, choosing web as the core, designing a master-detail dashboard, and defining completion and engagement metrics.
- [[Persona]] — Dashinsky segments freelancers by profession (designers, developers, writers, photographers, videographers, marketers), work arrangement (full-time, part-time, retainer), and experience level (new vs. existing), recognizing that new freelancers need guidance while experienced ones need efficiency.
- [[Problem Framing]] — Rather than asking "What features should a freelancer tool have?", the author frames the core problem as helping freelancers understand their business health and know what action to take next, derived from observing that administration is a burden that diverts time from billable work.
- [[UX Metrics]] — The chapter proposes "Up next" actions completion rate to validate that the dashboard successfully unblocks projects, and survey statements measuring perceived help with financial understanding, future planning, and forward momentum, tied to product-level metrics like project completion and retention.
- [[Product Thinking]] — Dashinsky recognizes that different freelancer segments need different approaches: new freelancers need guidance on setup, experienced ones need import and efficiency, and retainer contractors have fundamentally different needs that might require a separate product variant.
- [[Business Impact]] — Success is measured not only by completion rate and engagement, but by downstream effects: Does the product increase how much freelancers earn? Does it improve retention? Does it increase usage and NPS? These tie the dashboard design directly to business viability.
