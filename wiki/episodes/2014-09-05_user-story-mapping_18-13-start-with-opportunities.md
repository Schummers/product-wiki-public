# User Story Mapping: 13. Start with Opportunities

## Summary

This chapter opens the discovery phase of product development, introducing the concept of "opportunities" as the largest unit of work to be evaluated. Patton argues that before breaking stories into smaller pieces, product teams must first decide whether an opportunity is worth pursuing at all, using structured conversations to frame the business and user context. He would not use a story map to manage opportunities themselves, but recommends a high-level "now" map of the existing product, a form of journey map, as the backdrop against which opportunities are placed.

## Key Takeaways

- **Opportunity as the First Rock** — Stories are like rocks that can be broken into smaller parts, and the first unbroken rock is called an "opportunity." Opportunities represent ideas the team believes will solve a problem, and the goal of initial conversations is to decide whether to pursue them or discard them.

- **Structured Opportunity Conversations** — Teams should discuss five dimensions of an opportunity: who the solution is for (user types, target markets), what problems are being solved, what the product or feature might look like, why it benefits the organization, and initial size estimates comparing the opportunity to past work.

- **The Opportunity Canvas** — Patton says he used Marty Cagan's Opportunity Assessment template as a starting point and has come to prefer a canvas approach, of the kind Alexander Osterwalder and Yves Pigneur describe in *Business Model Generation*. The canvas provides a spatial organization of nine interconnected topics: problems/solutions, users and customers, how users solve problems today, user value, user metrics, adoption strategy, business problem, business metrics, and budget. It is a set of topics to discuss, not a form to fill out.

- **Opportunities Are Not Requirements** — Patton cautions teams not to treat roadmaps of "requirements" as mandates. Making go/no-go decisions on ideas requires shared understanding about problems and who benefits; if decision-makers are absent, include them in the conversation to validate assumptions before proceeding.

- **Story Maps Reveal Opportunity Hot Spots** — Creating a high-level map of the existing product and overlaying opportunities as colored sticky notes reveals patterns of need. Opportunities affecting high-frequency activities or critical user groups warrant earlier focus.

- **Two contributed sidebars on mapping the current journey** — Ben Crothers of Atlassian describes a multidisciplinary team mapping the end-to-end customer experience across more than ten products, adding pain points, opportunities and assumptions, then generating and dot-voting concepts on feasibility, viability and desirability, and turning the result into a 20-page comic. Erin Beierwaltes and Aaron White describe "rehearsal remapping": assigning personas to team members, giving them only a goal rather than steps, watching them act it out, then rebuilding the map from what they actually did and collecting pains and gains, in 2.5 hours.
- **Shared Understanding Drives Better Decisions** — The product owner makes the final go/no-go decision, but leveraging the team to inform that decision creates collective ownership and alignment that improves outcomes.

## Quotes

> I'm using opportunity for ideas that we believe will solve a problem. I'm not just a glass-half-full guy. It's just that it's a bad idea to consider every idea as something we need to include in our product, because you know that there isn't enough time and people to build all that stuff. And, even if you did have the time and people, your customers would be overwhelmed.

> A canvas organizes information spatially. It's big and all in one place so a group can see and work with it, which is hard to do in a slide or printed document, and the organization allows you to see dependencies. Information is adjacent to other information it depends on.

> Use this first big story conversation to frame the work you and your team may be getting into. Even though the answer to the go/no-go question may be "Go," make sure you leave the conversation with shared understanding about the problems you're solving, for whom, and how your organization benefits from building software.

## Concepts

- [[Product Discovery]] — Patton frames opportunity evaluation as the opening move in discovery, requiring structured conversations about user needs, business alignment, and feasibility before committing development resources; An opportunity is the largest unbroken story, representing a proposed solution to a problem; Patton introduces frameworks (the Opportunity Canvas) to evaluate opportunities across nine dimensions running from the user's problems and value through to the business problem, business metrics and the budget.

- [[Stakeholder Engagement]] — Go/no-go decisions on opportunities must include those who can make them; if decision-makers are unavailable, teams should articulate assumptions and invite correction rather than proceeding alone.
