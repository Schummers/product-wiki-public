# The Picture-Superiority Effect: Harness the Power of Visuals

## Summary

The picture-superiority effect refers to the cognitive phenomenon that people remember pictures better than words. According to psychologist Allan Paivio's dual-coding theory, images are stored in memory in two ways (as both image and word description) while words are stored only one way, making images more memorable and cognitively efficient. The article explains factors affecting this effect (discoverability, clarity, familiarity, uniqueness) and provides three strategies for leveraging it: placing visuals where users spend time and above the fold, choosing literal images over abstract ones, and picking unique imagery distinct from competitors and surrounding elements. Text remains essential for usability despite images' mnemonic advantages.

## Key Takeaways

- **Dual-coding theory** — images are memorable because the mind stores both the visual and a word/phrase describing it, while words only store the word itself; this dual representation increases memorability.
- **Affecting factors** — picture-superiority strength depends on discoverability (visibility duration), clarity (concrete vs. abstract), familiarity (recognizability), and uniqueness (distinctiveness from surrounding visuals).
- **Placement strategy** — place important visuals where users spend time (homepages receive most views); place information-carrying visuals above the page fold; ensure visuals persist rather than auto-rotating.
- **Literal over abstract** — choose realistic, literal images over abstract ones; abstract visuals require cognitive effort to interpret and remember less effectively.
- **Unique imagery** — images differing from surrounding visuals are noticed and remembered better; maintain visual cohesion while ensuring distinctiveness from competitors.

## Quotes

> You may have heard the popular saying: a picture is worth a thousand words. Pictures can communicate concepts better than words alone, partly because people tend to remember information better when presented visually.

> In contrast, words are stored in only one way — the word itself. While the mind can generate images for words, this process is not automatic and requires significant cognitive effort, making it less common.

> Adding text labels provides redundancy and will strengthen comprehension and memorability.
