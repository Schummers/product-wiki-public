# UX Research: Chapter 4: Qualitative Research Methods

## Summary

This chapter shifts focus from quantitative to qualitative research, defined as the study of subjective aspects including personal stories and challenges of customers. Unlike quantitative research which focuses on what can be measured, qualitative research explores why customers complete tasks and seeks to understand motivations and desires through comprehension and accessibility that may not be numerically measured but impact usability and desirability. The chapter covers where to find qualitative research (rooted in ethnography, anthropology, and psychology) and typically relies on smaller participant groups (5–10 to 20–40 people) rather than the large pools quantitative research requires. It then explores three phases of research: initial planning (landscape analysis to identify market gaps, heuristic reviews using established best practices), discovery and exploration (contextual inquiry with think-aloud protocols and 5–50 participants), and product testing and validation (moderated validation in labs, unmoderated remote validation, and remote moderated testing). The chapter emphasizes that qualitative and quantitative research are equals rather than opposites, and discusses how to combine them into data-driven personas and customer journeys that account for both task time and human needs.

## Key Takeaways

- **Qualitative Research Definition** — the study of subjective aspects including personal stories and challenges, seeking to understand customer motivations and desires through comprehension and accessibility rather than numerical measurement
- **Common Measures** — include pleasures or challenges of tasks, preferences for different tools, comprehension of content, comfort with systems or tasks, and workarounds or "MacGyvered" solutions
- **Participant Scale** — typically relies on smaller groups (5–10 people for narrow projects, 20–40 for broader scope) compared to quantitative research's large pools, though smaller data sets can still provide valuable insights
- **Initial Planning Methods** — landscape analysis identifies existing products reflecting similar functions or customer segments to understand market gaps and opportunities; heuristic reviews evaluate existing products against established best practices like Jakob Nielsen's 10 Usability Heuristics
- **Contextual Inquiry** — an ethnographic approach of following customers in their environment and observing task completion, using "think aloud" protocols to provide context and immediate observable feedback, conducted with as few as 5 or as many as 50 participants depending on scope
- **Product Validation Approaches** — moderated validation occurs in a lab or workplace with a researcher adjusting testing criteria on the fly and asking probing questions; unmoderated remote validation uses tools like UserTesting to allow participants to complete predefined tasks with audio and screen recording; remote moderated testing combines real-time interaction with digital communication tools
- **Participatory Design** — takes many forms including workshop brainstorming, sketching exercises, and collaborative visual adaptations with business stakeholders and customers, occurring at any research phase
- **Combining Qualitative and Quantitative** — the most successful projects balance both approaches; qualitative provides context and motivations while quantitative provides scale, together creating data-driven personas and customer journeys that account for both task time and human needs

## Quotes

> Qualitative research is the study of anything that is subjective, notably the personal stories and challenges of our customers.

> These factors cannot be statistically measured. At the same time, qualitative research provides insights and can use smaller data sets.

> Contextual inquiries start with a prompt to participants to "think aloud" about what they are doing. This provides context of actions as well as immediate, observable feedback on their tasks.

## Concepts

- [[Qualitative Research]] — the chapter's primary topic, defining qualitative research as the complement to quantitative methods, rooted in ethnography and psychology, suited to understanding motivations, desires, and subjective experience
- [[User Research]] — covered extensively through contextual inquiry (think-aloud and ride-along studies), where researchers observe customers performing tasks in context and ask probing questions to gain deeper understanding of motivations and barriers
- [[Usability Testing]] — product validation is presented in three forms: moderated testing where researchers adjust criteria and ask probing questions, unmoderated remote testing using audio and screen recording, and remote moderated testing combining real-time interaction with digital tools
- [[Workshop Facilitation]] — participatory design is explored as collaborative exercises ranging from simple brainstorming to formal sketching exercises where participants adapt their mental models in visual ways, occurring at any research phase with stakeholders and customers
- [[Stakeholder Engagement]] — emphasized as critical through stakeholder interviews during initial planning, as content strategy consultant Sara Wachter-Boettcher notes must happen with clients rather than for clients to ensure buy-in and lasting change
