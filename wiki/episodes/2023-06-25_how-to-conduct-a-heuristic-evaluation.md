# How to Conduct a Heuristic Evaluation

## Summary

A heuristic evaluation is a systematic usability-inspection method where evaluators assess a design against established guidelines (heuristics) to identify problems without user testing. The article provides step-by-step guidance for conducting evaluations, emphasizing that three to five independent evaluators produce more reliable results than a single evaluator. While heuristic evaluations cannot replace user research and are limited by evaluators' biases, they are cost-effective for identifying obvious problems early in design and for building UX evaluation skills.

## Key Takeaways

- **Multiple independent evaluators find more issues** — heuristic evaluations work best with three to five people who evaluate the design independently before consolidating findings, ensuring diverse perspectives and reducing individual blind spots.
- **Jakob Nielsen's 10 heuristics provide strong guidance** — these widely-adopted guidelines based on human behavior and psychology are recommended as the starting point, though domain-specific heuristics may supplement them.
- **Heuristic violations don't always equal problems** — a design element violating a heuristic may still be justified by context and tradeoffs; user research is necessary to validate whether violations truly harm usability.
- **Scope narrowing makes evaluations manageable** — focusing on specific tasks, sections, user groups, or device types prevents overwhelming evaluators and produces more detailed, actionable findings.
- **Practice develops UX instincts** — conducting multiple heuristic evaluations trains evaluators to recognize common usability problems quickly, eventually reducing reliance on explicit heuristics.

## Quotes

> "Heuristic evaluations are useful for stretching a limited UX research budget, because they help you find likely issues without having to test with participants."

> "Just because a design choice violates a heuristic, that does not necessarily mean it's a problem that needs to be fixed — it depends on the particular context and the available alternatives."

> "Heuristic evaluations become easier the more you conduct them. With practice, you'll need to rely less and less on the actual heuristics. You'll start to develop UX instincts, so you can quickly recognize potential usability problems."
