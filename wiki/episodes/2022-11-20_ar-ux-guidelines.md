# The Usability of Augmented Reality

## Summary

Augmented reality (AR) enhances the real world with an additional layer of information and has potential applications across fitness, entertainment, ecommerce, tourism, education, and entertainment. However, designers have limited guidelines for successfully designing and implementing AR experiences. Based on a usability study with 11 participants (4 remote, 7 in-person) testing AR apps across diverse domains, this article is the third in a series examining AR user experience, following articles on onboarding walkthroughs and calibration.

The study reveals that most users are unfamiliar with AR and confuse it with virtual reality, and many lack gaming experience that would otherwise help them interpret AR-specific UI patterns and signifiers. While users are generally enthusiastic about AR's potential, particularly for immersive, interactive educational experiences, they also question its gratuitous use in apps where it adds no value. Key findings include: AR features are often not discoverable or findable (users unaware of AR in frequently used apps), icons signifying AR are cryptic and unfamiliar, instructions fail to account for users' limited familiarity, and overlays can interfere with the AR experience. Effective AR design requires clear discoverability, simple instructions with high visual contrast, self-sufficient interactions (no need to exit AR to adjust details), and a balance between UI controls and immersive experience.

## Key Takeaways

- **Gaming experience transfers to AR patterns** — participants with gaming backgrounds quickly interpreted AR icons and interactions; non-gamers struggled with unfamiliar signifiers.
- **AR should not be gratuitous** — users appreciated immersive, educational, interactive AR but questioned AR features that added no functional value; technical limitations that prevent realistic product visualization undermine trust.
- **Make AR features discoverable** — many users don't know AR exists in frequently used apps (Amazon, Ulta, Best Buy, Target); add visual indicators on product listings or filtering options to highlight AR-compatible items.
- **Use clear, labeled icons and high-contrast instructions** — cryptic AR icons confuse users; pair icons with descriptive text labels and place instructions on solid backgrounds for visibility across variable real-world environments.
- **Make AR self-sufficient** — users should adjust object color, details, or dimensions within the AR scene without exiting to access product information; minimize UI overlays that crowd the screen and interfere with the experience.

## Quotes

> "Participants who had exposure to game UIs found AR patterns easier to figure out, compared to the participants who did not play any games."

> "Even though most users find delight in augmented reality, do not implement AR for the sake of AR, but, rather, make sure that it adds value to the overall user experience."

## Concepts

- [[Augmented Reality]] — effective AR design prioritizes immersive, interactive, educational experiences over gratuitous use; technical quality, realism, and interaction smoothness significantly impact user perception.
- [[Mobile Design]] — AR apps must account for users' limited familiarity with AR; instructions need to be high-contrast, unambiguous, and tailored to users without gaming experience.
- [[Discoverability]] — AR features hidden in apps lack discoverability; visual indicators on product listings, filtering options, and clear labels help users find and recognize AR-compatible items.
- [[User Experience]] — balancing UI controls with immersive experience is critical; overlays should be minimizable; users benefit from audio narration, gesture-based controls, and self-sufficient interactions within the AR scene.
