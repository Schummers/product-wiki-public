# Laws of UX: 9. Tesler's Law

## Summary

Chapter 9 examines Tesler's law (the law of conservation of complexity), traced back to Larry Tesler's work on the language of interaction design at Xerox PARC in the mid-1980s, and defined by him later at Apple. The principle states that every system contains an irreducible core of complexity that cannot be designed away—only transferred between the user and the system. Designers must choose whether to burden users or absorb complexity in the product itself. The chapter argues that designers have a responsibility to absorb as much of that complexity as possible rather than ship it to users, illustrated through email systems, checkout processes, AI-powered interfaces, and retail innovations.

## Key Takeaways

- **Irreducible Complexity** — Every process has inherent complexity that cannot be eliminated, only transferred. A designer must decide whether the user or the system bears the burden. Tesler's reasoning: "If a million users each waste a minute a day dealing with complexity that an engineer could have eliminated in a week by making the software a little more complex, you are penalizing the user to make the engineer's job easier."

- **Email Simplification** — Modern email clients reduce complexity by pre-populating the sender and suggesting recipients based on contacts. Gmail's Smart Compose further abstracts typing burden by suggesting sentence completions. The complexity hasn't disappeared; it has shifted from user to system.

- **Checkout Abstraction** — Ecommerce sites let shipping addresses inherit from billing details, eliminating duplicate entry. Apple Pay and similar services push complexity to payment service providers, allowing users to verify and purchase instantly without entering additional information.

- **Complexity Bias** — Humans favor complex solutions over simple ones because complexity is associated with intelligence and expertise. A 1989 study by Farris and Revlin showed participants preferred complicated hypotheses over the simple rule "list three numbers that go up," suggesting preference for overengineered solutions stems from incomplete understanding.

- **Amazon Go Checkout-Free Experience** — Advanced machine learning, computer vision, and AI are deeply integrated so customers simply enter, grab items, and leave. Friction is drastically reduced for users while designers absorb the dizzying complexity of the system.

- **Intent-Based Interaction** — Natural language interfaces like Mixpanel's Spark enable users to ask questions and get analyses without knowing system commands. Complexity shifts from users learning commands to systems understanding intent, democratizing access to powerful features.

## Quotes

> Tesler's law, also known as the law of conservation of complexity, states that for any system there is a certain amount of complexity that cannot be reduced.

> All processes have a core of complexity that cannot be designed away and therefore must be assumed by either the system or the user.

> Users never read software manuals but instead start using the software immediately.

## Concepts

- [[Tesler's Law]] — The principle that every system has irreducible complexity that must be transferred either to the user or to the designers and developers; the chapter places the responsibility for absorbing it on designers and developers.

- [[Cognitive Bias]] — The human tendency to favor complex solutions over simple ones, often because complexity is associated with expertise. Leads designers to overlook simpler solutions without sufficient problem understanding.

- [[Progressive Disclosure]] — An interaction design technique revealing only essential actions by default, with additional features accessible through dropdowns, accordions, or toggles. Reduces cognitive load and interface clutter while deferring advanced functionality.

- [[User Friction]] — Unnecessary complexity that creates barriers to task completion. Designers must strategically reduce friction by transferring inherent complexity to the system, as demonstrated by checkout simplification and intent-based interfaces.

- [[User Interface]] — The challenge of managing complexity while maintaining usability requires deliberate choices about which aspects of a system to abstract away and how to guide users without overwhelming them.
