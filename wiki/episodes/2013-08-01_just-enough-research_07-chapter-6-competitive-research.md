# Just Enough Research: Chapter 6: Competitive Research

## Summary

Chapter 6 addresses how to identify and analyze competitors in the context of product design. Rather than seeing competition narrowly as direct market rivals, Hall argues that a product's true competition includes all solutions—products, services, habits, behaviors—that users might choose instead. The chapter emphasizes that the hardest competitor to beat is what customers are using right now, since switching requires users to overcome inertia and the cost of change.

Hall presents structured frameworks for competitive research: SWOT analysis (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats) to position a product strategically, a competitive audit to assess how competitors position themselves and what differentiates them, and brand audit to evaluate whether a brand's identity, name, and visual presence support its intended value proposition. The chapter also recommends usability testing competitors' products to understand their user experience strengths and weaknesses. Overall, the chapter frames competitive research as essential to understanding the landscape and ensuring a product has the best chance to adapt and survive.

## Key Takeaways

- **Redefining competition** — Competition extends beyond direct market rivals to include any alternative that addresses the same user need: YouTube channels, social media, free services, bad habits, or simple inertia. Users choose based on what they are already using and what requires the least effort to change.
- **SWOT analysis framework** — The chapter borrows SWOT (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats), devised by management consultant Albert S. Humphrey, arranged in a 2×2 grid to guide strategy. Strengths and opportunities add up to the competitive advantage, and the research should focus on competitive opportunities and threats.
- **Competitive audit process** — Conduct an audit by listing potential competitors, then answering seven questions about each: their explicit positioning, who they appear to target, their key differentiators, how far they embody your positive and negative attributes, how the user needs they serve overlap with yours, what they do particularly well or badly, and where that leaves conventions, opportunities and practices to adopt. The audit should include marketing sites, applications, third-party storefronts, and physical touchpoints.
- **Brand audit essentials** — Hall gives three questions to ask about your own brand: which attributes you want associated with it and which avoided, what value proposition it communicates, and what associations customers actually report in ethnographic interviews. For many interactive products, the brand experience is inseparable from the user experience itself. Assess whether the brand name, logo, and visual identity communicate the intended attributes and value proposition appropriately.
- **Competitive brand name assessment** — The chapter calls it arguable that mint.com's name contributed to the product's success: short, memorable, and easy to build a brand and interface around (freshness, the green of American money) in a field where trust is at a premium. It contrasts it with the cohort of Geezeo, Buxfer and Wesabe, which together "sound like a group of playful macaques".
- **Logo assessment in context** — Logo importance depends on context: athletic apparel logos must work independently to differentiate products; web application logos matter less than name and keywords; mobile app icons must function reliably in a uniform space. Assess all contexts where users will encounter the logo.

## Quotes

> Your competition is Facebook, Apple, Twitter, the Haus of Gaga, Hulu, Wikipedia, a freaky Japanese YouTube channel, all of Google, everyone who ever had an idea for a startup, the nosey neighbor who offers unsolicited advice, the hot teaching assistant, all the people at the dog park, mom, dad, sloth, inertia, insecurity, fear, corporate bureaucracy, sunk infrastructure costs, memory lapses, duct tape, bubble gum, ADD, marijuana (medical or otherwise), the sofa, some hacker in Serbia you've never heard of, what all the kids are doing these days, what mother never told you, some modern Chinese secrets...and more!

> The hardest competitor to beat is the one your potential customers are using right now. If they have to stop using that one to start using yours, they may incur a switching cost. People are lazy, forgetful creatures of habit. Your target customers have to love you more than they hate change.

> Your brand is simply your reputation and those things that signify your identity and reputation to your current and potential customers. That reputation offers a promise of all the good things you do for your customers, most of which exist only in the customer's mind.

## Concepts

- [[Competitive Analysis]] — The chapter defines competitive analysis as identifying not just direct market competitors but all alternatives that solve the same user problem, then assessing positioning, differentiators, and user needs served. The competitive landscape includes products, services, habits, and sources of inertia; Hall borrows Albert S. Humphrey's SWOT as a 2×2 grid to organize internal strengths and weaknesses alongside external opportunities and threats, strengths and opportunities adding up to the competitive advantage.
- [[Branding]] — The chapter emphasizes that brand is reputation and identity, communicated through name, logo, attributes, and value proposition. For interactive products, the brand experience and user experience are inseparable, and brand should authentically reflect the product's actual positioning and values.
