# The 10x Method: Finding and Validating the Idea

## Summary

Chapter 2 challenges the conventional "1% idea, 99% execution" wisdom and argues that ideas matter more than typically acknowledged. The chapter defines a startup idea as a multidimensional framework combining problem, solution, and go-to-market strategy, not a one-liner. It introduces three categories of startup ideas: problem-solution fit (from personal frustration and industry expertise), market or technology shift (new capabilities enabling better solutions), and category creation (behavioral shifts creating entirely new ways of doing things). The chapter then presents Hexa's three-step validation process: becoming an expert through 25–30 user interviews, scoping the idea in writing across eight dimensions, and creating low-fidelity wireframes. The goal is to move beyond "I have an idea" to "I have a well-thought-out plan to build something exceptional."

## Key Takeaways

- **Startup idea as framework** — A robust idea combines three dimensions: the problem (specific pain point or need), the solution (the product you'll build), and the go-to-market strategy (how customers find and use it). It's more structured than a one-liner and evolves through research, feedback, and testing.
- **Three categories of ideas** — Category 1 (problem-solution fit from personal frustration or industry knowledge), Category 2 (new technology enabling dramatically better solutions to known problems), Category 3 (category creation through behavior change). Each requires different search strategies and evidence.
- **Innovation in one dimension is enough** — Every successful startup innovates in at least one of three areas: problem discovery, solution creation, or go-to-market strategy. Excelling in just one dimension can create substantial market value and differentiation.
- **Become an expert through interviews** — Spend time with 25–30 potential users in 30-minute interviews. Observe their workflows, ask open-ended questions ("Why?", "How?", "Can you show me?"), avoid leading questions. You'll hit 80% learning after enough conversations; don't chase the remaining 20%.
- **Write to force clarity** — Document the idea across eight dimensions: summary, problem, solution (MVP plus long-term vision), implementation, go-to-market, market size, opportunity (why now), competition, and hypotheses. Writing surfaces gaps and unexamined assumptions.
- **Validate with wireframes** — Create 3–4 low-fidelity wireframes showing what the product does, not how it looks. For non-visual products (APIs, AI agents), wireframes might take the form of API documentation or dialogue flows instead of screens.

## Quotes

> Execution is the engine, but the idea is the map. If you start driving without one, you can end up working incredibly hard... in the wrong direction.

> Ideas belong to their time, not to their creators. They emerge at the intersection of cultural shifts, new technologies, and evolving user behaviors. The best entrepreneurs aren't necessarily the first to think of something, they're the ones who recognize when an idea's moment has arrived and move fast to bring it to life.

> At the very beginning, what matters most is finding the right problem to solve and making sure you're building the right solution for that problem. Until you have that, everything else is noise.

## Concepts

- [[Ideation]] — Finding startup ideas emerges from staying close to real problems and paying attention to pain points others overlook. Ideas come from action (building, talking to users, testing tools) rather than sitting and waiting for inspiration; developing your "idea muscle" is a habit built through years of practice and engagement with the world.
- [[Idea Validation]] — Hexa's three-step framework (become an expert through interviews, scope the idea in writing, create wireframes) validates ideas iteratively without building blindly. This process has helped Hexa validate over 50 startup ideas and rule out many more before writing code.
- [[Innovation]] — For an idea to be worth pursuing, it must innovate in at least one dimension: discovering an unaddressed problem, creating a significantly improved or new solution, or developing a new go-to-market strategy. A well-timed, well-formed idea can go far even in imperfect hands.
- [[Business Strategy]] — The go-to-market strategy is a full dimension of startup ideas: you can succeed by innovating primarily in GTM while the problem and solution are already known. Scoping must include personas, first 100 customer acquisition channels, business model, and long-term GTM evolution.
