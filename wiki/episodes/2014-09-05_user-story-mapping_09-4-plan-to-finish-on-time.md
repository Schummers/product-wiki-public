# User Story Mapping: 4. Plan to Finish on Time

## Summary

Chapter 4 centers on Aaron and Mike at Workiva, who must deliver a feature by a specific date after completing product discovery. Rather than building the entire feature at once, they use a story map to create a development plan sliced into three phases: an opening game focused on core functionality end-to-end (the "functional walking skeleton"), a midgame that adds features and handles edge cases, and an endgame dedicated to refinement. Patton emphasizes that effective estimation comes from shared understanding—developers who truly grasp what they're building estimate more accurately—and that managing delivery means treating estimates as a budget that teams can measure their actual velocity against.

The broader principle is artistic rather than mechanical: drawing on Leonardo da Vinci's approach, a visualization Patton credits to John Armitage's 2004 paper "Are Agile Methods Good for Design?", he argues that great work iterates and refines continuously within a time constraint, rather than building pieces sequentially and hoping they fit together at the end. Risk management is implicit in this strategy: by building the riskiest, most technically challenging parts first in the opening game, teams surface obstacles early.

## Key Takeaways

- **Map only what you need** — A story map does not require charting an entire product; map just the feature or scope needed to support your specific conversation and planning.

- **Shared understanding drives accurate estimation** — Developers who truly understand what they're building, including who will use it and why, produce better estimates than any estimation method applied to unclear requirements.

- **Slice for an opening game, midgame, and endgame** — This is the strategy Patton says he personally relies on, borrowed from chess and, he admits, possibly misused since he barely plays. Divide work into three phases: opening game (core functionality end-to-end, addressing technical risk), midgame (filling in features and handling edge cases), and endgame (refinement and polish).

- **Functional walking skeleton reveals risk early** — Patton borrows the term *functional walking skeleton* from Alistair Cockburn, and notes others call the same thing a "steel thread" or a "tracer bullet." Building a thin slice through the entire system lets teams validate technical assumptions, test performance and scalability, and spot unforeseen challenges before committing to the full schedule.

- **The slices are milestones, not releases** — Patton is explicit that none of the three slices is released to customers and users: each is a point where the team stops and takes stock, and from a user's perspective the result is incomplete.
- **Treat estimates as a budget to manage** — Monitor actual velocity against the original estimate as work proceeds; if the team is halfway through its time but only a third of the way through the scope, take action to adjust, replan, or escalate.

- **Risk stories on the map, per Chris Shinkle (SEP)** — In a sidebar contributed by Chris Shinkle of SEP, a team building a wireless access-control system for a large security company was missing milestones because of unplanned work; they increased the frequency and fidelity of their story map and added "Risk Stories" alongside the usual activities, tasks and details, so the map showed the unknown work as well as the known.

## Concepts

- [[Release Planning]] — Involves creating a development plan that slices the work into opening game (technical foundations), midgame (feature completion), and endgame (refinement); teams estimate and manage this plan as a budget.

- [[Shared Understanding]] — The foundation of accurate estimation; developers and the whole team must understand who the user is, what they're trying to accomplish, and why it matters.

- [[Risk Management]] — Tackled by building technically risky, challenging parts first in the opening game so obstacles are surfaced early rather than late in the schedule.

- [[Iterative Design]] — Applied through the three-phase approach where each slice builds on the previous, with the team learning and adjusting course as they go, rather than building sequentially and assembling at the end.

- [[Agile Development]] — Improved by involving developers in mapping conversations, treating estimates as budgets to manage actively, and measuring actual work against estimates to improve future predictions.

- [[Story Slicing]] — The technique of dividing the feature into three crosscutting slices (opening, midgame, endgame) rather than horizontal layers; each slice runs the full width of the feature and builds toward a working end-to-end system.
