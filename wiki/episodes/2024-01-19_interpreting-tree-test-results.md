# Tree Testing Part 2: Interpreting the Results

## Summary

Tree testing generates quantitative data measuring how well users understand categories and labels in information architecture. Key metrics include success rate (percentage of users finding the right category), directness (percentage going immediately without backtracking), and time spent. Median success rate across studies is 62 percent; suggested benchmarks are poor (<40 percent), fair (41-60), good (61-80), very good (80-90), and excellent (>90). Tasks should be evaluated within context of importance and hierarchy level, not against absolute standards. Analyzing first-click data reveals where users initially expected to find information; final-destination analysis shows category overlaps. Comparing multiple trees through between-subjects designs identifies which IA structure works best, though sample sizes of 50+ participants per tree are needed for statistical significance.

## Key Takeaways

- **Three metrics capture user understanding** — success rate indicates findability, directness measures effort/struggle, time spent indicates mental load; all three together reveal user experience.
- **Success rate benchmarks provide context** — 62 percent is median; good is 61-80 percent; but mission-critical tasks should exceed 90 percent; task importance matters more than absolute numbers.
- **First-click data predicts success** — once users reach the correct top-level category, context cues make finding the answer likely; incorrect first clicks are often disastrous.
- **Directness reveals user struggle** — tasks with high success rate but low directness mean users succeeded despite difficulty; high backtracking indicates category labels or structure needs improvement.
- **Comparing multiple trees requires statistical significance testing** — comparing two trees needs 50+ participants per tree and manual statistical testing; ANOVA required for 3+ trees.

## Quotes

> Tree testing evaluates the categories and labels in an information architecture. Unlike think-aloud usability testing, tree tests are usually run as unmoderated studies and generate only quantitative data.

> Success rate seems simple: higher is better. But to act based on this metric, you first need an appropriate frame of reference to determine what a 'good' success rate is for both the overall tree and for a specific task.

> Tasks with high success rates can still be a poor user experience if users must retrace their steps and try multiple places before finding the right answer.
