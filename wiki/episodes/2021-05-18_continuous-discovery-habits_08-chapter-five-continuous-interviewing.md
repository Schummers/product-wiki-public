# Continuous Discovery Habits: Chapter Five: Continuous Interviewing

## Summary

This chapter explains why regular customer interviews are critical to discovering opportunities—customer needs, pain points, and desires—despite the common belief that customers cannot articulate what they want. Torres draws on cognitive psychology research to show that direct questions about behavior lead to unreliable data because people rationalize their actions post-hoc rather than accurately recall why they did something. The chapter teaches story-based interviewing as the reliable method: asking customers to recount specific recent experiences elicits their actual behavior rather than idealized behavior. Additionally, it covers interview snapshots for synthesizing findings, various recruiting strategies (in-product, customer-facing colleagues, advisory boards), and the importance of conducting interviews weekly as a team habit.

## Key Takeaways

- **Direct questions invoke cognitive biases** — People are poor at answering factual questions about their own behavior; they construct coherent but unreliable narratives (the "left brain interpreter") to explain actions they cannot fully recall, so asking "Why did you buy jeans?" yields idealized stories, not actual decision drivers.
- **Story-based interviewing surfaces actual behavior** — Asking customers to describe a specific recent instance ("Tell me about the last time you bought jeans") yields more reliable data grounded in memory of actual behavior rather than speculation about general patterns.
- **Interview snapshots synthesize and index research** — A one-page summary with memorable quote, customer photo, quick facts, identified opportunities, and a drawn experience map helps teams remember specific customer stories weeks or months later and surfaces patterns across interviews.
- **Weekly interviewing sustains the habit** — Interviewing every week is easier to maintain than starting and stopping; automated recruiting via in-product prompts, customer-facing teams, or advisory boards is essential to make weekly interviews feasible without constant effort.
- **Product trios should interview together** — All three roles benefit from hearing customers directly; a diverse interviewing team hears different salient details (product manager, designer, engineer each pick up distinct signals) and avoids concentrating "voice of the customer" power in one person.
- **Excavate stories through temporal prompts** — Prompts like "What happened first?", "What happened next?", and "Who was with you?" guide customers through their own stories by treating narratives as having a beginning, middle, and end with supporting characters and obstacles.

## Quotes

> Instead of asking, "What criteria do you use when purchasing a pair of jeans?"—a direct question that encourages our participant to speculate about their behavior—we want to ask, "Tell me about the last time you purchased a pair of jeans."

> An interview snapshot is a one-pager designed to help you synthesize what you learned in a single interview. It's how you are going to turn your copious notes into actionable insights.

> When you continuously interview customers, there's no clear point at which to stop and synthesize what you are learning. Instead, you'll need to synthesize as you go.

## Concepts

- [[Continuous Interviewing]] — The chapter defines and advocates for weekly customer interviews conducted by the product team as a foundational habit that surfaces unmet needs and prevents the team from relying solely on what customers say they want versus what they actually do.
- [[User Interviews]] — The chapter teaches story-based interviewing as a reliable method to uncover customer opportunities (needs, pain points, desires) by asking customers to recount specific recent experiences rather than generalize about their behavior.
- [[Experience Map]] — The chapter uses experience maps (introduced in Chapter 4) as a guide during interviews to track the customer's journey, identify nodes and steps in their story, and compare individual customer stories against the generalized experience map.
- [[Opportunity Solution Tree]] — The chapter frames customer opportunities as the needs, pain points, and desires discovered through interviews; these opportunities form the target for the discovery process and are distinct from feature requests or solutions.
