# Startup Studio Playbook: The Studio Funnel

## Summary

Part III of the Startup Studio Playbook details the complete venture-building process from market selection to portfolio management. The studio funnel is the main asset enabling a studio to launch startups "by the dozen" through a disciplined, repeatable process. Szigeti describes eight sequential stages: creating an abundance of ideas, validating and selecting ideas, trashing unsuccessful concepts, initial product build, making go/no-go decisions, raising seed funding, scaling and independence, and eventually exit. Each stage has specific inputs, outputs, success criteria, and resource requirements. The chapter emphasizes cost discipline, emotional detachment from ideas, rapid failure cycles, and building collective organizational knowledge through post-mortem and pre-mortem processes.

## Key Takeaways

- **Abundance mindset** — Generate 300+ viable startup concepts per year through daily 60-minute ideation workshops. Sunk cost fallacy is one of the leading reasons studios burn cash, so having abundant alternatives makes it psychologically easier to kill ideas early and redirect resources to more promising ones.
- **Idea scoring and selection** — Rate ideas on three dimensions (problem severity, market size, confidence in building something innovative that grows fast). Budget strictly per idea (e.g., 10k–20k EUR and 3–6 months for sharing economy concepts) so that killing one idea doesn't threaten the entire portfolio.
- **Rapid and transparent killing** — Make fast, hard decisions with clear success/failure criteria and fixed deadlines. Postponing the decision to kill a project keeps the team frustrated and burns resources. The studio's advantage over traditional startups is the ability to move from one failed concept to another without losing the core team.
- **Build phase inputs** — Assign a dedicated CEO and possibly CTO to each initiative. With a few weeks of build time for simple web apps and one core feature with nice design, aim to generate traction and test user acquisition before seeking follow-on funding.
- **Go/no-go criteria** — Successful metrics at the end of the initial build phase include a working MVP, active users, and a scalable growth engine (or higher bar: Product-Market fit). The studio decides whether to fund seed rounds and spin-off the startup or redirect resources.
- **Seed funding and spin-off** — Studios can leverage their portfolio track record to attract investor interest. The spin-off process includes negotiating financial agreements on services the studio continues to provide, discussing founder equity adjustments, and maintaining the studio's role as active co-founder.
- **Exit strategy** — Studios can pursue low-hanging-fruit, quick acquisitions to build an experienced local CEO pool and recycle capital into new ventures, or aim for long-term unicorns that capture entire markets.

## Quotes

> Do this once a day and you'll have 300+ viable startup concepts in a year to chose from. It's easier to avoid sunk cost mistakes when you have plenty of new directions available to you.

## Concepts

- [[Studio Funnel]] — Szigeti presents an eight-stage repeatable funnel: ideation, validation, trashing failed ideas, initial build, go/no-go decision, seed funding, scaling and independence, and exit, designed to process ideas systematically and cost-efficiently at scale.
- [[Idea Validation]] — The validation stage uses landing pages, online ads, and direct customer contact to measure market interest and customer acquisition cost, typically lasting a few days to weeks with a small budget, before deciding which ideas advance to the build phase.
- [[Stage Gate]] — At the end of the initial build phase, the studio evaluates whether an MVP has generated traction, active users, and a scalable growth engine (or Product-Market fit), then decides whether to fund seed rounds or terminate the startup and reallocate resources.
- [[Portfolio Strategy]] — The studio manages a portfolio of parallel startups with shared resources, making intentional decisions on how many ventures to pursue simultaneously (e.g., 12–15 per year) based on available capital and core team capacity, treating failure as a dispersed risk rather than portfolio-destroying loss.
