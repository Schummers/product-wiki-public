# User Story Mapping: 14. Using Discovery to Build Shared Understanding

## Summary

This chapter outlines Patton's systematic approach to product discovery, structured in four essential steps: framing the idea, understanding customers and users, envisioning the solution, and minimizing and planning. Unlike a simple top-down requirements process, discovery is about learning—asking and answering questions about problems being solved, valuable solutions, usability, and feasibility. Patton emphasizes that discovery yields more than just smaller stories; it produces simple models (personas, journey maps, story maps, UI sketches, and architectural sketches) that build shared understanding. The chapter covers practical techniques: lightweight persona creation (his example is a persona built with the nonprofit Mano a Mano) (done collaboratively rather than locked away in documents), mapping how users work today to understand current pain points, using story maps to envision future solutions, combining words and pictures to avoid miscommunication, validating completeness and engineering feasibility, and the critical final step of minimizing scope. Patton stresses that good discovery ends with more ideas discarded than kept, and that prioritization must flow from specific business goals and target users, not from a ranked feature list.

## Key Takeaways

- **Discovery is learning, not building** — Discovery work focuses on understanding problems, valuable solutions, usability, and feasibility; it is not about producing shippable software or capturing exhaustive requirements.
- **Create multiple models, not just stories** — Effective discovery produces simple models: personas, journey maps, story maps, UI sketches, architectural sketches, and the organizational profiles his friend Lane Halley calls *orgzonas*, all supporting shared understanding and different types of conversation.
- **Build lightweight personas collaboratively** — Instead of creating detailed personas locked away in a document that no one reads, build simple personas together with the team using flipchart paper, discussing what you know and have observed from users.
- **Map current workflows to understand pain** — Mapping how users work today reveals hot spots of pain and pockets of joy; this understanding frames what the new solution should improve. Patton illustrates it with what Duncan Brown of the Caplin Group calls a *narrative journey map*, whose body carries facts, observations, pains and joys.
- **Words and pictures together prevent miscommunication** — Describing a product idea in words alone leads developers to imagine something different than intended; combining story maps, UI sketches, and storyboards ensures shared vision.
- **Design Studio for collaborative ideation** — Patton recommends a Design Studio, an approach he says he first saw described by Jeff White and Jim Unger: invite eight to twelve people, describe the problem, have everyone sketch, share and build on ideas in groups of four, consolidate, then let the discovery team make the final call — quoting Leisa Reichelt, "design by community is not design by committee."
- **Two contributed sidebars** — Josh Seiden, with artwork by Demian Repucci, describes replacing a wall of thousands of module-organized user stories at an education startup with a usage-centric story map spine, illustrated as storyboards. David Hussman of DevJam argues discovery can stay simple: suggest an idea, pick the people who would benefit, build a range of concrete examples from obvious to complex, use them to map journeys, and pick the journeys that teach you the most, avoiding "product arrogance."
- **Minimize before you start** — The majority of discovery work is eliminating ideas; viable solutions balance valuable, usable, and feasible aspects for a specific set of customers and uses; if you're not cutting more than you keep, you're not doing discovery right.
- **Prioritize goals and users, not features** — The secret to prioritization is starting with specific business outcomes, which drive focus to specific users and their goals, which then drives focus to needed features; most teams make the mistake of prioritizing features first.

## Quotes

> If the only thing you create while making sense of a big, ambiguous opportunity is smaller stories, then you're probably doing it wrong.

> If you're not cutting away more ideas than you keep, you're probably not doing discovery work right.

> Prioritize specific business goals, customers, and users, and then their goals, before prioritizing features.

## Concepts

- [[Product Discovery]] — Patton defines discovery as a deliberate, multi-step process that begins with learning about the opportunity, moves through framing, understanding users, envisioning solutions, and ends with ruthless minimization; it is structured work, not a brainstorm.
- [[Shared Understanding]] — The entire chapter returns to this core theme: discovery's primary output is not a list of stories but a shared mental model that product, design, engineering, and stakeholders all agree on; this is built through collaborative creation of models (maps, sketches, personas).
- [[Persona]] — Patton advocates lightweight persona creation done collaboratively with the team during discovery, grounded in what the team knows and has observed, to focus discussion and build empathy; he cautions against producing detailed documents that no one reads.
- [[Validated Learning]] — The chapter emphasizes that assumptions about what users need should be tested: teams should validate completeness against user workflows, validate engineering feasibility with architects, and be ready to learn from examples across a range of use cases.
- [[Prioritization]] — Patton articulates the principle that prioritization starts with specific business goals (not the term "business value"), flows to specific users and their goals, and only then flows to specific features; reversing this order leads to over-scoped products.
