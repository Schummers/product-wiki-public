# Product Management for UX People: CHAPTER 10: Roadmaps and How to Say No

## Summary

This chapter defines the product roadmap as a forward-looking plan organized around outcomes and themes rather than a list of features or a fixed launch schedule. Roadmaps work best structured into three time horizons (Now, Next, Later) and should ladder up to company objectives through product strategy. The chapter then explores how to prioritize the many competing demands that feed into a roadmap, examining methods like impact-vs.-effort matrices and the Eisenhower matrix, while remaining cautious about the proliferation of frameworks. Finally, it addresses the difficult work of saying no to stakeholders while maintaining relationships and building consensus around priorities.

## Key Takeaways

- **Roadmap vs. launch plan** — A roadmap is a forward-looking document for prioritizing near- and medium-term efforts, not a commitment to ship specific features on fixed dates. Treating it as a launch plan causes misalignment and broken trust with stakeholders.

- **Three time horizons** — Roadmaps should use Now (currently being worked on), Next (prepared to work on after current items), and Later (important but not yet active) as organizing frames, with increasing vagueness the farther out you go.

- **Outcomes over features** — Roadmaps should contain desired outcomes and results (e.g., "double retention," "support user collaboration") organized by themes that connect to company strategy, not lists of feature requests or solutions.

- **Product strategy ladders to company objectives** — Roadmaps align team goals with larger organizational strategy (e.g., through OKRs), with different organizational levels owning different pieces of the roadmap.

- **Prioritization requires systematic weighing** — Common methods (impact vs. effort, Eisenhower matrix, RICE, T-shirt sizing) help communicate decisions but are tools for discussion, not perfect decision-makers; frameworks help you articulate reasoning but not eliminate subjectivity.

- **Saying no requires respect and clarity** — Declining requests without diminishing their value involves understanding the problem they aim to solve, connecting it to current priorities, asking what existing commitments should be dropped to make room, and requesting data to justify priorities.

- **Matt LeMay's counterpoint: ask "why" before yes or no** — Against the chapter's own "art of saying no" framing, LeMay warns of the danger in that negative framing: "I see PMs fall in love with saying 'no' and miss out on critical information." His rule is to ask why before you say yes or no, because there is a reason behind an exec's new idea and it is the PM's job to understand it.

- **The framework overview is Andrea Saez's** — The chapter routes readers to Andrea Saez of ProdPad, whose "What is the best framework to prioritize what to work on next?", published at Product Manager HQ, it presents as a good overview of prioritization methods and of knowing which approach to use when.

- **A SaaS PM's own day closes the chapter** — The "A Day in the Life of a SaaS PM" box is contributed by Ian Johnson, Principal/Director of Product at Flow Commerce, Inc. It is his day, not a generic one.

- **Roadmaps need constant care** — Monthly reviews ensure the roadmap reflects reality; quarterly reviews move items between horizons; regular stakeholder updates manage expectations about when items will ship.

## Quotes

> “That’s a great idea,” I remember telling a Ph.D. with deep expertise into human interaction who was working at the time in the innovation lab of a huge tech company. “I love it, but how are you going to get it onto the roadmap?”

> Matt LeMay said, “I like to think of all of these as communication frameworks. The framework won’t help you make a perfect decision, but it will help you communicate how you made a necessarily imperfect decision.”

> Prioritization is not a linear process, and happens at different stages of development. Start with your objectives, find problems to solve, and run discovery to better understand how different items may give you the desired outcome. Frameworks help you understand and visualize information, foster conversations, and discussions. They are not there to make decisions for you.

## Concepts
