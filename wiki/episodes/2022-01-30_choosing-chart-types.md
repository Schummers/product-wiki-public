# Choosing Chart Types: Consider Context

## Summary

Quantitative UX data can be powerful for decision-making and demonstrating value, but many UX professionals struggle to present data in compelling, easy-to-interpret charts. Applying user-centered design principles to chart creation involves asking one key question: "What is your goal?" Once that is clear, designers should tailor charts to that goal while maintaining data integrity. Nielsen Norman Group introduces the "3 Cs for Better Charts"—context, clutter-free, and contrast—with this article focusing on providing context and choosing appropriate chart types. Context means numbers are meaningless without comparison; people need to compare a data point against another to interpret it.

## Key Takeaways

- **Context makes numbers meaningful** — A single number (e.g., 24% completion rate) is meaningless without comparison; people interpret data by comparing against other numbers (previous years, competitor products, different user groups, alternative tasks).
- **Bar charts are most effective for comparisons** — Researchers find bar charts easiest to comprehend for comparing values; they enable quick, accurate perception of differences between data points.
- **Paired bar charts require thoughtful grouping** — When displaying two variables, arrange bars to highlight the main comparison goal; placing key comparisons adjacent helps viewers quickly see the intended point.
- **Horizontal bar charts work for long labels** — When visualizing items with long names (feature names, task descriptions), horizontal orientation prevents abbreviation or rotation and keeps labels readable.
- **Line charts suit time-based trends** — For showing data trends over time, line charts work better than bar charts; use data-point markers to clarify timeline data-collection points.
- **Avoid stacked bar charts and complex visualizations** — Stacked bar charts are among the hardest to understand; pie charts, bubble charts, and other complex types requiring users to assess angle, area, or volume should be avoided unless they add unique value.

## Quotes

> "You don't have to have extensive visual-design skills to create good charts from your analytics data or other quantitative UX data. Simply apply the same user-centered–design approach that you would use when designing an interface."

> "What is your goal? In other words, what point are you trying to make? What do you want your audience to understand when they look at your chart? What action do you want them to take?"

> "Researchers have found that bar charts are much easier for people to comprehend than other types of charts (like pie charts). This is because bar charts make it easy for people to quickly and accurately perceive the differences between values."

## Concepts

- [[Data Visualization]] — Context is essential for meaningful data visualization; numbers require comparison against other numbers for audiences to interpret them correctly.
- [[Chart Types]] — Bar charts, line charts, and scatter plots are easiest for audiences to comprehend; complex visualizations requiring assessment of angle, area, or volume should be avoided unless they provide unique value.
- [[Quantitative Research]] — When presenting quantitative UX findings, apply user-centered design principles: define your goal first, provide context through comparison, and select chart types based on what enables viewers to understand your main point.
- [[Visual Design]] — Chart design should follow minimalist principles focused on data, not decoration; avoid Excel's default styles and complex visual effects that obscure meaning.
