# Banner Blindness Revisited: Users Dodge Ads on Mobile and Desktop

## Summary

Banner blindness persists across three decades as users intentionally ignore page elements they perceive as advertisements. This selective attention mechanism helps users manage overwhelming stimuli and focus on goal-relevant content. Users learn to ignore ads based on ad-specific placement (top and right rail), ad-like visual treatment (animation, colors, formatting), and proximity to actual ads. The "hot-potato" phenomenon causes users to avoid entire sections after seeing ads there. Legitimate content must not look like ads, and ads should not poison adjacent content through false proximity associations.

## Key Takeaways

- **Selective attention mechanism** — banner blindness results from limited attention capacity; users learn which elements are typically irrelevant and ignore them to focus on goal-directed content.
- **Location-based blindness** — users have learned that top banners and right rails typically contain ads and skip these areas; even non-ad content in these locations gets ignored.
- **Visual treatment signals** — elements using animation, fancy formatting, colored backgrounds, or embedded text are assumed to be ads and ignored, even if they are legitimate content.
- **Hot-potato phenomenon** — users who encounter an ad in a page section may assume the entire section contains only ads and avoid it completely, poisoning adjacent legitimate content.
- **Mobile inline ads** — mobile users struggle more with inline ads because screen size makes ads harder to distinguish from content, and large ads relative to screen size catch eyes despite user intent to ignore them.

## Quotes

> Banner blindness is an instance of selective attention: people direct their attention only to a subset of the stimuli in the environment — usually those related to their goals.

> Content placed in the same part of the screen as an advertisement is often considered to be an ad and is also ignored.

> Banner blindness has now been documented across 3 decades. It's a strong and robust phenomenon and, like advertisements themselves, is not likely to go away any time soon.

## Concepts

- [[User Behavior]] — understanding how users selectively filter stimuli based on learned associations with irrelevant elements.
- [[Branding]] — maintaining brand trust by not using deceptive ad-like visual treatments for legitimate content, even if it reduces salience.
- [[Eye Tracking]] — using eyetracking data to reveal which page sections receive no attention due to ad-like characteristics.
- [[Mobile Design]] — addressing the unique challenge of inline ads on mobile where screen size magnifies ad prominence and makes them harder to distinguish.
- [[Cognitive Psychology]] — applying principles of selective attention and learned behavior to understand why users ignore ads and adjacent content.
- [[Visual Design]] — carefully choosing colors, formatting, and styling to differentiate legitimate content from ads while maintaining visual hierarchy.
