# Articulating Design Decisions: Chapter 1. Great Designers Are Great Communicators

## Summary

This chapter establishes that great designers distinguish themselves through communication ability rather than design skill alone. Greever illustrates this with an anecdote about a massive three-hour meeting with a CEO where the focus was entirely on communicating and presenting design ideas—the time spent on actual design creation was trivial compared to preparation for the presentation. The chapter then explores why design is inherently subjective, how stakeholders inevitably influence projects despite lacking design expertise, and why design-by-committee and phenomena like the "CEO button" undermine user experience. Finally, Greever argues that communication is fundamentally the job, and that being articulate—crafting messages that yield desired responses—determines whether designers succeed at getting stakeholder support. The foundation for becoming a great designer rests on solving problems, making designs easy to use, and gaining stakeholder support through three key questions: What problem does it solve? How does it affect the user? Why is it better than the alternative?

## Key Takeaways

- **Great designers are communicators** — The difference between good and great designers is not problem-solving ability but the capacity to articulate solutions in compelling, convincing ways that win stakeholder support.
- **Design is subjective** — Because design is inherently subjective, all designs have legitimate alternatives, and multiple solutions exist for every problem, making it impossible to rely on designs "speaking for themselves."
- **Design-by-committee ruins UX** — When stakeholders without design expertise contribute unchecked suggestions, conversations spiral into groupthink and mob mentality, resulting in compromised user experiences (exemplified by phenomena like the "CEO button" or "home page syndrome").
- **Communication is the job** — Designers must recognize that communication about their work is not separate from design; it is the job itself, more important than technical design skill.
- **Being articulate confers multiple benefits** — Articulate designers impart intelligence, demonstrate intentionality, express confidence, and show respect, each of which builds stakeholder trust and support.
- **Three questions anchor success** — Every design must be evaluated against: (1) Does it solve a problem? (2) Is it easy for users? (3) Is it supported by everyone on the team? All three are equally necessary.
- **Conscious design awareness is required** — Designers must deliberately document their thought process, not just design intuitively, to be able to explain their decisions to non-designers.

## Quotes

> The way we talk about design to our stakeholders is critical to the success of our projects.

> Communicating about the designs was more important than the designs themselves.

> Good communicators win.

## Concepts

- [[Design Rationale]] — This chapter emphasizes that designers must write down and be consciously aware of why every design decision was made, including the problem being solved and how it affects users.
- [[Stakeholder Engagement]] — Greever identifies that getting stakeholder support is as important as solving problems and ensuring usability; without it, even revolutionary designs will not be implemented.
- [[Design Presentation]] — The chapter stresses that being articulate—using words to yield desired responses—is how designers win support and ensure their expertise remains central to decision-making.
- [[Team Collaboration]] — The dangers of design-by-committee are detailed, showing how unchecked collaboration without strong communication leads to compromised solutions.
- [[Active Listening]] — While focused on designers' ability to speak, the chapter hints at the need for designers to understand alternatives and stakeholder concerns, requiring careful observation of feedback.
- [[UX Career]] — Greever argues that communication skills are more likely to advance a designer's career than artistic talent, and that both junior and senior designers must continually practice articulation.
