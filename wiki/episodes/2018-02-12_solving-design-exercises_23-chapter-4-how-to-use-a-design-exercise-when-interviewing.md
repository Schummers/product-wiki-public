# Solving Product Design Exercises: Chapter 4: How to use a design exercise when interviewing

## Summary

This chapter focuses on how to effectively implement design exercises as part of the hiring process for design candidates. It covers the rationale for using two different exercises to test different skill sets, the distinction between take-home and on-site exercises, specific guidance for running on-site interviews, and methods for creating appropriate exercise prompts. The author advises against using a company's own product as an exercise due to interviewer bias and ethical concerns.

The chapter balances rigor in skill assessment with candidate experience, offering practical frameworks for evaluators to assess communication, critical thinking, and problem-solving abilities.

## Key Takeaways

- **Dual-exercise approach** — Test different skills with two exercises: a take-home exercise (4-8 hours, 2-7 day deadline) to evaluate visual and deliverable skills, and an on-site exercise (whiteboarding or quiet thinking) to assess product thinking and problem-solving under pressure.
- **Interview evaluation criteria** — Assess whether candidates ask questions, take feedback well, communicate clearly, follow a process, make valid assumptions, connect solutions to company mission, think about audience and context, explore non-obvious solutions, and define success metrics.
- **On-site interview guidance** — Make candidates comfortable with clear logistics, friendly demeanor, and empathetic support; offer one pushback to test handling of criticism; avoid making candidates feel trapped even when stuck.
- **Task ideation sources** — Generate exercise ideas by identifying market opportunities (industry research), finding real problems (news, Product Hunt, idea communities), or exploring established companies' business needs and underserved user segments.
- **Avoid company product exercises** — Do not use the company's own product as an exercise because it creates interviewer bias (familiarity advantage), risks aligning the candidate to the interviewer's existing perspectives, and may feel like free work from the candidate.

## Quotes

> The first thing you should understand before giving a candidate design exercises is what skills you are trying to test.

> Solving a problem in a short amount of time and presenting it to strangers is hard, especially if the candidate is not used to it or didn't have the chance to do this in their previous job. Try to make the candidate feel comfortable. Be friendly and empathetic.

> You're also heavily biased towards decisions that you helped make [...] In the end, you will choose the candidate who just happens to try solutions that align with your perspective.

## Concepts

- [[Design Exercise]] — Guidelines for implementing design exercises in hiring: separate take-home (visual and deliverable skills) from on-site (product thinking) to surface candidate weaknesses early and test multiple competencies.
- [[Design Hiring]] — The chapter details what skills to test (communication, critical thinking, feedback handling, high-pressure performance), how to run interviews that make candidates comfortable while maintaining rigor, and specific evaluation questions to guide assessment decisions.
- [[Product Thinking]] — On-site exercises test whether candidates follow a process, make valid assumptions, define goals and metrics, and connect their solutions to company mission and user context rather than jumping to solutions.
- [[Design Presentation]] — On-site interviews evaluate candidate communication clarity, ability to handle pushback and feedback, and willingness to explore ideas beyond the obvious, during both the solution phase and the presentation phase.
- [[Context of Use]] — A core evaluation criterion is whether candidates understand and articulate the target audience, user context, and customer needs before proposing solutions, distinguishing strong candidates from those who skip this step.
