# Defining Helpful Filter Categories and Values for Better UX

## Summary

Effective faceted search requires filter categories and values that are appropriate, predictable, free of jargon, and prioritized. Filter categories (properties of items like color or price) and filter values (specific values or ranges like "red" or "under $100") must be customized to user needs in specific domains rather than applying generic filters universally. Users appreciate filters for narrowing overwhelming options, but poorly designed filters with vague labels, jargon, or inappropriate prioritization confuse users and undermine the sense of control that good filters should provide.

## Key Takeaways

- **Customize filters for content type** — Don't use generic filters across all product types; washing machines need different filters than dishwashers. Specialized websites can provide superior UX by understanding niche domain needs.
- **Use concrete predictable labels** — Avoid vague labels like "Item Type" or "Format." Use specific labels that users can predict contain relevant options, such as "Product Categories" or "Genres" for movies.
- **Eliminate jargon from filter values** — Users shouldn't need internal knowledge to understand filter options. Avoid using internal color codes or abbreviations without clear explanations.
- **Prioritize filters by relevance** — Place general, high-level filters at the top and specific ones at bottom, unless specific characteristics are particularly influential to user choice for your domain.
- **Research user language and priorities** — Use interviews, surveys, search logs, and analytics to understand which characteristics matter most to users and what words they use to describe them.

## Quotes

> When well-designed, they create a positive feeling of control and choice, even in the face of overwhelming numbers of options.

> Filters are useless if people don't understand what they mean.

> Relevance to users is the ultimate prioritization criterion.
