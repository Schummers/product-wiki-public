# How to Deal With Bad Design Suggestions

## Summary

This article addresses the common situation where designers receive unsolicited design suggestions from executives, colleagues, and stakeholders. Rather than dismissing ideas outright, the article advocates for a thoughtful process that separates the underlying problem the suggestion addresses from the proposed solution. The key steps include: losing ego and acknowledging that non-designers may have valuable perspectives, hearing out the suggestion fully, identifying the core problem being addressed, assessing both benefits and costs of the proposed solution, conducting research and exploration, and communicating rejection respectfully when warranted. For powerful stakeholders, the article recommends using aikido-like principles to explore alternatives that achieve the same goals without implementing the problematic idea. The ultimate goal is to educate colleagues and colleagues about UX principles while preserving team relationships and channeling contributions productively.

## Key Takeaways

- **Separate Solution from Problem** — A poorly proposed solution may address a legitimate underlying problem worth solving; ask what the person is trying to achieve to distinguish the valuable problem from the flawed solution.
- **Acknowledge Both Benefits and Costs** — Even bad ideas have some benefit; evaluate trade-offs honestly by recognizing both the appeal and the usability problems of the proposed solution, helping stakeholders understand design complexity.
- **Research and Buy Time** — Rather than saying "no" immediately, offer to research the idea, review usability guidelines, and explore alternatives; this builds trust and allows solutions that meet stakeholders' goals without implementing the problematic design.
- **Educate Through Rejection** — When rejecting an idea, explain the reasoning based on UX research and principles rather than simply saying "no"; this turns rejection into a learning opportunity and builds UX knowledge across the organization.
- **Aikido Approach for Authority Figures** — When powerful stakeholders make demands, agree to explore the suggestion and present alternatives that achieve their underlying goals without implementing the problematic design; avoid direct rejection when it's politically risky.
- **Prevent Issues Through Early Input** — Seek design input from stakeholders at scheduled, strategic times; this reduces surprise requests and allows structured input that is more productive than late-stage demands.

## Quotes

> "Diversity of perspectives leads to more effective ideation: even if those making the suggestions have little design knowledge, they may have a different kind of expertise or experience which you lack, and may bring a valuable perspective to the problem you are trying to solve."

> "You may find that, while the solution being proposed isn't a good fit, the problem addressed is important."

> "The moment people are engaged enough to make a suggestion is the ideal time to help them learn more about UX and spread more UX knowledge throughout your team."

## Concepts

- [[Storytelling]] — How designers explain and justify design decisions to non-designers; effective communication acknowledges others' contributions and educates stakeholders about UX principles rather than dismissing their input.
- [[Stakeholder Engagement]] — Navigating input and pressure from people with power and influence over design decisions; requires political skill, relationship-building, and strategic timing of research and alternatives.
- [[Design Trade-off]] — Most design decisions involve balancing competing goals; understanding and articulating trade-offs helps stakeholders recognize why the proposed solution may not be optimal despite its appeal.
- [[Design Advocacy]] — The role of designers in championing user needs against pressure for other goals; effective advocacy uses research, education, and collaboration rather than authority or rejection.
- [[Team Collaboration]] — Involving multiple perspectives in design decisions; when managed well, diverse input improves outcomes; when mismanaged, it becomes a source of conflict and poor decisions.
