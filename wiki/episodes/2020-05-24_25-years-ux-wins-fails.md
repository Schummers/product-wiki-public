# Biggest Wins and Fails in 25 Years of UX Columns

## Summary

After publishing 1,330 UX articles over 25 years, Jakob Nielsen reviewed his first 250 articles (1995-2001) to assess which predictions remained true and which proved wrong. Ten major successes proved durable: response time importance, how users read on the web, search as primary navigation, banner blindness, bandwidth growth (Nielsen's Law), user conservatism, WAP rejection, mobile device design, animation critique, and PDF usability issues. Seven major failures included predictions about file system obsolescence, semantic web encoding, web-based loyalty programs, increasing returns, reputation managers, micropayments, and request marketing. Nielsen notes he was generally right when conservative (relying on user resistance to change) but wrong when optimistic about systemic change. The analysis reinforces that user behavior patterns driven by fundamental human perception remain remarkably stable over decades.

## Key Takeaways

- **Response time matters more than ever** — the 1997 finding that fast sites have higher conversion rates remains true in 2020, with current targets of 1 second for great usability and 10 seconds maximum wait still valid.
- **User conservatism persists** — people prefer simplicity and don't care about latest technology, making staying a few years behind the curve optimal for usability, a principle as true in 2020 as 1998.
- **Search as primary interface endures** — the 1997 prediction that search should be global, always available, and user-controlled has held true through 25 years of web evolution.
- **Banner blindness and format blindness are real** — users ignore ads and ad-like elements, a phenomenon repeatedly confirmed across decades despite changing ad formats and technologies.
- **Optimistic predictions fail; conservative predictions succeed** — Nielsen's pattern shows he was right about user resistance to change but wrong about business model evolution and systemic improvements.
- **Small-N testing remains valid** — the finding that qualitative usability testing with 5 users generates significant learnings continues to prove true in practice.

## Quotes

> "I was mainly right when I was being conservative: when I relied on users being resistant toward fancy technology and preferring simplicity. It's striking how many of these long-lasting insights were based on a small base of qualitative user research."

> "Conversely, I mainly failed when I was being optimistic and expecting big changes. Complex systems, such as the internet and the business ecosystem, are hard to move and often resist improvements that seem valuable but are too hard to make happen."

> "specificity easily makes predictions wrong, even when the general thrust is right"
