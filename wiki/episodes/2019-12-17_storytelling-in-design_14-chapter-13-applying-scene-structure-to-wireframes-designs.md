# Storytelling in Design: Chapter 13. Applying Scene Structure to Wireframes, Designs, and Prototypes

## Summary

This chapter translates scene structure from traditional storytelling into the design of pages and views. Dahlström argues that each page tells its own three-act story with an opening (above the fold), middle, and closing scene, and that the entire product experience is made up of interconnected scenes. The chapter provides seven practical methods for defining and testing page stories: defining purpose, identifying first-time and returning user needs, estimating time-on-page, prioritizing content across devices, defining content flow, structuring CTAs, and using user feedback to evaluate narrative coherence. Wireframes and prototypes serve as screenplays and storyboards—tools for communicating the intended story to teams and stakeholders before visual design or development.

## Key Takeaways

- **Every page is a scene with a beginning, a middle and an end** — Borrowing novelist C.S. Lakin's guidelines (opening scenes set up the promise and are loaded with character, middle scenes carry the conflict and raise the stakes, climactic scenes build to a riveting climax), Dahlström treats what is above the fold as the page's opening scene, the one that sets expectations. Below the fold is the main content of the story, the middle and closing scenes the user reaches by scrolling; the closing scene should provide a strong ending with a clear link to the next scene, not fade away. The order and hierarchy of information shape whether the user perceives a coherent story or confusion.

- **Two competing lists of scene elements, and Dahlström picks one** — Author Jane Friedman refers to seven layers in a scene: time/setting, dramatic action, conflict/tension, character, the protagonist's goal, character change or emotional development, and thematic significance. Ali Luke has another take. Dahlström says both are good reference points, but that Luke's elements have the closest connection to product design and can be almost directly applied: the characters are your main user, the one about to take action or no action; the dialogue is the brand messaging; the surroundings description is the accompanying messaging and content that gives enough context for the user to decide.

- **Purpose-driven page design starts with one or two sentences** — According to Jerry Jenkins's blog, one of the first things to do when starting a scene is to define its purpose in one or two sentences, and to throw out any scene you cannot find a purpose for. Dahlström does the same before sketching or wireframing, starting from "The purpose of this page/view is to…". If you struggle to pinpoint just what the purpose is, there is more work to be done, either to define it or perhaps to rethink the whole page.

- **Identify needs separately for first-time and returning users** — Ali Luke advises writing three to seven bullet points describing what will happen in a scene, so there is neither too much nor too little in it; Dahlström transposes this into two columns under the purpose statement, "As a first-time user, I should be able to…" and "As a returning visitor, I should be able to…". Writing the content down shows that what seemed clear in our heads usually is not, and the exercise works retrospectively on a page already built.

- **Estimate time-on-page as a checkpoint for coherence** — Being specific about what a good duration is for a new versus a returning visitor gives another checkpoint on whether the page supports the user's journey or distracts from it. Dahlström recommends keeping it a ballpark figure rather than trying to pinpoint exact milliseconds, and combining analytics with tools such as Hotjar, which show where users look and how they scan the page, to assess what story the page is currently telling, perhaps nondeliberately.

- **Choreograph content flow across devices before wireframing** — Prioritize desktop or mobile content first, then sketch how modules reflow at breakpoints. Content stacking defines the priority order; something that doesn't need to be on mobile probably doesn't belong on desktop either. Dahlström holds mobile first to be critical for how we develop designs, but not for how we think through content and layout: she personally starts with the most complex screen, usually desktop, and says she would challenge anyone insisting wireframes be done mobile first without a very good reason. What matters is working both screen sizes in conjunction rather than completing one full set before the other.

- **Primary and secondary CTAs tell the story through navigation** — Dahlström relays Mark Bell's idea that a well-designed website needs no main navigation at all, users navigating instead through the content and story of the page itself; contextual navigation is an often overlooked opportunity, and the result of neglecting it is that users fall back to the main navigation or to search. Primary CTAs advance the main plot; secondary CTAs and related-content links enable exploration of subplots. Too many options confuse; the page should guide the user through the narrative.

## Concepts

- [[Scene Structure]] — Pages and views are scenes: each has an opening (above the fold that sets expectations), a middle (body content with conflict and clarity), and a closing (below the fold with a strong ending and link to the next scene). Wireframes and prototypes are tools for defining this structure, just as screenplays define scene structure in film.

- [[Wireframing]] — Wireframes are screenplays, not finished products. They communicate hierarchy, content placement, and intended length to designers, developers, and stakeholders—not prescriptive visual layouts. A good wireframe answers: what content goes where, in what order, and why, without dictating colors or styling.

- [[Prototyping]] — Prototypes test interactions and flows, revealing whether the narrative structure works. They serve both as tools for team iteration and as deliverables for stakeholders to experience the intended story before development. Clickable prototypes can visualize journeys and test whether all branches of the narrative remain coherent.

- [[Design Presentation]] — The story of the page must be communicated to teams, clients, and stakeholders before visual design or build. By grounding presentations in purpose statements, user needs, and narrative structure rather than aesthetics, designers help audiences understand why content is arranged as it is and whether the story works.

- [[Content Strategy]] — Content must be sequenced and prioritized to deliver signal without noise. Each page needs a clear purpose, identified content needs for different user types, and a defined flow across devices. State, context, and user familiarity drive what content should appear where.

- [[Visual Hierarchy]] — Visual hierarchy (how design draws attention to certain parts of the page) shapes how the user perceives the story. Dahlström cites Xinyi Chen of the Nielsen Norman Group, for whom "relevant information is 'signal,' while irrelevant information is 'noise,'" and sets a high signal-to-noise ratio in content, page design and CTAs as the goal for any UX or visual designer. Testing whether a wireframe tells a story when stripped of styling reveals whether the hierarchy is sound or if design is compensating for unclear structure.
