# Problem Statements in UX Discovery

## Summary

A problem statement is a concise description of the problem that needs solving. Before beginning discovery research, teams should identify and frame the problem; a well-articulated problem statement provides direction, creates alignment, and prevents discovery from meandering. Problem statements answer the five Ws: Who is affected, What is the problem, Where it occurs, When it occurs, and Why it matters. Effective problem statements include background (the problem and its origin), people affected (with multiple user groups if relevant), and organizational impact (reputational, financial, or operational consequences). Opportunity statements use the same format but frame the problem as a positive opportunity rather than a deficit.

## Key Takeaways

- **Scoping Device** — Problem statements focus teams on specific problems to explore, making clear what is in scope and what is out of scope; they prevent discovery from tackling too many unrelated issues.
- **Communication Tool** — Well-written problem statements gain stakeholder buy-in on why exploration is important; they communicate to leadership and teams why a problem merits investigation and resources.
- **The Five Ws Framework** — Answering Who, What, Where, When, and Why provides a structured approach to developing problem statements; teams may not know all answers upfront (especially root causes), but discovery fills those gaps.
- **Three Required Elements** — Effective problem statements include the problem background and context, the people affected (internal and external), and the organizational impact (financial, reputational, operational consequences).
- **Opportunity vs. Problem Framing** — Problem statements can highlight deficits ("users struggle to do X") or opportunities ("there's potential to make X faster and easier"); both are structured the same way and serve as discovery starting points.

## Quotes

> If you don't know the problem, you're not going to have much luck solving it!

> A discovery effort should have one problem statement, and the problem statement should be focused on one problem.

> Problem statements are effective when they're concise.

## Concepts

- [[Problem Framing]] — Defining the problem is a critical first step before design work; problem statements articulate what needs to be solved, who is affected, and why it matters, creating focus for downstream research and design.
- [[Product Discovery]] — Discovery is the research phase that explores problems, uncovers root causes, identifies needs, and informs subsequent design work; problem statements guide this exploratory work by establishing scope and direction.
- [[Stakeholder Engagement]] — Problem statements serve as communication tools to align internal stakeholders (leadership, teams, departments) on why a problem is worth investigating and what resources it merits.
