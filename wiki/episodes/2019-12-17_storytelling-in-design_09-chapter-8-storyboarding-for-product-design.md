# Storytelling in Design: Chapter 8. Storyboarding for Product Design

## Summary

This chapter adapts storyboarding, a foundational film and animation technique, for product design. Dahlström argues that experience maps are underutilized tools for capturing end-to-end user journeys, and storyboards complement them by bringing narrative to life visually. The chapter traces storyboarding from Walt Disney Studios (sketches of frames from the 1920s, storyboards popularized in their modern form in the 1930s) through contemporary product teams at Airbnb and The Atlantic, then moves into how storyboarding helps designers spot invisible problems and opportunities that linear processes miss.

## Key Takeaways

- **Experience maps visualize the full end-to-end experience** — Experience maps show not just what happens on screen but offline aspects and how changes ripple across the journey; the Rail Europe Experience Map (courtesy of Chris Risdon and Adaptive Path) was developed as part of an overall diagnostic evaluation so the company could understand customer journeys across touch points and decide where to focus design, development and budget. Of experience maps in general, Risdon says they should be seen as a catalyst, "something that is actionable and will get people talking."
- **Storyboards originated in Disney's animation process** — Disney Studios had used sketches of frames since the 1920s and made storyboards popular in their modern form in the 1930s; separately, Disney was the first studio to have a specialist story department apart from the animators, introduced because Disney had realized audiences would not care about a film unless the story gave them reason to care about the characters. Storyboards allow directors, cinematographers, and clients to visualize scenes and identify problems before production begins.
- **In product design, storyboards reveal the offline-online dance** — Dahlström cites Tony Fadell on habituation: humans encode everyday behaviors into habits to free brain capacity, but this habituation can blind us to invisible problems; storyboards force designers to think narratively about the full experience, which led Airbnb to recognize that its service is not a website but an offline experience.
- **Tony Fadell's framework for noticing invisible problems** — Fadell advises: first, look broader at the steps leading up to and after a problem; second, look closer at tiny details and ask if they are important or simply inherited; third, think younger and ask optimistic questions (e.g., why does a mailbox not tell us when there's mail?).
- **Storyboards combine character, script, scene, and plot** — Ben Crothers recommends four elements, inspired by Aristotle's seven golden rules of storytelling: the persona the narrative is about (character), inner dialogue and what others say (script), the scenario (scene), and the unfolding narrative toward the goal (plot); the environment and solution can be added.
- **Three ways to incorporate storyboards** — Storyboards can be standalone deliverables, elements within customer journey maps, or part of experience maps; Crothers advises that when a storyboard is used as a communication tool, be clear on what it communicates — solve an existing user experience problem, identify the impact of an existing situation or issue, or define a desired user experience for a solution — and Dahlström adds that, in her opinion, the same three scenarios apply when the storyboard is an ideation or thinking tool.

## Quotes

> One of the great benefits, and opportunities if used correctly, is that experience maps are brilliant as a do-once-and-keep-on-using kind of document. They are living and breathing documents that the team and project can derive great value from by constantly evaluating and improving, not to mention use as a reference point throughout the project as various aspects are being worked through.

> Just as in theater, film, and animatics, through storyboarding you start to explore the world in which the experience will take place, and you start to explore the structure.

> Our job, Fadell says, is to go one step further and not just notice the problems, but fix them.

## Concepts

- [[Storyboarding]] — Dahlström demonstrates that storyboarding brings narrative to life and helps teams explore both the world of the experience and its structure; in product design, it serves multiple roles—as a thinking tool to spot gaps and opportunities, as a communication tool for buy-in, and as a deliverable in its own right or as part of journey or experience maps.
- [[Experience Map]] — The chapter emphasizes that experience maps should be living documents, continuously evaluated and improved; they are most valuable when printed large and visible to the team, showing how everything is intricately linked and how a change at one end impacts something elsewhere.
- [[Customer Journey]] — Dahlström argues that storyboarding is equally valuable for customer journey mapping as it is for experience mapping; storyboards within a journey map add visual narrative and emotional flow, helping teams see the experience through the user's eyes.
- [[Design Presentation]] — Among the reasons Crothers lists for storyboards, they are an effective communication tool "that almost anyone understands and can engage with"; Dahlström adds that how complex an experience map looks to people is a matter of visual presentation and of how you talk through it.
- [[Empathy]] — Storyboarding helps create empathy and understanding within teams and with clients by bringing personas and their journeys to life through drawing; the act of drawing itself lowers people's guards and creates space for "what if..." thinking.
- [[Shared Understanding]] — By collaborating on storyboards and experience maps, teams break down organizational silos and build buy-in; Dahlström stresses that different parts of the business must come together to create products that truly incorporate user insight and business requirements.
