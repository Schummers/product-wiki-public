# Using the Microsoft Desirability Toolkit to Test Visual Appeal

## Summary

The Microsoft Desirability Toolkit (also called the Microsoft Reaction Card Method) is a structured methodology for measuring users' emotional and aesthetic responses to interfaces. Developed in 2002, it provides a controlled vocabulary of product-reaction words that participants use to describe designs, reducing the variability and ambiguity of open-ended feedback. The method can be adapted to focus on visual appeal by selecting and curating a subset of words (around 25 or fewer for online surveys) that address aesthetic dimensions while including negative, positive, and neutral terms.

## Key Takeaways

- **Controlled vocabulary reduces variability** — providing a predefined set of reaction words ensures consistent terminology across participants and simplifies data analysis compared to free-form comments.
- **Adapt the word list to your goals** — include words you expect users to use and that align with study objectives; remove words unrelated to aesthetics when focusing on visual appeal.
- **Balance positive, negative, and neutral words** — at least 40% of words should be negative or neutral to avoid bias toward flattering responses and capture genuine reactions.
- **Keep lists short for online surveys** — use 25 words or fewer in online contexts to reduce respondent fatigue and maintain completion rates.
- **Randomize word order** — presenting words in random order reduces lazy selection of the first five words and improves response quality.
- **Show screenshots without interaction** — reduce distractions from functionality and content by presenting design mockups rather than live, interactive interfaces.

## Quotes

> A major benefit of this methodology is that it introduces a controlled vocabulary for participants. The variability in word choice that naturally occurs during free-form qualitative evaluation can be problematic for data analysis.

> Include words that you expect your users to use for describing the interface, as well as words that would be relevant for your study goals.

> Your results won't be useful if you let participants choose only flattering words to describe your UI.
