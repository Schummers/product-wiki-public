# Distracted Driving: UX's Responsibility to Do No Harm

## Summary

UX designers have a responsibility to consider that their products may be used while driving, even if that is not the intended use case. Rather than designing specifically to facilitate distracted driving, designers should make choices based on known UX guidelines that reduce the dangers of people using devices while driving. This includes reducing cognitive load, using strong signifiers, providing good defaults, and carefully considering gamification and addictive design patterns. The article frames this as part of a broader UX professional responsibility to do no harm, similar to the Hippocratic Oath in medicine.

## Key Takeaways

- **Don't overload working memory** — Weak signifiers, unclear labels, and confusing conventions increase cognitive load and time on task, which is especially dangerous when driving. Use strong visual signals for interactive elements.
- **Reduce interaction cost with good defaults** — Context-aware defaults can significantly reduce the interactions needed to complete top tasks while driving, such as searching for nearby restaurants based on current route.
- **Use glanceable typography** — Larger, wider, and capitalized text outperforms smaller text for quick reading while distracted, supporting rapid comprehension of critical information.
- **Question addictive design patterns** — Avoid excessive gamification, constant notifications, and reward systems designed to encourage habitual app use, which increase the likelihood of using products while driving.
- **Apply harm-reduction principle** — Even if people shouldn't use products while driving, they will. Design choices that reduce distraction severity through clearer interfaces and simpler interactions can make a difference.

## Quotes

> If our users are safely sitting at their desks or on their couches, the cognitive strain that results from lack of usability may seem just a minor inconvenience, but if they're driving at 55 miles per hour, a design that requires an extra 5 seconds to decipher means they could have traveled the length of a football field with their eyes off the road.

> As a first step, I urge you to consider your own design priorities and choices in the same way that responsible physicians do when they take the Hippocratic Oath, saying "first, do no harm."

> Our users are constantly distracted. Shouldn't we be considering these distractions if we're truly doing context-sensitive, human-centered design?

## Concepts

- [[Distracted Driving]] — The article addresses the problem of mobile device use while driving and calls for UX professionals to apply harm-reduction design principles that reduce (without eliminating) dangers of using products in this high-risk context.
- [[Cognitive Load]] — Weak signifiers, confusing interfaces, and wordiness all increase cognitive load, which is particularly dangerous when users are distracted by driving and have limited mental resources to decipher unclear designs.
- [[Responsible Design]] — The article frames ethical design as a professional responsibility, drawing parallels to the Hippocratic Oath and arguing that UX professionals must consider real-world contexts including dangerous situations beyond intended use cases.
