# User Story Mapping: Read This First

## Summary

Patton presents two essential concepts that shape the entire book: the goal of using stories is not to write better stories, but to achieve shared understanding; and the goal of product development is not to make products, but to change the world through outcomes. He frames the problem through the telephone game metaphor and real-world failures like the NASA Mars Climate Orbiter crash (caused by mixing metric and imperial units) to show that written documents consistently fail to create shared understanding. Instead, shared understanding emerges from conversation where people externalize ideas through words, pictures, sticky notes, and sketches, discovering where they differ and building a common understanding through dialogue; Patton credits the four-frame cartoon he uses to make that point to a former coworker, Luke Barrett, who himself did not remember where he had first seen it. Patton defines the core model: moving from the current world (where people are unhappy) through ideas and requirements to future outcomes (people's lives changing for the better). He distinguishes output (what we build) from outcome (how people use it to reach goals) and impact (long-term business benefits), crediting the clear language separating output from outcome to a talk by Robert Fabricant, "Behavior Is Our Medium." He argues that the real job is to build less (minimize output) while maximizing outcome and impact, which requires focusing on specific customers and users rather than trying to please everyone. Finally, Patton reframes "requirements" not as specifications but as ideas to solve problems, warning that the word requirements often stops conversations about people and their needs.

## Key Takeaways

- **Shared understanding requires conversation, not documents** — Written documents, no matter how well-crafted, are interpreted differently by different readers; shared understanding emerges only from dialogue with externalized thinking (sketches, pictures, sticky notes).
- **Output, outcome, and impact are different** — The output/outcome distinction is one Patton says was first made clear to him by Robert Fabricant's talk "Behavior Is Our Medium." Output is what we build; outcome is how people use it to reach their goals and change their lives; impact is the long-term business benefit. Focus conversations on outcome and impact, not output.
- **Build less and maximize outcome** — Since there is always more to build than time or resources allow, the real job is to minimize output while maximizing outcome and impact by focusing on specific customer and user problems, not trying to please everyone.
- **Requirements end conversations** — Patton emphasizes that "requirements" often function to stop dialogue about people and their needs; instead, think of specifications as ideas to solve specific problems for specific people. He notes he is paraphrasing the way Kent Beck cautions against misuse of the term in *Extreme Programming Explained*.

## Quotes

> Shared documents aren't shared understanding.

> The real goal of using stories is shared understanding.

> Minimize output, and maximize outcome and impact.

## Concepts

- [[Shared Understanding]] — Patton establishes shared understanding as the true goal of stories and story conversations, achieved through collaborative dialogue and externalized thinking, not through written documents or specifications.
- [[User Story]] — Patton redefines stories as a mechanism for conversation and building shared understanding about solving problems for customers and users, fundamentally different from a requirements writing format.
- [[Product Outcome]] — Patton introduces outcome as the central strategic concept, in language he credits to Robert Fabricant: how people's lives change as a result of using what we build, distinct from output (what we build) and impact (business benefit), and positions outcome as the measure that matters most.
