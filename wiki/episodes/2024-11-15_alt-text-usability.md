# Alt Text: Not Always Needed

## Summary

While alt text is important for web accessibility, not every image requires alt text. The article argues that adding alt text to redundant images — images whose information is already communicated elsewhere on the page — can actually harm the user experience for screen-reader users by increasing interaction cost and wasting their time and attention. The focus must be on usability, not just accessibility: the real goal is enabling assistive-technology users to accomplish their tasks easily, just like sighted users do.

## Key Takeaways

- **Screen-reader users prioritize function over form** — Screen-reader users do not navigate digital devices the way sighted users do; they do not rely on visual information and are not interested in designers' attempts to recreate visual experiences for them.
- **Alt text should communicate purpose, not appearance** — Screen-reader users want to know what role an image plays in completing their task, not what it looks like; focusing on description over function wastes screen-reader users' time.
- **Redundant images often don't need alt text** — Images that repeat information already communicated in page copy can increase interaction cost for screen-reader users without providing additional value.
- **Three-question decision tree** — Determine whether alt text is needed by asking: (1) Is the information repetitive? (2) Is the image referenced by page copy? (3) Could alt text help users complete tasks more efficiently?
- **Quality page copy is often the key** — Well-written page content is more important than alt text for making web content accessible; alt text supplements page copy as needed rather than being the primary accessibility mechanism.
- **Automatically generated alt text can harm usability** — Tools that promise to automatically generate alt text for all images are not making things easier for users and may add unnecessary interaction cost.

## Quotes

> **Screen-reader users navigate digital devices differently**— they do not rely on visual information, which means they are not interested in designers' attempts to recreate visual experiences for them.

> Most of the time, screen-reader users don't wonder what images look like. Instead, they want to know their purpose.

> If screen-reader users can skip interacting with images, they will. Adding alt text to every image does not automatically make webpage content easy to navigate and consume.

## Concepts

- [[Alt Text]] — Provides a nuanced perspective on when alt text is appropriate, arguing against universal alt text on all images and instead advocating for context-dependent decisions.
- [[Accessibility]] — Focusing purely on accessibility (alt text for every image) without considering usability (whether screen-reader users actually need that information) fails to achieve the real goal of making the web usable for all; screen-reader users navigate and process web pages sequentially, fundamentally different from sighted users' visual scanning patterns.
- [[Interaction Cost]] — Demonstrates how redundant alt text increases the interaction cost for screen-reader users by requiring them to listen to information they already have access to elsewhere on the page.
- [[Task-Based Design]] — Emphasizes designing for users' actual tasks rather than adhering to accessibility rules; the question is whether alt text enables task completion, not whether every image technically has alt text.
