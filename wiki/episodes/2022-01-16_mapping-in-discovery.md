# Why Map in Discovery: 3 Mapping Methods

## Summary

During discovery, teams learn many new things about the problem space at a fast pace, making it challenging to manage information volume and make findings actionable. Mapping creates shared understanding and helps manage complexity by breaking down large problems into smaller chunks while highlighting key actors, causal links, and root causes. Humans have mapped for thousands years for wayfinding, orientation, and complexity management. In UX discovery, maps leverage the picture-superiority effect (people remember visual information better than text) and expose gaps and unknowns that teams need to address before making key decisions. Nielsen Norman Group recommends three commonly used mapping methods: ecosystem maps, chronological maps, and process maps.

## Key Takeaways

- **Ecosystem maps reveal stakeholders and relationships** — Hub-and-spoke or concentric-circle designs show people and things connected to a central user, with distance representing metaphorical closeness; useful at the beginning of discovery when there are many unknowns about domain complexity.
- **Chronological maps capture experience over time** — Experience maps, service blueprints, and user-journey maps visualize different perspectives of experiences over time; each answers different discovery questions (new products/services, business process problems, customer experience improvements).
- **Process maps zoom in on specific workflows** — More granular than chronological maps, process maps (flowcharts, sequence diagrams, HTA diagrams) detail how a given process works; created later in discovery when a specific critical area has been isolated.
- **Visual format improves memory and retention** — The picture-superiority effect shows people remember and retain information better when presented visually rather than in text-based format.
- **Mapping should be collaborative and iterative** — Start with low-fidelity maps (flipchart paper and sticky notes) to signal that maps will change as learning progresses; schedule team sessions to update maps as research evolves; digitize or finalize only at end of discovery.
- **Three mapping types can be used together** — Different maps serve different discovery purposes; selecting the one that aligns most closely with discovery goals keeps the effort focused rather than overwhelming.

## Quotes

> "Discoveries can be messy because there are many unknowns, which often necessitate the use of exploratory research methods, such as field studies, interviews, and diary studies."

> "Mapping forces creators to ask questions such as: What happens after this? What's the process here? How does this event affect users or our business? These questions can expose important unknowns that a team needs to learn before it can move forward with making key decisions."

> "Mapping doesn't have to be done in one sitting. In fact, mapping should be an ongoing process during discovery. Start a map, even if you don't have much information. Keep it low-fidelity — such as a flipchart paper and stickies on a wall — to set expectations and communicate to others that it's supposed to change as you learn new things."

## Concepts

- [[Product Discovery]] — During discovery, mapping helps manage the volume of new learnings about the problem space and makes findings actionable by creating shared understanding.
- [[Ecosystem Mapping]] — A visual representation using hub-and-spoke or concentric-circle design showing people, organizations, products, and services a user may interact with; most useful at the beginning of discovery.
- [[Journey Mapping]] — A chronological map visualizing the process a user goes through to accomplish a goal; helps locate opportunities to improve the customer experience of a specific product or service.
- [[Process Mapping]] — A zoomed-in, detailed visualization (flowchart, sequence diagram) of how a specific process works; created later in discovery when critical areas have been isolated for investigation.
