# Team Members Behaving Badly During Usability Tests

## Summary

Involving stakeholders and team members in usability testing is essential for fostering collaboration and buy-in, but sometimes they behave in ways that violate research ethics or good testing practices. Well-intentioned requests and behaviors can undermine study validity and participant welfare. UX researchers have an obligation to handle these situations delicately while upholding ethical standards. Common ethical lapses include forcing participants to continue, lying to participants, rescinding payment for poor-fit participants, allowing bosses to observe subordinates, team defensiveness, and name-calling. The article provides guidance for handling each scenario while maintaining ethical research standards and research quality.

## Key Takeaways

- **Participants must be free to leave** — Users must always be allowed to take breaks or leave at any time; coercing participation is unethical and likely illegal; continued participation from unengaged users taints findings and violates dignity principle.
- **Transparency over white lies** — Don't lie about observers, one-way mirrors, or recording; white lies seem harmless but erode trust; instead acknowledge that usability testing feels unnatural and be transparent about setup.
- **Pay participants regardless** — Regardless of recruitment errors resulting in poor-fit participants, pay the full incentive; participants invested time and effort showing up, and payment errors undermine future recruitment.
- **Separate bosses from subordinates** — Don't allow managers to observe their direct reports' sessions; employees fear judgment and performance impact; anonymize findings and ensure no linkage to performance reviews.
- **Set observer etiquette rules** — Remind observers that usability testing is about receiving feedback, not giving it; establish that observers remain silent except at session end; include this expectation in participant introduction.
- **Limit observers to improve comfort** — Restrict in-room observers to 3 maximum; users feel less outnumbered and more comfortable; broadcast sessions to a separate room if more observers needed; ensure participants know observers beforehand.
- **Reframe failures as interface problems** — When users struggle, attribute it to the interface design, not user incompetence; help teams stop name-calling and focus on what the interface failed to communicate.
- **Ethical standards protect research** — Adhering to codes of conduct (avoid harm, respect dignity, act with integrity, maintain confidentiality) protects participants, the profession, and the organization from legal issues.

## Quotes

> Involving stakeholders and team members in usability testing is essential in order to foster collaboration and buy-in.

> A person who is no longer interested in participating should never be coerced in staying because doing so is unethical (and probably illegal).

> Help team members get in the habit of reframing conversations to what is wrong with the interface, not what is wrong with the user.
