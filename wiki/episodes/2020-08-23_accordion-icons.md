# Accordion Icons: Which Signifiers Work Best?

## Summary

This quantitative study tested four icon types as signifiers for accordions in mobile navigation menus (caret, plus, right-facing arrow, and no icon) plus a foil control icon to identify which best signals that content will expand in place. The caret proved the safest icon choice for indicating accordions. The study also found that users tap fairly equally on icons and text labels, making split-button designs (where icon and label have different functions) unsafe. Users strongly dislike right-facing arrows as accordion signifiers and prefer not to have ambiguous or no icons.

## Key Takeaways

- **Caret as optimal choice** — The downward-facing caret significantly outperformed other options in conveying that content would stay on the page and expand. Users showed a statistically significant tendency to tap on the caret icon more than on text labels.
- **Avoid split buttons** — Users tap fairly equally on both icons and labels for most standard icons (except the caret). This means assigning different functionality to the icon versus the label is unsafe; users expect both to trigger the same action.
- **Arrow risks** — The right-facing arrow was not statistically different from no icon or foil icons at conveying accordion function. Users did not necessarily expect to stay on the page or leave it, contrary to designer assumptions.
- **Icon importance** — Users tend to interact with strong, clear signifiers. When no icon was present, users were much more likely to tap on text labels than on empty space, showing preference for obvious signifiers.
- **Plus icon performance** — Plus icons perform adequately at conveying accordion function but are not significantly better than having no icon at indicating the expectation to stay on the page.
- **Standard icon advantage** — Making up new icons or using no signifiers at all violates user expectations. Using standard signifiers (caret, plus) conveys clear accordion intent better than foil or no-icon conditions.

## Quotes

> Users tend to click fairly equally on both the accordion icon and the accordion label, so avoid dissociating those by assigning them different functionalities.

> For none of the icon conditions was there a statistically significant tendency to tap on the icon more than on the label except for the caret (p<0.01) — in other words, when the accordion was signaled by a caret, people tended to tap more on the icon than on the label.

> For an accordion, where we want to convey that the page won't change, the rate should ideally be under 50%.

## Concepts

- [[Icon Design]] — Icon choice matters significantly for signaling interaction patterns. The caret icon is specifically effective at indicating accordion expansion on mobile, while other icons like arrows may confuse users about whether they'll stay on the same page.
- [[Accordion UI]] — Accordions are essential tools on mobile for collapsing content, but their signifiers must clearly indicate that content expands in place rather than navigating away. The caret provides the strongest signal of this pattern.
- [[Affordance]] — Clear, recognizable signifiers are critical for helping users understand what will happen when they interact with UI elements. Users expect icon and label to work together rather than having separate functions.
