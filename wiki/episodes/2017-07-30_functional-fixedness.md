# Functional Fixedness Stops You From Having Innovative Ideas

## Summary

Functional fixedness is a cognitive bias driving people to use objects only in their traditional, intended ways. While this mental shortcut helps solve recurring problems quickly, it prevents seeing alternative solutions. Children show greater creative flexibility than adults; a classic experiment found 5-year-olds solved a candle-mounting puzzle faster than older children because they weren't fixated on a box's containment function. Functional fixedness strengthens with age and experience. To overcome it, designers should abstract problems to remove surface details, identify distantly related domains of expertise, and draw inspiration from these unrelated fields to generate novel solutions.

## Key Takeaways

- **Functional fixedness strengthens over time** — The more you practice using something one way, the harder it is to see alternatives. Experience that should broaden perspective instead narrows it through fixation on conventional uses.

- **Young children outperform adults in creative flexibility** — 5-year-olds see more uses for objects because they haven't yet internalized strict functional associations. As we age, functional fixedness increases, limiting innovative thinking.

- **Abstract problems to break fixation** — Reframe design problems by stripping away surface details and domain-specific language. This abstraction minimizes functional fixedness and reveals related fields where solutions might be found.

- **Look for inspiration in distant domains** — Problems reframed abstractly reveal structurally related but distant fields of expertise. A power-outlet spacing problem abstractly becomes a space-packing challenge; solutions come from contortionism, landscaping, or Japanese aesthetics.

- **Delay judgment to enable divergence** — During ideation, resist immediately evaluating ideas or jumping to solutions. First generate many options, consider unlikely alternatives, and only then converge on best approaches.
