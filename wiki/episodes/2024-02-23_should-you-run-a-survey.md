# Should You Run a Survey?

## Summary

Surveys are ubiquitous in UX research yet frequently misused, generating unreliable data despite being widely adopted. Three common myths lead to poor survey practice: the misconception that surveys are easy (survey methodology is complex and requires rigor), that large samples alone ensure reliability (flawed methodology cannot be rescued by sample size), and that surveys are gentler on customers than interviews (both are equally acceptable if done professionally). Surveys are uniquely positioned as the only quantitative, attitudinal research method—making them ideal for specific questions about user attitudes or satisfaction measured at scale. However, they are poorly suited for behavioral or qualitative research questions. The key to sound survey practice is matching research questions to methods: only use surveys for quantitative, attitudinal questions; use qualitative methods for understanding why, and behavioral methods for observing actions.

## Key Takeaways

- **Surveys are complex to do well** — small question wording changes significantly affect results; survey methodology combines quantitative and qualitative research complexity.
- **Large samples don't fix bad methodology** — designing valid surveys requires sufficient time and rigor; sample size alone cannot compensate for poor design.
- **Don't use surveys to avoid upsetting customers** — surveys are not gentler than interviews; researchers should have flexibility across methods with appropriate safeguards.
- **Surveys are quantitative and attitudinal** — this unique positioning makes them ideal when research questions ask "what percentage of users believe or feel X" but unsuitable for other research types.
- **Use the quantitative-qualitative and behavioral-attitudinal matrix** — this framework clarifies which method suits each research question type.
- **Surveys pair well with other methods** — combining surveys' quantitative insights with qualitative research findings provides convincing, multifaceted evidence for stakeholders.

## Quotes

> I usually recommend product teams (and most others honestly) to stay as far away from surveys as possible. I truly believe surveys are the hardest research method to do well (yet the easiest to launch in the next 10 minutes).

> Surveys are, in fact, the only method that can be categorized as both quantitative and attitudinal.

> Ensure research viability by using surveys to answer only research questions that are quantitative in nature and deal with attitudinal responses.
