# Articulating Design Decisions: Chapter 11. How Executives Can Help Designers

## Summary

Chapter 11 is written for stakeholders, executives, and non-designers working with design teams, offering practical guidance on how to support and collaborate with designers effectively. Greever proposes the Four "izes"—Realize (expertise), Prioritize (needs), Authorize (decisions), and Recognize (humanity)—as foundational principles. The chapter includes ten practical tips for stakeholder-designer collaboration, a design project checklist for establishing alignment at project start, and an argument that companies serious about design must place designers in executive leadership roles, not just at the team level.

## Key Takeaways

- **Recognize designer expertise** — Designers solve problems strategically, not merely "make things look nice"; they apply trained usability practices and generate solutions through iterative work that stakeholders don't see, so their recommendations deserve trust grounded in their domain knowledge.
- **Prioritize designer needs** — Designers require clear business requirements, timelines, technical access, stakeholder contact, and budget for usability research; equipping them removes blockers and allows them to start work immediately without delays caused by waiting for information.
- **Authorize quick decisions** — Projects move at the speed of decision-making, not the speed of talent; daily design reviews and fast decision cycles yield better results than weekly meetings or changing decisions; empowering designers to make calls (with accountability) creates momentum.
- **Recognize their humanity** — People perform best when recognized as human; building genuine relationships, showing interest in people beyond the project, and using kind, problem-focused language (not attacking the designer) creates an environment where designers thrive.
- **Use the design checklist** — Establishing vision, goals, metrics, user understanding, workflow cadence, technical requirements, and decision-makers at the beginning of a project makes projects move faster and produce a better-quality experience; Greever presents it as a flexible guide, not a fixed list.
- **Put a designer at the executive table** — Companies that consistently deliver great products do so because design leadership sits at the highest level; without designer executives, even talented design teams operate under constraints that prevent optimal solutions.

## Quotes

> You hired them and have them on your team for a reason: because they're experts in design and are better at it than you are.

> The main difference between a scrappy startup and a bureaucratic behemoth is how quickly they make decisions and move forward. It has little to do with talent, resources, or ideas, and everything to do with the team's momentum.

> The only way to truly incorporate design thinking into your company values, processes, and products is to put designers in positions of authority.

## Concepts

- [[Design Leadership]] — the chapter argues that design leadership must exist at executive and organizational levels, not just within design teams; without designers influencing strategy and decisions at the top, even skilled teams cannot optimize for user experience.
- [[Stakeholder Engagement]] — executives and stakeholders succeed with designers when they prepare in advance (reviewing work, formulating feedback), communicate clearly using problem-focused language, and treat the designer relationship as a partnership requiring ongoing engagement.
- [[Team Collaboration]] — daily or frequent design reviews, clear decision-making authority, and explicit task assignment (with names and deadlines) establish a rhythm that keeps projects moving; slow meeting cycles and committee decision-making kill momentum.
- [[Empathy]] — recognizing designers as people with lives beyond the project, showing genuine interest, and using kind language creates psychological safety and enables designers to take creative risks and propose innovative solutions.
- [[UX Maturity]] — the project checklist (covering vision, users, workflow, access, and technical requirements) ensures both stakeholders and designers are aligned at the outset, Greever presents it as a flexible guide rather than a fixed list, and reports using it with his own clients at the start of every project.
- [[UX Career]] — designers' ability to grow and thrive depends on having autonomy to make decisions, access to the executive suite, and recognition that their expertise is as valuable as finance or engineering; organizations that invest in these conditions attract and retain better talent.
