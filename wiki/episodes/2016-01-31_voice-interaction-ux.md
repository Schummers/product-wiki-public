# Voice Interaction UX: Brave New World...Same Old Story

## Summary

Amazon's Echo and other voice-controlled devices represent a new category of voice interaction systems distinct from smartphone-based systems like Siri and Google Now. While the Echo appears less capable than smartphone assistants, it demonstrates superior voice recognition due to hardware design (seven microphones vs. two) and a focused approach. Traditional usability heuristics remain applicable to voice interfaces, even when the modality differs from visual displays. The article examines how error prevention, flexibility, efficiency, and recognition vs. recall manifest in voice-only systems.

## Key Takeaways

- **Error prevention through hardware** — the Echo prioritizes voice recognition with specialized microphones and background-noise filtering, making it more reliable than smartphone systems in real-world conditions like noisy kitchens.
- **Focused scope improves usability** — while the Echo is "less smart" in absolute terms, its narrower functionality reduces error rates and semantic misinterpretation compared to Siri's bias toward general web searches.
- **Recognition over recall is harder without visual feedback** — voice-only interfaces constrain a core usability principle; users cannot see a list of available options and must rely on memory, making the system less usable than visual alternatives.
- **Visibility of system status is limited** — the Echo's animated light ring provides minimal feedback compared to rich textual and visual output from screen-based systems.
- **Usability principles transcend modality** — despite the shift from visual to auditory interaction, established principles about error prevention, efficiency, flexibility, and system status visibility still apply and matter to user success.
- **Task complexity determines appropriateness** — voice control works well for simple, short tasks (checking a timer) but becomes inefficient for repetitive or multi-step tasks (adding multiple items to a shopping list).

## Quotes

> Most usability principles have more to do with human capabilities and limitations than with technology.

> For short tasks, failing to hear a command the first time can easily tip the balance, and make the voice system more cumbersome and time consuming than an existing physical alternative, such as glancing at a digital timer or walking across the room to flip a light switch.

> No matter how different the technology, the people who are using it haven't changed.
