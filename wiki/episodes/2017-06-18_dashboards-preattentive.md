# Dashboards: Making Charts and Graphs Easier to Understand

## Summary

Dashboards communicate critical information at a glance without requiring deep analysis. They exist in two forms: operational dashboards for time-sensitive decisions and analytical dashboards for insight and strategy. Effective dashboards leverage preattentive visual processing, using attributes like length and 2D position that humans perceive quickly without conscious attention. Bar charts and line charts are more effective than pie charts, donut charts, or treemaps because they use these efficient preattentive features. 3D visualizations distort data and should be avoided.

## Key Takeaways

- **Use preattentive attributes for speed** — Leverage visual features users perceive without full attention: length (bars), 2D position (line charts, scatterplots), and color. These enable rapid understanding without cognitive effort.

- **Prefer linear over area-based charts** — Bar charts and line graphs excel at communicating quantities because they use length and position, both strong preattentive variables. Pie charts, donut charts, and treemaps use area and angle, which humans judge poorly.

- **Avoid 3D visualizations** — 3D renderings distort visual features, making it harder to compare values accurately. A 3D bar chart or pie chart obscures relationships more than the 2D equivalent.

- **Use color and shape for categories, not quantities** — Color and shape are preattentive but don't communicate magnitude; they work for grouping related items. Use them to reinforce relationships shown through position or proximity.

- **Distinguish operational from analytical dashboards** — Operational dashboards prioritize speed for time-sensitive decisions; analytical dashboards support thoughtful analysis. Both need single-screen, at-a-glance layouts.

## Quotes

> For example, in a display containing many lines of the same size, the one line that is longer will "pop out," without the user having to engage in a visual search to locate it.

> Although all preattentive features stand out easily, not all of them are equal. With some, people simply perceive a difference without being able to exactly pinpoint the size of the difference: for example, although we can effortlessly notice a large rectangle in a display containing many small rectangles, we are not good at judging how much bigger the large rectangle is, compared with the others.

> Pie charts (and their even less helpful cousin, donut charts) are notoriously poor at most information-communication tasks (other than simply showing overwhelming disparities in percentage shares, such as a single source of revenue that represents the vast majority of a company's profits), and should be avoided most of the time.
