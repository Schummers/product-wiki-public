# Recruiting High-Income Participants: Challenges and Tips

## Summary

Recruiting high-income individuals for user research presents unique challenges because they are difficult to contact, busy, and not motivated by typical financial incentives. Traditional recruitment panels undersample wealthy individuals because those most likely to register are seeking extra cash. This article shares strategies from a study on luxury-brand websites that successfully recruited high-income participants. Key approaches include using word-of-mouth referrals (which proved more effective than recruitment services), ensuring referrals don't feel like solicitation (by having existing contacts introduce the researcher), respecting privacy concerns, enabling remote participation, offering flexible scheduling, minimizing participant burden, and providing appealing incentives (higher amounts or charitable donations).

## Key Takeaways

- **Word-of-mouth recruitment is most effective** for high-income participants, particularly when existing contacts make introductions rather than cold outreach.
- **Avoid asking about income directly** as it can offend; instead identify participants by profession or shopping behavior.
- **Remote sessions are essential** because wealthy participants value time convenience and may have geographic constraints.
- **Flexible scheduling across weeks, evenings, and weekends** is necessary to accommodate demanding schedules.
- **Minimize presession demands** — avoid long surveys and allow consent forms to be signed during the session.
- **Higher financial incentives or charitable donations** motivate high-income participants more than standard study payments.

## Quotes

> Unfortunately, it's often much harder to find high-income participants through these services. This is due to a selection bias — those most likely to register with recruitment panels tend to be looking for some extra cash.

> Wealthy people are often wary of being asked for money, so it's important to ensure that potential participants don't feel harassed or suspicious of your motivations.

> My high-income participants appreciated the opportunity to give back to the causes they cared about.
