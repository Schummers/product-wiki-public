# Psychology for UX: Study Guide

## Summary

Bringing psychology and technology together is at the heart of UX design because UX is about people. Most psychological principles relevant to UX are easy to understand yet make significant differences when applied correctly. The best designs are built for people as they really are, not who designers wish they were. Key psychological areas for UX include attention (selective focus, change blindness), Gestalt principles (perception of groups), memory (recognition vs. recall), sensemaking (mental models, information foraging), decision-making (loss aversion, satisficing, choice overload), motor processes (interaction cost, Fitts's Law), motivation (autonomy, competence, relatedness), cognitive biases (anchoring, availability heuristic, confirmation bias), persuasion (social proof, scarcity, authority), and emotion (visceral, behavioral, reflective levels).

## Key Takeaways

- **Humans have limited attention** — tunnel vision and selective attention mean users focus narrowly; change blindness causes users to miss changes outside their focus area.
- **Gestalt principles describe how users perceive visual groupings** — proximity, similarity, common region, closure, and continuation determine how users interpret spatial relationships between elements.
- **Memory is limited and imperfect** — recognize over recall; working memory is strained by too much information; chunking, priming, and spatial memory support retention.
- **Cognitive biases lead to systematic deviations from rational thinking** — confirmation bias, anchoring, availability heuristic, negativity bias, and false-consensus effect predictably influence user judgment.
- **Interaction cost directly affects user behavior** — the total mental and physical resources required for an action determines whether users will attempt it; minimize interaction cost for desired actions.
