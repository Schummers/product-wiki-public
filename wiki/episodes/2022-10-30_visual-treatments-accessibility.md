# 5 Visual Treatments that Improve Accessibility

## Summary

With 61 million adults in the United States living with disabilities, accessible design is not optional but essential. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) and platform-specific guidelines (Apple's Human-Interface Guidelines, Google's Material Design) provide frameworks, but designers need practical guidance on applying accessibility principles to visual design. This article presents five key visual-treatment guidelines that improve accessibility and, as a bonus, enhance usability for all users.

The five guidelines address color contrast ratios, visual cues beyond color, keyboard focus states, robust alternative text for images, and testing with real users with disabilities. Effective accessibility requires high contrast between text and backgrounds (at least 4.5:1 for normal text, 3:1 for large text), additional visual signals (icons, patterns, strokes) beyond color alone to communicate important information, clearly visible focus indicators for keyboard navigation, descriptive but succinct alt text for images that doesn't duplicate captions, and user testing with people who have varied accessibility needs. Testing should include using assistive tools directly, trying keyboard navigation, and recruiting participants with diverse accessibility requirements.

## Key Takeaways

- **Ensure text-to-background color contrast of at least 4.5:1** — low-contrast text harms readability for users with low vision and improves overall comfort for all readers; use contrast-checking tools to verify compliance.
- **Use visual cues in addition to color** — color-blind users cannot distinguish elements by color alone; combine color changes with patterns, icons, strokes, or other non-color visual signals.
- **Make keyboard focus states visually obvious** — a clear stroke or border around the focused element helps keyboard users and screen-reader users know where they are; default outline is expected.
- **Write succinct, robust alt text** — describe images clearly without replicating captions; avoid alt text on purely decorative images to reduce cognitive load; reach out to content designers if unsure.
- **Test designs with real users with disabilities** — five users with varied accessibility needs (low vision, motor difficulties) reveal issues that automated tools and expert review cannot catch.

## Concepts

- [[Color Contrast]] — text-to-background contrast of at least 4.5:1 is required by WCAG and improves readability for users with low vision and all users; tools like accessible-colors.com help verify and improve contrast.
- [[Accessibility]] — accessible design benefits all users; the five visual-treatment guidelines ensure information is perceivable and operable for users with vision, motor, or cognitive disabilities, and designers should understand how assistive tools such as screen readers, magnifiers, and speech-input interact with focus states, alt text, and semantic HTML.
- [[Visual Indicators]] — icons, patterns, strokes, and other non-color signals must accompany color changes to convey information to color-blind users; the grayscale test reveals whether color alone communicates.
