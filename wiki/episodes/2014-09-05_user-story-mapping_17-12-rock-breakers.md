# User Story Mapping: 12. Rock Breakers

## Summary

The chapter challenges the Agile assumption that a single product owner writes all stories and leads all conversations. Two core reasons make this impossible: there are too many conversations for one person, and one person cannot hold the diverse expertise—business, user experience, engineering—needed to find truly good solutions. Instead, Patton advocates for small, cross-functional discovery teams (a "triad" of two to four people) led by a product owner who orchestrates but doesn't monopolize the work. During story workshops, the "three amigos" work together to finalize detailed stories: Patton numbers them as a tester, a member of the core discovery team, and a user experience designer or business analyst, alongside a developer from the team that will build the software. He notes the name is shared with a 1986 comedy but says he does not know who first applied it to Agile. He cites the triad he met at Atlassian in Sydney as an example of the discovery-team version. He borrows the valuable-usable-feasible framing from Marty Cagan's *Inspired*. The chapter also warns against the client-vendor anti-pattern, where conversations are replaced by requirements specifications and handoffs, and proposes a doctor-patient or producer-artist relationship instead.

## Key Takeaways

- **One Person Cannot Do It All** — A single product owner cannot write all stories and participate in all conversations; this becomes a bottleneck. One person also cannot possess the business acumen, user experience expertise, and technical knowledge required to find the valuable-usable-feasible sweet spot Marty Cagan describes in *Inspired*.

- **Cross-Functional Discovery Team** — A small team of two to four people (dinner-conversation-sized) led by a product owner, including someone who understands users and can prototype, and a senior engineer, moves much faster and finds more innovative solutions than a lone product owner would.

- **Product Owner as Leader, Not Order-Taker** — The product owner keeps the team focused on business vision and strategy, makes final decisions when tradeoffs are needed, and holds accountability for a cohesive product. This is not design by committee; the product owner decides, but after incorporating diverse expertise. Patton borrows the line from his friend Leisa Reichelt: "Design by community is not design by committee…design is never democratic."

- **The Three Amigos in Story Workshops** — Patton's first amigo is a tester (often the best at playing "What-About"), the second a member of the core discovery team, the third a user experience designer or business analyst who has worked through how the software should look and behave; a developer from the building team is in the conversation too. Together they finalize story details and acceptance criteria during the last best conversation before building, and often split the story into one-to-three-day development stories.

- **Avoid the Client-Vendor Anti-Pattern** — When conversations become specifications and handoffs, with a client role describing requirements and a vendor role estimating delivery, the relationship breaks down. Clients cannot predict solutions; vendors lack the context. Reframe as doctor-patient (expert listening deeply) or producer-artist (guide helping create success).

- **Product Owner as Producer** — Patton attributes the music-producer metaphor to a casual conversation with his friend David Hussman, an Agile practitioner and ex-guitarist: in organizations with business stakeholders separate from development, the business analyst or product owner should act like a producer helping artists succeed, not like an order-taker. This means offering expertise, sometimes saying no, and helping stakeholders reach their goals rather than executing their stated requirements verbatim.

## Quotes

> Requiring a single product owner to write all of the stories and be present for all story conversations doesn't work.

> A small, cross-functional team led by a product owner orchestrates product discovery work.

> Design by community is not design by committee…design is never democratic.

## Concepts

- [[Product Trio]] — Patton describes the ideal small discovery team as a cross-functional triad (product owner, UX/user researcher, engineer) that together identifies solutions that are valuable, usable, and feasible. The term "triad" refers to the three concerns, not necessarily three bodies.

- [[Team Collaboration]] — The chapter emphasizes that effective product development requires collaboration across roles and expertise, not a hierarchical handoff. Collaborative problem-solving in small groups (two to five people) is more productive than large committees or sequential handoffs.

- [[Product Management]] — The product owner role is leadership and orchestration: setting direction, incorporating diverse input, making tradeoffs, and ensuring product coherence. It is not writing all stories or making all decisions unilaterally; it is ensuring the team has what it needs to make good decisions and then making final calls when necessary.

- [[Workshop Facilitation]] — Story workshops—the last best conversation—bring together testers, developers, and product representatives to finalize details. Facilitation skills (asking questions, drawing, listening, building shared pictures) matter as much as domain expertise. "Story workshop" framing emphasizes this is a place to get work done together, not a meeting.
