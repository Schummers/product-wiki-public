# User Story Mapping: 5. You Already Know How

## Summary

Chapter 5 teaches the fundamental mechanics of story mapping through a hands-on exercise: mapping your morning routine using sticky notes. The chapter introduces core vocabulary—user tasks, goal levels, narrative flow, activities, and slices—that Patton argues are already intuitive to anyone. By organizing sticky notes left-to-right (narrative flow), then adding vertical depth (alternatives and variations), then grouping tasks under higher-level activities, the reader discovers that story mapping is simply a structured way of telling a story about what people do. The chapter emphasizes that these concepts work equally well for mapping software usage because human behavior—whether getting ready in the morning or using a product—follows the same underlying patterns of tasks, alternatives, and goals.

The chapter also introduces the idea of "now maps" (mapping how people work today) versus future maps (how they will work with new software), and shows that both use identical mechanics. Patton concludes that effective mapping requires talking directly with people about their work, building shared understanding, and capturing the reality of how they actually do things, not how designers or developers imagine they should. He also boils the preceding four chapters down into a six-step process he presents as the one that works well for him, not the only right way: frame the problem, map the big picture, explore, slice out a release strategy, slice out a learning strategy, slice out a development strategy.

## Key Takeaways

- **User tasks are verb phrases describing actions** — The atomic unit of a map is a task: a short phrase like "Hit snooze" or "Take a shower" that describes something a person does to reach a goal.

- **Goal level organizes tasks by scale** — Alistair Cockburn's concept helps determine whether a task is summary-level (many smaller tasks roll up into it), functional-level or "sea-level" (a natural stopping point), or subtask-level (something done as part of a larger task).

- **Narrative flow runs left-to-right** — Organize tasks in the order they happen; reading across the map from left to right tells the story of the journey; hidden conjunctions like "and then" connect each sticky.

- **Add depth for alternatives and variations** — Once the main flow is laid out, play what Patton's friend David Hussman calls "What-About": what happens when things go wrong, what if the user is in a hurry, what are ideal versions—and write those tasks vertically below the main narrative.

- **Activities are clusters of tasks with a common goal** — Group related tasks under higher-level activities that describe what the person is doing (e.g., "Get ready," "Make breakfast"); activities form the backbone of the map and are read left-to-right like a high-level story.

- **Two contributed sidebars carry the chapter's field reports** — Rick Cusick of Reading Plus (Winooski, Vermont) recounts running the morning-map exercise with nine colleagues and the empathy and simultaneity questions it raised, and recommends an icebreaker asking each person why they took an action. Andrea Schmieden describes scaling user story mapping at SAP, where about 20,000 developers make multi-team products the norm: pilot workshops (the largest with 14 Scrum teams), a one-day facilitated format with an experienced coach, a product-vision exercise, personas, a three-tier breakdown from usage steps to activities to user stories, color-coded stickies, dot or simplified-Kano voting, and more than 200 facilitated workshops run in the end.
- **Slices identify the tasks needed for a specific outcome** — Draw a line across the map horizontally and move all tasks below the line that wouldn't be needed to reach a particular goal; this reveals priorities and shows different paths through the map.

## Quotes

> User tasks are the basic building blocks of a story map.

> Use the goal-level concept to help you aggregate small tasks or decompose large tasks.

> Maps are organized left-to-right using a narrative flow: the order in which you'd tell the story.

## Concepts

- [[User Story]] — Framed through the lens of tasks: each story describes something a user does, using a verb phrase; organizing these tasks reveals the structure of the user's journey and priorities.

- [[Story Mapping]] — The mechanics are introduced through the morning routine exercise: tasks arranged in narrative flow (left-to-right), depth added for alternatives and edge cases, and tasks grouped under activities to form a backbone.

- [[Shared Understanding]] — Built by mapping together with real people; seeing the map laid out reveals disagreements (e.g., whether to brush teeth before or after breakfast) and forces the group to agree on what matters and what's just preference.

- [[Customer Journey]] — The left-to-right narrative flow of a story map documents the customer's or user's journey; alternatives and variations captured in depth show the many ways the journey can play out.

- [[Product Outcome]] — Slicing the map to a specific outcome shows which tasks and details are essential to reach that goal; changing the goal hung on the left of the map changes which tasks stay above the line.

- [[Workshop Facilitation]] — Patton demonstrates running the mapping exercise in a group; the shared drawing and discussion build alignment and reveal what people actually know and don't know about users and their work.
