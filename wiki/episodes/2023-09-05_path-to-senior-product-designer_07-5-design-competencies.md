# The Path to Senior Product Designer: 5. Design Competencies

## Summary

Part II of *The Path to Senior Product Designer* begins by introducing design competencies matrices—documents companies use internally to structure career progression and calibrate designer evaluations. The chapter analyzes competency matrices from about 50 companies including Intercom, Figma, Etsy, Dropbox, and Zendesk to identify what competencies appear most frequently and in what order of importance. Rather than examining a single company's framework, the author distils an industry-average competency list to help designers build a universal growth plan applicable across most organizations.

The chapter argues that competencies matrices are used to grow designers not out of altruism but because promotion and career growth correlate with the value a designer brings to their business. It then breaks down eight competency categories: craft (technical design skills), communication, collaboration, ownership, strategy, mentorship, leadership, and community. Within each category, the chapter provides what companies expect from designers and why they value each skill.

## Key Takeaways

- **Competencies matrices are structured documents** — companies often organize them by title/level, competencies themselves, general level descriptions, and competency descriptions for each level; they are adopted more often in larger, mature design organizations with experienced leadership.
- **Craft is only one of eight competency categories** — the chapter states that hard skills (craft) are just one category out of eight and that the rest are soft skills. It cites, as a separate example, Mike Davidson deliberately making technical skills, empathy and vision worth only 25% of the evaluation formula when he was VP of design at Twitter, alongside three other pillars each worth 25%: getting things done, creating strong relationships, and improving the team.
- **Soft skills matter as much as technical skill** — designers often overtrain in hard skills and undertrain in soft skills, yet soft skills are required equally for career progression; the book examines all eight competency categories equally.
- **Competencies are ordered by importance to employers** — the author says he ordered them by how often employers mention them in the competency documents of about 50 companies he reviewed, starting with craft and communication and ending with mentorship, leadership, and community.
- **Competencies apply beyond product design** — while craft is specific to product designers, the other seven competencies apply to UX researchers, design operations, motion designers, and other design roles.
- **The chapter lists specific sub-skills for each competency** — such as typography and layout under visual design, user research and accessibility under UX design, and stakeholder management and interpersonal skills under collaboration.

## Quotes

> Design craft is the obvious skill to have to succeed as a designer. Although, many designers at the beginning of their career may falsely assume that it's the main and only competency.

> As you can see from the list above, hard skills (craft) are just one category out of eight, while the rest are soft skills. No one gets promoted purely for learning every possible Figma trick.

> The tendency is for craft-led designers to be overtrained in hard skills and undertrained in soft skills.

## Concepts

- [[Design Competencies]] — the chapter defines eight competency categories companies use to evaluate designers, ranked by how frequently they appear in competency matrices; provides a framework for understanding what skills matter across organizations.
- [[Career Ladder]] — competency matrices establish the progression framework for designer roles from junior to principal by specifying what is expected at each level.
- [[Performance Review]] — companies use competency matrices to calibrate internal evaluation and set growth goals for designers; the chapter stresses that this is driven by business value rather than altruism.
