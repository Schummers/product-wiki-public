# UX Research: Chapter 10: Improv in Research

## Summary

Because humans are unpredictable, no amount of planning can mitigate every variable that arises in research. Chapter 10 introduces improvisational techniques as a mindset for approaching the unknown with confidence. Improv is fundamentally about storytelling and adaptability; the chapter draws on established improv troupes (Second City, Saturday Night Live, Monty Python's Flying Circus) and teaching organizations like Toastmasters to outline the ten rules of improv the authors selected for their direct application to design and research, each paired with a game to practice it. These rules—acceptance, stopping trying to be funny, accepting failure, listening to the scene, supporting your players, setting the location, raising the stakes, yielding to the strongest offer, not being the star, and remembering everything—apply to research facilitation, design presentations, ideation sessions, and handling unexpected challenges like technical failures or uncooperative participants.

## Key Takeaways

- **Everything is True: Practice Acceptance** — In improv, all participants' statements are true from their perspective; this aligns with design brainstorming where there is no bad idea. In research, correcting or trying to change a participant's behavior breaks the teacher-student model and puts them on the defensive; instead, note the data point for later validation with stakeholders or other participants.

- **Listen to the Scene, Not Your Script** — Active listening is central to improv; the goal is not to be clever or funny but to focus on what the participant is saying and leaving unsaid. By using the discussion guide flexibly to hit milestones rather than rigidly following it, you can make real-time adjustments and uncover richer feedback while keeping the conversation engaging.

- **Support Your Players** — Improv and research are not solitary tasks. Players support one another in improv to allow everyone stage time and avoid burnout. In research, support may be subtle (balancing moderator and note-taker roles) or direct (rotating responsibilities between sessions to prevent mental fatigue). The focus is on making teammates' skills shine and relieving strain.

- **Failure is OK and Expected** — Not all research initiatives succeed; technical issues, dud participants, or off days happen. Improv teaches that failure is straightforward to identify but how you respond matters. When technology fails, you can shift a validation session to a design session, or reframe a requirements meeting as a discussion of business needs and market segmentation.

- **Apply Improv Across Product Work** — Beyond research, improv applies to sales meetings (where you adjust canned responses to specific client needs), presentations (where stakeholder feedback or pushback requires on-the-fly reframing), idea generation (where supporting teammates is key), and design workshops (both convergent and divergent types benefit from improvisational thinking).

- **Raise the Stakes to Expose Hidden Opportunities** — Just as a skit moves from a boat to the Titanic or a road trip to being stranded in the desert, research should consider how a problem may be larger than it first appears. For a roadside assistance app, design against the harder case (freeway breakdown) rather than the easy one (driveway), as the former involves drama and risk that expose hidden design opportunities.

## Quotes

> Improv gives researchers the ability to think on our feet and pivot at a moment's notice based on the reactions and feedback we receive.

> In research, it is important to remember that participants are human and that rich data points or sound bytes can't be forced. Improv skills help you facilitate an engaging conversation with participants, allowing interesting data points to naturally develop.

> The focus should be on making your teammates' skills shine, and relieving strain if a session or activity becomes stressful for them.

## Concepts

- [[Improvisation]] — The chapter frames improv as a set of learnable techniques—acceptance, active listening, support, comfort with failure—that help researchers and designers think on their feet, adapt in real time to unexpected situations, and maintain engagement even when plans fall apart.

- [[Active Listening]] — Active listening is a core improv principle: focus on what is being said rather than on what you plan to say next. In research, this means hearing the participant, noticing what they leave unsaid, and using the discussion guide flexibly to hit milestones rather than treating it as a script.

- [[Team Collaboration]] — Improv and research alike depend on team support: moderators and note-takers balance each other, responsibilities rotate to prevent burnout, and the goal is to make teammates' contributions shine rather than to dominate the session or take stage time.

- [[Workshop Facilitation]] — The chapter describes both convergent workshops (seeking consensus on a single design direction) and divergent workshops (exploring many avenues through participatory design, paper prototyping, card sorting) as improvisational activities where facilitation skills and improv techniques directly apply.

- [[Stakeholder Engagement]] — In sales meetings, presentations, and workshops with stakeholders outside the core product team, improv techniques help adjust messaging on the fly and reframe feedback positively. UX strategist Adam Polansky, in the chapter's practitioner interview, describes getting everyone to pick an adjective to define project success, so clients think about the essence of the goal rather than the carefully crafted objective statement they prepared; separately, he asks clients to imagine the best possible outcome for the user as if the laws of physics did not count, which he says shifts the urgency of the conversation from "what" to "why." He adds that it does not always work.
