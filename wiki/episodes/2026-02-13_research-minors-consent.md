# UX Research with Minors: Consent vs. Assent

## Summary

Minors cannot legally provide informed consent; researchers must secure both written parental/guardian consent and verbal assent from the child. Consent is legally binding permission from an informed, competent adult; assent is affirmative agreement from someone who cannot legally consent but can indicate willingness to participate. Including minors in research surfaces real usage patterns, helps teams design with empathy, ensures products are safe and appropriate, and boosts inclusivity. Successful studies require developmentally appropriate assent conversations, clear consent forms covering purpose, data use, and compensation, and decisions about whether parents should be present (ages 3–6 may benefit from parental proximity; teens typically participate alone). Compensation should be fair and age-appropriate.

## Key Takeaways

- **Consent vs. Assent Are Both Required** — Parents/guardians provide written consent; minors give verbal assent; both forms of agreement are necessary to move forward.
- **Assent Must Be Developmentally Appropriate** — Children must understand what's asked and feel free to decline or stop; use plain language matching their age.
- **Parental Presence Varies by Age** — Ages 3–6 may stay near parents (positioned behind, silent); ages 7–12 are comfortable with parents nearby or outside the room; teens typically fine alone.
- **Consent Forms Must Cover Essentials** — Purpose, what the child will do, data storage and protection, compensation, voluntary participation, and researcher contact.
- **Verbal Assent Script Approach Works** — NN/g example: explain what you're doing, what they'll do, that they can stop anytime; look for clear verbal agreement, not just nodding.
- **Compensation Must Be Fair and Age-Appropriate** — Parents receive monetary compensation (time and effort); older children (9+) receive small gift cards; younger children (3–8) choose from pre-approved toys.

## Quotes

> Minors — typically defined as individuals under age 18 — cannot legally provide informed consent on their own. If you're planning on conducting ethical research with children or teens, you'll need to secure both written parental or guardian consent and verbal assent from the child or teen.

> You should never expect a child to read or comprehend an adult-style consent form. Once you have guardian consent, talk to the child directly and respectfully.

> Respect for both legal and emotional boundaries leads to better research and better experiences for everyone involved.

- [[Research Ethics]] — Research with minors requires extra ethical care, legal compliance, and respect for both child autonomy and parental authority.
- [[Participant Recruitment]] — Recruiting minors involves navigating legal, developmental, and emotional considerations; transparency and fairness are essential.
- [[Young Users]] — Including children and teens in research ensures products reflect real needs; developmental understanding informs both study design and interpretation.
- [[Legal Compliance]] — Consent and assent requirements vary by jurisdiction but generally require written parental consent and child assent; documentation protects researchers and participants.
- [[Inclusive Research]] — Including young users in research ensures inclusivity; it surfaces usability issues for nonreaders and ensures age-appropriate, safe design.
