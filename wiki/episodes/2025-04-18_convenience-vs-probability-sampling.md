# Convenience vs. Probability Sampling in UX Research

## Summary

This article compares two participant-recruitment approaches in UX research: convenience sampling and probability sampling. Convenience sampling (nonrandom, based on ease of access) is the UX research default because it is fast, cost-effective, and sufficient for identifying usability issues. Probability sampling (random selection ensuring every population member has a known chance of selection) is slower, costlier, and more complex but provides higher confidence that findings accurately represent a broader population. The article guides teams in choosing the right approach based on research goals and stakes.

## Key Takeaways

- **Convenience sampling is the UX research default for good reason** — recruiting readily available participants is fast and cost-effective; sufficient for qualitative usability testing, exploratory research, and iterative feedback cycles; most studies aim to find usability problems, not to make statistically rigorous generalizations.
- **Probability sampling requires a complete population list and random selection** — more complex, time-consuming, and expensive than convenience sampling; requires well-defined target population, complete contact list, random selection method, and larger sample sizes (40+ for quantitative usability testing, 100+ for surveys).
- **Quotas do not equal randomness in convenience sampling** — even with quotas restricting a convenience sample (e.g., 5 users per age group), hidden biases (emotional state, cultural influences, interest in participation) can still skew results; nonrandom selection means not everyone has equal selection chances.
- **Use probability sampling for high-stakes, population-level decisions** — appropriate when inferring behavior prevalence in broader populations, user bases are diverse and recruitment bias could skew results, decisions carry high stakes (healthcare, finance, automotive), or research involves statistical comparisons between groups.
- **Both sampling methods have trade-offs** — convenience sampling introduces selection bias and limits generalizability; probability sampling reduces bias and increases external validity but is impractical for most usability studies due to logistical complexity and inability to obtain complete population lists.

## Quotes

> **Convenience sampling** refers to the nonrandom selection of study participants who are readily available and convenient to access.

> Probability sampling is based on **random selection**, which ensures that everyone in the population has a known probability of being included in the study.

> Most UX research studies involve convenience sampling: recruiting users that are easy to access.
