# Dangerous UX: Consequential Options Close to Benign Options

## Summary

Placing high-consequence actions (delete, block, mute) next to benign options is a persistent UX mistake found in contextual menus, dropdown menus, dialogs, error messages, and wizards. Users rely on System 1 thinking during repetitive work and fall into automaticity, making them vulnerable to accidental clicks when similar-looking options are adjacent. Industrial design pioneer Alphonse Chapanis solved pilot error during WWII by shape-coding controls to feel different without requiring conscious attention. Digital designers can apply similar principles through visual differentiation (color, icons, size, alignment) and spatial separation (leveraging Fitts' Law to make destructive actions slightly harder to access).

## Key Takeaways

- **Proximity and automaticity create accident risk** — users performing repetitive work rely on automatic System 1 thinking and habitual patterns; consequential options placed next to benign ones invite accidental clicks.
- **Shape coding prevents errors through physical differentiation** — during WWII, B-17 bomber pilots confused landing-gear and wing-flap levers; changing lever shapes reduced pilot error by signaling intent through tactile properties.
- **Visual redundancy differentiates benign from consequential** — color, icons, text size, and alignment can separately signal which option is destructive (e.g., red delete vs. gray option).
- **Spatial separation leverages Fitts' Law** — placing consequential actions farther from related benign actions increases motor movement time, providing a safety buffer against accidental activation.
- **System 1 thinking requires preattentive cues** — when users are on autopilot, they respond to visual and spatial cues (color, proximity) before engaging conscious System 2 thinking.

## Quotes

> Right next to the option to use one of the suggested spellings, there is Add to Dictionary. Literally, next to the option to fix a mistake is the option to make sure that I will forever make that same mistake!

> Designers often overlook the fact that the user may not be paying full attention to their design.

> Chapanis helped redesign the controls on the B-17 bomber to avoid pilot error by changing the shape of one of the two levers, so pilots could quickly tell which lever they had in hand.
