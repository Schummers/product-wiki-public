# Are Chinese Websites Too Complex?

## Summary

Despite common assumptions from foreign observers that Chinese websites are overly complex and busy, empirical usability testing with native Chinese users and expatriates reveals a more nuanced picture. Chinese users are accustomed to and tolerant of high information density, viewing complexity as normal and traditional, but they still encounter significant usability problems on complex sites and spend more time finding information. Well-documented usability problems like poor search, carousels, inconsistent navigation, and insufficiently user-centric content plague Chinese websites just as they plague Western sites.

Chinese users' acceptance of complexity stems from design tradition and cultural factors, yet this tolerance doesn't mean complexity is ideal. Simple, responsive designs may face resistance because users expect information density, but oversimplified designs also fail. The optimal approach for Chinese audiences is simpler design than the current high-complexity tradition, but not oversimplified or minimalist.

## Key Takeaways

- **Complexity is prevalent and accepted** — Chinese study participants viewed complex design as common, normal, and traditional; they appreciated information density more than foreign participants did.
- **Chinese users are not superhumans** — Chinese people exhibit the same usability struggles as Western users with poorly designed interfaces; complexity tolerance doesn't eliminate difficulty.
- **Same mistakes, different tolerance** — Problems like carousel confusion, disappearing navigation, non-standard UI elements, and lack of related-article links appear on both Chinese and Western sites; Chinese users complain less but still struggle.
- **Ads degrade the experience** — Advertising interspersed with editorial content makes sites harder to use and frustrates both Chinese and foreign users; this problem is widespread on Chinese news and portal sites.
- **Information density expectations matter** — Chinese users reject overly sparse designs as lacking useful content; simple design must still include meaningful information, not just pretty empty space.
- **Standard usability guidelines apply globally** — Clarity, visual hierarchy, legible text, standard interface elements, and user-centric content improve usability regardless of cultural context or regional design traditions.

## Quotes

> Foreigners often say that Chinese websites are overly complex and busy in their design. However, since they usually can't use the sites — not being able to read Chinese — such impressions, formed purely by looking instead of using, are not a valid user experience assessment.

> Chinese people are people and not some kind of superhumans who violate the laws of user interface psychology and easily master designs that stump users in the rest of the world.

> Simpler design, but not oversimplified or minimalist, will benefit Chinese users and likely increase revenue for businesses.

## Concepts

- [[Localization]] — Design preferences and usability issues differ across cultures; assumptions based on visual inspection without user testing are invalid, and excessive complexity causes usability problems and user frustration while users accustomed to higher information density may reject overly simple designs.
- [[Usability Testing]] — Empirical testing with native speakers and target users reveals design problems that casual observation misses; the same methods work across cultures.
- [[Information Density]] — Users' tolerance for information density varies by cultural design tradition; optimal design balances information richness with clarity, providing meaningful content without overwhelming users.
