# UX Research: Chapter 2: Good Research Starts with Good Questions

## Summary

This chapter establishes that good research begins with carefully crafted questions, and outlines the common pitfalls that derail research sessions. It explains why researchers struggle to write effective questions and introduces the anatomy of a good question: setup (what, why, how, when, where), area of inquiry, laddering (follow-up probing), and segue to the next question. The chapter categorizes question types—process, explanation, description, time-based, frustration, and ideals—and provides guidance on tying each question back to research goals while remaining open to unexpected rabbit holes. It emphasizes that mastery comes through practice, dry runs with coworkers, and iterative refinement of the research guide.

## Key Takeaways

- **Four pitfalls to avoid** — Leading questions inadvertently suggest answers to participants who want to be helpful; shallow yes/no questions give participants an easy out; personal bias allows researchers' beliefs to sneak into questions; unconscious bias reflects social norms and expectations the researcher is not aware of.
- **Question structure anatomy** — A good question has a setup that frames what, why, how, when, or where; an area of inquiry about what you want to learn; laddering that probes for deeper understanding; and a segue to the next question to maintain conversational flow.
- **Strategic rule-breaking** — Once practiced, researchers can strategically use leading questions to build trust or validate comments, shallow questions to ease participants into comfort, and personal bias to spark deeper discussion through devil's advocacy.
- **Relate questions to research goals** — Every question should tie back to the original research objectives; if a question does not provide information that helps understand these goals, it should be removed from the interview guide.
- **Practice through dry runs** — Researchers should validate interview guides with coworkers and conduct full dry runs before sessions with actual participants; dry runs provide comfort and feedback without negatively impacting the data collected.

## Quotes

> Asking for a participant to go into more detail or to explain the rationale behind their response is known as laddering, and it's an aspect of a question that helps you get to deeper information and potentially impactful stories.

> The motto of a good researcher is "there is no such thing as user error."

> Every researcher from any field has been through the same struggles. In terms of design, what really matters is that you care enough about your product that you get out of your own way and reach out to people for help.

## Concepts

- [[User Interviews]] — The chapter provides detailed guidance on structuring interview questions, avoiding common pitfalls like leading and shallow questions, and using techniques like laddering to uncover deeper insights from participants.
- [[Research Planning]] — Research begins with planning questions around your goals; every question should tie back to research objectives, and questions should progress naturally through a conversation rather than read like a verbal questionnaire.
- [[Bias in Research]] — The chapter lists four factors that can lead to misinformed or poor research results, two of them biases: leading questions that point participants toward predetermined answers, shallow yes/no questions that avoid depth, personal bias that introduces the researcher's own beliefs, and unconscious bias from social norms, personal history, past experiences or expectations, which the chapter calls the hardest to catch.
- [[Study Design]] — The structure of a research session—the flow of questions, the balance between openness and guidance, the management of rabbit holes versus staying on topic, and the progression of a conversation from comfort to depth—are deliberate design choices.
