# Laws of UX: 10. Doherty Threshold

## Summary

Chapter 10 of Laws of UX explains the Doherty Threshold, a fundamental principle that establishes 400 ms as the critical response time threshold. When a computer responds to users within this timeframe, productivity soars because neither the user nor the system must wait on the other. The chapter connects this technical observation to flow state psychology and explores practical techniques for meeting this threshold through perceived performance optimization, including skeleton screens, blur-up image loading, progress bars, and optimistic UI patterns.

## Key Takeaways

- **Doherty Threshold at 400 ms** — system response times under 400 ms are critical to maintaining user productivity and preventing frustration; delays beyond 1 second cause users to think about other things and lose focus on their task.

- **Flow state** — flow is the state of complete immersion that occurs when the difficulty of a task is balanced against the user's skill; the chapter's levers for designing for it are giving the feedback that tells users what has been done, optimizing efficiency and system responsiveness by removing unnecessary friction, and keeping content and features discoverable.

- **Perceived performance over actual speed** — when processing genuinely takes longer than 400 ms, techniques like skeleton screens, progress bars, and blur-up image loading make the system appear faster, reducing the perception of waiting.

- **Animation as engagement** — animations and visual feedback during loading keep users focused and reassured, with research showing that progress bars make wait times feel more tolerable regardless of accuracy.

- **Strategic friction for trust** — intentionally adding delay to processes can increase perceived value and build trust, particularly in sensitive operations like privacy scans where users need confidence the action is thorough.

- **Page weight escalation** — the average desktop page weight in 2023 was 2,286 KB compared to 634 KB in 2010–2011, making load-time performance increasingly critical.

## Quotes

> Productivity soars when a computer and its users interact at a pace (<400 ms) that ensures that neither has to wait on the other.

> When users find themselves in a flow state, they are completely absorbed by their tasks, free from inner criticism, and up to five times more productive.

> Purposefully adding a delay to a process can actually increase its perceived value and instill a sense of trust, even when the process itself actually takes much less time.
