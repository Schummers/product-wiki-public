# User Story Mapping: 7. Telling Better Stories

## Summary

Chapter 7 addresses the practical challenge of running good story conversations. Patton traces the "as a... I want... so that..." template to Connextra, an early Extreme Programming adopter in the late 1990s, telling the story through his friend Rachel Davies, who worked there and still holds one of the example cards printed for XPDay 2001; he is careful to say the template evolved across the organization rather than being any one person's invention. He then warns against "template zombies"—a term he takes from Tom DeMarco et al., *Adrenaline Junkies and Template Zombies*—teams that become slavishly attached to the template and try to force ideas into it, mistakenly believing stories aren't real unless written that way. He compares the template to a snowplow: useful for learning to ski, but not best practice for all terrain. The chapter provides a detailed checklist of what to actually talk about in story conversations: who (be specific about user types, customers, stakeholders), what (user tasks, but also services and edge cases), why (dig deeply with the "why stick"), context (where, when, who else is present), what goes wrong (failure modes), assumptions and questions, better solutions, how (solution approach), and how long (estimates). Patton emphasizes that teams should record these discussions on whiteboards or flipcharts as they talk—"vacation photos"—so that the visual record becomes a shared memory of decisions.

The overarching message is that the real value of stories lies not in the writing but in what teams learn through conversation; excessive focus on formatting or precise wording misses the point entirely.

## Key Takeaways

- **The template is a learning tool, not a requirement** — The "as a... I want... so that..." format, which came out of Connextra, is useful for starting story conversations and remembering to consider who and why, but it's not mandatory; teams should be willing to adapt or discard it when it doesn't fit.

- **Avoid template zombies** — The term is Tom DeMarco et al.'s, defined as a team letting its work be driven by templates instead of by the thinking needed to deliver products. Don't force stories into the template or refuse to acknowledge a story unless it's written that way; this reverts to document-driven thinking and kills the conversation.

- **Really talk about who** — Be specific about user types, talk about different kinds of users doing the same thing, include customers (not just users) and other stakeholders; there's rarely just one person who matters.

- **Really talk about what** — Focus on user tasks, but don't shy away from backend services, technical constraints, and UI specifics when relevant; don't lose sight of who cares and why.

- **Really talk about why** — Dig deeply into reasons with the "why stick": why does this user care, why do other users care, why does the organization care; these layered whys hide important insights.

- **Really talk about context, failure modes, assumptions, and better solutions** — Include where and when users work, who else is present, what happens when things go wrong, what you don't know, and whether there's a more effective solution than the one originally proposed.

- **Rediscovering the small conversation, per Mat Cropper** — In a contributed sidebar, Mat Cropper of ThoughtWorks describes a 25-person UK government team whose whole-team iteration planning was "a car crash"; splitting the work into tighter, smaller conversations (a four-person grooming group, then ~20-minute story write-ups with a developer pair) made the large sessions a breeze and story sizes more uniform.
- **Record decisions as vacation photos** — Write and sketch on whiteboards or flipcharts as conversations happen, take photos, and share them; the visual record helps teams remember details and makes the conversation's outcome tangible.

## Quotes

> It doesn't need to be written in a template to be considered a story.

> The real value of stories isn't what's written down on the card. It comes from what we learn when we tell the story.

> Be respectful of the expertise of others in the conversation. Don't tell a highly trained technical person how to do her work. Don't tell someone intimately familiar with users and their work that he doesn't understand. Ask questions, and genuinely try to learn from each other.

## Concepts

- [[Storytelling]] — The real work is telling stories conversationally; the template is just a conversation starter; what matters is what the team learns together, not what gets written on a card.

- [[Team Collaboration]] — Story conversations require respectful exchange between people with different expertise: those who understand users and problems, and those who can build solutions; the conversation structure ensures both perspectives shape the outcome.

- [[Shared Understanding]] — Built by talking through a checklist of considerations (who, what, why, context, failure modes, assumptions, solutions); teams that record these discussions on whiteboards and photograph them create shared memory.

- [[Documentation]] — Vacation photos (photographs of whiteboards and sketches made during conversations) capture decisions better than formal story cards; the visual, contextual record helps teams recall what was decided and why.

- [[Product Discovery]] — Story conversations are part of the discovery process; the checklist items (questioning assumptions, discussing user context, exploring better solutions) are how teams learn what's really needed.

- [[Workshop Facilitation]] — Running good story conversations means working through the checklist rather than a template, recording thinking visually as you go, and being respectful of the expertise each person brings; Mat Cropper's sidebar shows the same effect obtained by shrinking the group rather than by facilitating a large one better.
