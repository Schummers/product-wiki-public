# User Story Mapping: 10. Bake Stories Like Cake

## Summary

Using his family's baker, Sydnie, and her approach to a custom birthday cake, Patton illustrates how stories work in practice. Just as the baker asks clarifying questions (who, what, why, context, timing) before deciding on a plan, a development team gathers details during story conversations. However, if the story describes work that is too large or expensive, the team must consider alternative solutions that still meet the underlying goal. When a story is large but affordable, the key practice is breaking it down not by technical layer (frontend, backend, database) but into small, complete deliverables—like cupcakes—that allow the team to see and evaluate progress sooner, learn from users faster, and manage risk by tasting the cake before the wedding.

## Key Takeaways

- **Clarify Before Planning** — Like a baker gathering context (who it's for, preferences, occasion, timing, technical constraints), development teams need conversations that establish who the user is, what problem they face, and why before rushing into technical planning.

- **Small Deliverables, Not Big Plans** — When a story describes large work, resist the traditional instinct to break it into layers (weeks of frontend, weeks of backend). Instead, break it into small, end-to-end slices (cupcakes) that users can evaluate, so the team learns sooner and can adjust course.

- **Taste the Cake Before Serving** — Building and delivering software in small increments allows the team to validate early that they are on the right track. Waiting too late to see a complete feature wastes time and hides mistakes. Smaller increments reduce risk and uncertainty.

- **If the Cake Is Too Expensive, Bake a Pie Instead** — When a solution is unaffordable, don't just attempt it anyway. Step back and ask whether there is a different, smaller solution that still addresses the core need and outcome the user is trying to achieve.

- **Half a Baked Cake, Not a Half-Baked Cake** — The mantra is one Patton credits to his friend Luke Hohman. A complete (though partial) deliverable that users can use and evaluate is far better than a partial version of everything. Small working slices combine into a whole product while keeping feedback loops tight.

## Quotes

> If the story describes a solution that's too expensive, consider a different solution that helps you reach the goal.

> If the story describes a solution that's affordable but big, break it into smaller parts that allow you to evaluate and see progress sooner.

> Half a baked cake may not be enough to feed a wedding party, but it's enough to taste and leave everyone looking forward to the rest of the cake.

## Concepts

- [[Story Slicing]] — The chapter defines the practice of breaking large stories into small, complete, end-to-end slices (cupcakes) that deliver value and allow early evaluation, rather than breaking by layer or subsystem.

- [[User Story]] — Patton reinforces that a user story is a conversation prompt anchored in who, what, and why—not just a technical requirement. The conversation explores not just the solution but the goal and outcome the user seeks.

- [[Product Discovery]] — Discovery is where the team learns what problem to solve and what solution is small enough and valuable enough to build. Conversations during discovery constrain the solution and eliminate waste.

- [[Iterative Design]] — By delivering small, evaluable pieces and gathering feedback from use, the team designs not in isolation but through repeated cycles of building, learning, and refining, reducing the risk of building something no one wants.
