# Focus Groups 101

## Summary

Focus groups are a qualitative, attitudinal research method where a facilitator conducts a 1-2 hour discussion with 6-9 people about their experiences with a product or service. While often misused for evaluating usability or predicting behavior, focus groups excel at exploring emotional experiences, values, and mental models during early-stage discovery. The article clarifies when focus groups are appropriate and when alternative methods are necessary, emphasizing that success depends on proper facilitation, clear planning, and combination with other research methods to mitigate their inherent limitations around bias and self-reported behavior.

## Key Takeaways

- **Definition and scope** — Focus groups are attitudinal discussions lasting 1-2 hours with 6-9 participants designed to understand opinions and impressions, not detailed usability insights or reliable predictions of future behavior.
- **Poor fit for usability testing** — Focus groups cannot provide behavioral data or usability insights; observational methods like usability tests or field studies are needed for those purposes, since people do not always do what they say they will do.
- **Cognitive biases limit reliability** — Memory is fallible, negativity bias skews discussions toward negative experiences, the peak-end rule overemphasizes memorable moments, and priming causes participants to overemphasize aspects others mention.
- **Group dynamics shape results** — Dominant personalities influence participation, groupthink can suppress disagreement, and under-facilitation risks representing the opinions of only the most vocal participants rather than the group as a whole.
- **Best suited for discovery** — Focus groups work well for gauging familiarity or interest in concepts, understanding users' mental models and vocabulary before design, and cocreation workshops with sponsored customers.
- **Requires strong facilitation** — Success depends on recruiting representative participants, including diverse seniority levels, structured warmup activities, both written and verbal participation options, and careful use of open-ended questions with the funnel technique.

## Quotes

> A poorly run focus group can be a great way to pay 9 people for the opinions of three.

> Focus groups don't accurately predict future behavior. However, they can help gauge attitudes and guide future exploration, thus avoiding wasted research time. Still, they should be considered a starting point to further research, rather than a validation step.

> When run properly, they can yield rich qualitative insights due to a format similar to semistructured interviews. Unlike questionnaires — which can sometimes limit the level of detail covered — focus groups give facilitators the flexibility to explore topics in which the participants are interested.

## Concepts

- [[Focus Groups]] — A qualitative, attitudinal method involving 6-9 participants in 1-2 hour discussions, best for early discovery and mental models, not usability or behavior prediction.
- [[Attitudinal Research]] — Focus groups measure what people say they think and believe, not what they do; must be combined with behavioral methods for complete understanding.
- [[Discovery Research]] — Focus groups excel during discovery phases to gauge customer familiarity, understand mental models, and define problem spaces before deeper research.
- [[Bias in Research]] — Focus groups are vulnerable to negativity bias, peak-end rule, priming, and group dynamics; participants are also prone to memory fallibility and social desirability bias.
- [[Workshop Facilitation]] — Dominant personalities can disproportionately influence results, groupthink can suppress diverse opinions, and poor facilitation risks capturing only the loudest voices.
