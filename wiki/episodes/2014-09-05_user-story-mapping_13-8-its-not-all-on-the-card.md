# User Story Mapping: 8. It's Not All on the Card

## Summary

In the product development journey of a story, the physical card itself is just a token—a conversation trigger and organizational tool, not a repository for all knowledge. Patton explains that different team members (product managers, business analysts, testers, UX designers, project managers) each approach the same story with different concerns and questions. To avoid misunderstandings and poor outcomes, teams must intentionally design multiple conversation channels: some physical and face-to-face for building shared understanding, others digital for tracking progress and preserving decisions once they are made. Patton's running example is Atlassian, where his friend Sherif, a product manager, showed him walls of stickies and wireframes cross-referenced with ticket numbers, and Confluence pages combining photographs, short videos and discussion.

## Key Takeaways

- **Multiple Parallel Conversations** — Each role on the team (product manager, business analyst, tester, UX designer, project manager) has legitimate but different conversations about the same story because they each care about different aspects: market hypotheses, business rules, failure modes, usability, dependencies, and schedule.

- **Story Card as Token, Not Container** — The card functions like a library catalog card: it identifies what you're talking about and helps organize it, but all the real information exists elsewhere—in conversation notes, on walls, in wireframes, and in tools. The card points to the growing body of knowledge; it doesn't contain it.

- **Information Radiators vs. Information Ice Boxes** — Patton credits Alistair Cockburn, in *Agile Software Development: The Cooperative Game*, with coining *information radiator* for big visible information on the wall that radiates into the room; information that lives only in tools tends to accumulate passively, becoming increasingly stale and inaccessible, like frost in the back of a freezer.

- **Different Tools for Different Jobs** — Physical tools (whiteboards, sticky notes, flipcharts, camera) are ideal for real-time, collaborative sense-making and shared understanding. Digital tools excel at tracking progress, generating insights from aggregate data, and storing information durably when conversations are done. Patton cites Cardboard, co-created by David Hussman, as an example of a tool letting remote participants build the same map in real time as someone maps on a wall.

- **Keep Conversations Documented** — Photograph or capture the outputs of conversations (wall drawings, whiteboard sketches) and store them where the team can refer back to them. This preserves the details the team decided on without requiring every future conversation to rehash them.

## Quotes

> There are many different kinds of conversations with different people for every story.

> The metaphor that works for me is a card in a library card catalog, for people who are old enough to remember when libraries actually had card catalogs.

> When collaborating remotely, use tools that allow everyone to see, add to, and organize the model concurrently.

## Concepts

- [[Team Collaboration]] — Patton emphasizes that different team members need different conversations about the same story, requiring intentional collaboration design so each role can contribute its expertise.

- [[Shared Understanding]] — Building shared understanding is the critical first goal when presenting a story; it requires conversations and externalizing ideas on walls or in shared spaces, and Patton warns against trying to do it with a tracking tool, which is the wrong tool for that job.

- [[Product Trio]] — Although not called a triad in this chapter, the diversity of roles (product manager, designer, tester, developer, analyst, writer) and their distinct concerns illustrates why product decisions require cross-functional input and conversation.

- [[Workshop Facilitation]] — Patton describes practices for facilitating conversations—face-to-face work at whiteboards, video conferencing with camera-to-wall focus, concurrent editing in shared digital spaces, and photography of outputs—as the machinery that turns individual notes into team understanding.
