# Two Tips for Better UX Storytelling

## Summary

This article presents two key strategies for improving user stories in UX work. The story triangle framework emphasizes that effective storytelling is a dynamic exchange between the story, storyteller, and audience. The story-mountain template, adapted from Freytag's pyramid, provides a five-part structure: exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, and resolution. Together, these tools help UX professionals craft memorable stories that build empathy, establish shared vocabulary, and secure organizational buy-in for UX endeavors.

## Key Takeaways

- **Story triangle dynamics** — Storytelling is a conversation, not a broadcast; audiences make different inferences based on their own experiences, so storytellers must invite feedback and account for misinterpretation.
- **Detail balance is crucial** — Too few details leave room for audience imagination and potential distraction; too many details can feel rigid and limit emotional involvement. The right amount depends on your storytelling goal.
- **Five-part story structure** — Exposition establishes the user and setting, rising action presents the problem, climax shows the turning point, falling action shows resolution steps, and resolution demonstrates the outcome.
- **Active audience role** — The audience fills in missing information and connects stories to their own experiences, which is both an opportunity to engage emotions and a risk of misunderstanding.
- **Structure aids retention** — The story-mountain template helps stories have main characters with clear problems and closure, resulting in actionable recommendations that audiences remember.

## Quotes

> Effective storytelling is a dynamic exchange between the story, the storyteller, and the audience; these three elements form the story triangle.

> An engaging story is a conversation rather than a one-way broadcast. The same story can be understood differently by different audiences because each will make slightly different inferences and fill in missing information based on highly specific prior experiences.

> The story mountain is often associated with Freytag's pyramid. Published in the middle of the 19th century by Gustav Freytag, a German novelist and playwright, this structure originally provided a five-part map for a tragedy: introduction, rise, climax, return or fall, and catastrophe.
