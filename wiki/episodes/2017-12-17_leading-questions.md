# Avoid Leading Questions to Get Better Insights from Participants

## Summary

This article addresses a critical pitfall in user research: the use of leading questions that bias participant responses. A leading question includes or implies the desired answer in its phrasing, making it difficult or socially awkward for participants to disagree with the interviewer. The article demonstrates how three versions of a follow-up question produce different reliability: "I saw you were having difficulty" assumes what was observed and is not neutral, "Why did you have difficulty" implies difficulty occurred, while "What was easy or difficult" allows participants to define their own experience without suggestion or assumption. Leading questions produce biased or false answers because participants tend to mimic interviewer language and assumptions. The article outlines common traps to avoid: rephrasing in interviewer's own words, suggesting answers, naming interface elements, and assuming user emotions. Effective research requires open-ended questions that allow participants to define their own experience.

## Key Takeaways

- **Leading Questions Bias Responses** — Questions that include or imply the desired answer make it difficult for participants to disagree; participants are prone to mimic interviewer language and confirm the interviewer's assumptions rather than express their own experience.
- **Participant Deference to Authority** — In usability testing, the interviewer is perceived as an authority; many participants are reluctant to disagree with someone in a position of power, making leading questions especially problematic as they set up social pressure to confirm.
- **Question Phrasing Matters Enormously** — The exact wording used to describe observations significantly affects participant responses; identical situations framed negatively or positively can produce different answers due to participants accepting the interviewer's framing.
- **Open-Ended Questions Essential** — Questions designed to elicit explanations rather than yes/no answers are necessary to gather unbiased user insights; they allow participants to define their own experience without suggested answers.
- **Subtle Traps to Avoid** — Common mistakes include rephrasing observations (which can misrepresent the user's experience), suggesting answers through wording, naming specific UI elements (which primes terminology), and assuming emotions the user felt.
- **Unexpected Insights Lost** — Leading questions rob researchers of opportunities to hear surprising, creative insights that might reveal important design problems or solutions the researcher did not anticipate.

## Quotes

> "Honest, unbiased participant feedback is critical for user research. When we ask questions, we want to learn more about the user's actions."

> "Leading questions result in biased or false answers, as respondents are prone to simply mimic the words of the interviewer."

> "Leading questions ultimately rob us of the opportunity to hear an insight we weren't expecting from the user."

## Concepts

- [[Bias in Research]] — Systematic errors in data collection or interpretation that skew results; leading questions are a major source of bias, causing participants to confirm interviewer assumptions rather than express genuine experience.
- [[Survey Design]] — The craft of creating questions that elicit genuine participant responses; requires understanding how wording, framing, and phrasing influence how people answer.
- [[Usability Testing]] — Research method where users attempt tasks on an interface; relies on follow-up questions to understand what users experienced; leading questions compromise the validity of findings.
- [[Participant Response]] — What participants say and do during research; influenced not only by the interface but by how questions are asked, interviewer demeanor, and the social dynamics of the research situation.
- [[Open-Ended Question]] — A question designed to elicit an explanation or description rather than a yes/no answer; essential for unbiased research because it allows participants to define their own experience.
