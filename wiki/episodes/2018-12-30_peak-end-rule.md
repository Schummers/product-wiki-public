# The Peak–End Rule: How Impressions Become Memories

## Summary

This article explores the peak-end rule, a cognitive bias documented by Daniel Kahneman and Barbara Frederickson that demonstrates how people's memories of experiences are not accurate records but rather weighted by emotionally intense moments (peaks) and final moments (ends). The research shows that a small improvement at the end of an experience can radically shift how people recall the entire event. The article demonstrates how understanding this principle enables UX designers to create more memorable products and improve users' subjective opinions through strategic attention to peaks and endings.

## Key Takeaways

- **Memory is weighted by peaks and endings** — People remember experiences as snapshots dominated by emotionally intense moments and the final moments, not as accurate averages. In Kahneman and Frederickson's cold-water study, 80% of participants chose to repeat the longer, more painful trial because the final moments were slightly warmer, altering their memory of the entire experience.

- **Negative peaks are memorable** — Moments of confusion and frustration create emotionally charged "peaks" that disproportionately affect how users later recall their experience. A difficult navigation menu experienced early in a website visit influenced a user's overall assessment and willingness to recommend the company.

- **Design for positive peaks** — Elements that highlight convenience, comfort, and delight transform pleasant experiences into memorable ones. Apps like Duolingo reinforce positive moments through playful feedback, enhancing intrinsic satisfaction.

- **Last impressions are lasting impressions** — Users judge experiences heavily based on how they conclude. Progress indicators that speed toward completion feel faster; celebratory endpoints (like TurboTax's tax-filing completion screen) leave positive lasting impressions.

- **Handle errors gracefully at endpoints** — Technical difficulties, errors, and service disruptions create negative peaks, especially when unexpected. Unclear error messages that blame users or offer no resolution path worsen these moments and leave negative lasting impressions.

## Quotes

> "Our minds quickly average the moments that most stand out in our memories to form our opinion of the past. The most emotionally intense points of an experience and the end of that experience are heavily weighted in how we remember an event."

> "A small improvement near the end of an experience radically shifted people's perception of that event."

> "Designing with attention to detail around the important moments of the customer journey, with particular emphasis on the last step, allows you to build digital products that are worth remembering."

## Concepts

- [[Peak-End Rule]] — A cognitive bias where emotionally intense moments and final moments disproportionately weight how people remember experiences, affecting their subjective satisfaction and likelihood of recommendation.

- [[Memory and Experience]] — Human memory does not accurately record experiences but rather creates impressions influenced by emotional peaks and endings, making design for these moments crucial to perceived quality.

- [[Emotional Design]] — Strategic use of visual feedback, celebratory elements, and graceful error handling to create positive emotional peaks and endings that improve users' overall memory of experiences.
