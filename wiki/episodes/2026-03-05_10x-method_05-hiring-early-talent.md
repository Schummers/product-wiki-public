# The 10x Method: Hiring Early Talent

## Summary

Chapter 5 argues that hiring and retaining talent at the earliest stage is as critical as at later stages, but with even higher stakes: early hires shape everything, including product quality, customer acquisition, brand, and company culture. The chapter rejects the assumption that structured hiring practices (scorecards, one-on-ones, feedback) are premature at early stages, demonstrating that founder attention to recruitment pays immediate dividends. It details who to hire first (engineers with flexibility, then a founder associate; notably, not a product manager), followed by practical guidance on active recruitment, building employer brand, writing compelling job descriptions, and running effective interviews. The chapter emphasizes that compensation goes beyond salary, covering equity allocation timing, vesting structures, and ESOP communication. Finally, it addresses retention through management routines (weekly one-on-ones, monthly development meetings), compensation frameworks, and the difficult decision of parting ways with underperformers.

## Key Takeaways

- **Your first 10 hires are cofounders without the title** — Early hires carry outsized weight and shape the company's trajectory. The bar must be high from day one because these first people set standards and hire the next generation. Founders should spend 30–40% of their early-stage time on recruiting and HR; this cannot be delegated, and understanding hiring firsthand is essential before bringing in specialist roles.
- **Hire engineers first, then a founder associate** — Your first 1–3 hires should be flexible, full-stack engineers willing to adapt as the product evolves. Never outsource the first build to freelancers; technical founders must remain hands-on, embedding long-term vision into code. The first non-technical hire should be a versatile founder associate (a generalist executive, not a specialist like a PM or Head of Sales), capable of plugging into whatever the company needs most.
- **Do not hire a product manager early** — Product vision must live with founders until product-market fit is achieved. Hiring a PM too early creates misalignment and slows the rapid learning loops essential at this stage. PMF cannot be outsourced; it demands founder obsession, talking to customers, shipping fast, and adjusting constantly.
- **Hire for attitude AND skills, not just attitude** — Unlike later-stage hiring, early-stage candidates need both. They must be highly adaptable, deeply autonomous, and entrepreneurial; they should already know how to operate in fast-moving environments and solve problems independently. Hire people who share your superpower (reinforce what you do best) rather than always filling your weaknesses.
- **Use scorecards and structured evaluation to reduce bias** — Create objective frameworks with 6–12 clearly defined criteria (must-have, should-have, nice-to-have) mixing hard and soft skills. Ask the same questions to every candidate, conduct thorough reference checks (with structured scripts to uncover actual working style), and use case studies based on problems you've already solved. This rigor keeps the bar high and compensates for founder inexperience in hiring.
- **Compensation structure: meaningful equity plus empowerment** — Early salaries rarely compete with scale-ups; instead, offer meaningful equity and genuine ownership. Use a simple salary grid (not à la carte negotiation) to ensure fairness and consistency. Allocate 5–10% of post-seed cap table to the ESOP; first 5–10 hires receive outsized grants tied to performance, not just salary. Standard vesting is 4 years with a 1-year cliff. The story and vision matter more than the share count; strong onboarding and consistent management routines (weekly one-on-ones, monthly development meetings) drive long-term retention.

## Quotes

> Consider the first 10 people you hire with the same level of rigor as you would for a cofounder. Some first employees can eventually become late- stage cofounders, their impact can be just as crucial.

> A company isn't built by founders alone, it's built by the entire team. But it can be undone by the founders alone... That's why your early hires are so important: they set the tone for the culture, the ambition, and the way your company thinks as it scales.

> One thing that's been key is involving engineers deeply in the product. Product scopes and defines the goals, but engineers co-design the solution with them. That tight collaboration has been part of our DNA from day one.

## Concepts

- [[Early Hiring]] — Early employees set the company's standard, culture, and execution rhythm. The first 10 hires warrant the same rigor as cofounder selection because their impact shapes everything downstream: product quality, customer acquisition, brand, and retention. Founders must spend 30–40% of their time on recruitment, understanding the hiring process firsthand before delegating it, and moving fast to close candidates (time-to-hire should be 2–3 weeks, time-to-fill under 3 months).
- [[Team Compensation]] — Early-stage compensation combines modest salaries with meaningful equity and genuine ownership. Use a simple salary grid to ensure fairness without à la carte negotiation; allocate 5–10% of the post-seed cap table to the ESOP, with first 5–10 hires receiving outsized grants tied to performance. Standard vesting is 4 years with a 1-year cliff. The story, vision, and empowerment matter more than the share count; well-structured ESOP communication helps early hires understand upside and aligns incentives across the company.
- [[Operating Rituals]] — Weekly one-on-one conversations (30–60 minutes) track progress, surface blockers, and address challenges; monthly development meetings zoom out to assess career goals and co-create development plans. Onboarding milestones anchor integration (week one), feedback exchange (month one), and go-no-go decisions (month three). These routines keep performance and growth on track, embed feedback as a habit, and scale naturally as the team grows.
- [[Founder Mindset]] — Early hires must be builders, not task-doers; highly adaptable, deeply autonomous, and entrepreneurial. They should already know how to operate in fast-moving environments, solve problems independently, and contribute from day one. Assess entrepreneurial mindset through structured questions about what they're exploring, why they're looking, and how they'd decide between offers; look for people whose inner motivation aligns with building something new, not chasing prestige or compensation alone.
