# Storytelling in Design: Chapter 2. The Anatomy of a Great Story

## Summary

Chapter 2 examines the foundational structures and principles that underlie great stories, establishing a bridge between traditional storytelling theory and product design practice. Dahlström presents Aristotle's seven golden rules of storytelling (plot, character, theme, diction, song/chorus, decor, and spectacle) and shows how they translate across industries like film and UX design. She then traces two major structural models—Aristotle's three-act structure (setup, confrontation, resolution) and Freytag's five-act pyramid (exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, denouement)—before addressing the challenge that most digital product experiences are non-linear and lack a single endpoint. The chapter introduces the concept of "circular narratives" where multiple substories link together, requiring designers to think about multiple entry points, middles, and ends. Finally, Dahlström draws five key lessons from diverse storytelling disciplines: the dramatic question (from screenwriting), Disney's attention to detail, Pixar's story basics, video games' immersive worlds, and the shape of great public speeches, all of which offer actionable insights for product design.

## Key Takeaways

- **Aristotle's seven golden rules, aimed at Greek theater, remain relevant and are considered essential for screenwriting** — Plot, character, theme/thought/idea, speech, chorus, decor and spectacle. Interpretations rename some of them by industry: in the table Dahlström sources to Jeroen Vaan Geel, film turns chorus into song/music and decor into production design/art direction, while the UX interpretation turns speech into diction and chorus into melody. Aristotle held plot to be more important than character, though most screenwriters today would call them two sides of the same coin.
- **Narrative structure requires a clear beginning, middle, and end with causal relationships** — Although Aristotle defined this for linear stories, even non-linear digital experiences need discernible narrative shape; the structure guides both the user's experience and what the designer communicates about the product's purpose.
- **Digital experiences are circular, not linear: multiple beginnings, middles, and ends** — Most product journeys involve overlapping substories and multiple entry/exit points; users jump between touchpoints across devices and contexts, requiring designers to plan for multiple narrative loops rather than one finite story arc.
- **The dramatic question focuses the entire experience** — Just as a novel's central dramatic question keeps readers engaged, a product's major (and supporting) dramatic questions clarify what users are trying to achieve and what the experience is designed to resolve.
- **Detail and delight matter deeply** — Disney's philosophy of meticulous attention to every aspect of an experience, combined with a practice of "plussing" (continuously improving), demonstrates that thoughtful details compound into memorable, emotionally resonant experiences.
- **Story structure translates into design decisions at every scale** — Pixar's guidelines (focus on theme, simplify and combine, challenge characters, know your ending first) and insights from video games (UX designer Joanna Ngai on the player being dropped into a fully fleshed-out world whose aesthetic touches interface, voice and interactions) teach designers to articulate their intentions explicitly and structure the user's journey as a coherent whole.

## Concepts

- [[Narrative Structure]] — Dahlström establishes narrative structure as fundamental to storytelling, and traces dramaturgy back to Gotthold Ephraim Lessing, who invented it in the 18th century as a practice-based discipline examining the context of a play. She presents both Aristotle's three-act model (setup, confrontation, resolution) and Freytag's five-act pyramid (exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, denouement) as proven frameworks that guide how stories unfold and how events logically follow from one another.
- [[Character Development]] — Character is one of Aristotle's seven golden rules, and the chapter locates character development in Act 2 of the three-act structure: the *character arc*, where the protagonist has to learn new skills and come to a higher awareness of who they are.
- [[Experience Shape]] — Dahlström introduces the concept that stories have recognizable shapes—visual arcs that communicate emotional intensity over time; she references Nancy Duarte's observation that great talks follow a shape (What Is → What Could Be → What Is...New Bliss) that resonates emotionally with audiences.
- [[Emotional Design]] — The power of great stories, across all mediums examined (Disney parks, Pixar films, video games, public speaking), stems from their ability to create emotional connection; designing for emotional resonance—not just functionality—is what transforms a product experience into a memorable story.
- [[Storytelling]] — The chapter expands the understanding of storytelling by showing its universal principles across traditional media (theater, film, novels) and demonstrating how those same principles apply to product and digital experiences; Dahlström argues that mastering these structures is essential for designing holistic, engaging user experiences.
