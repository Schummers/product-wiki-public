# Checklist for Moderating a Usability Test

## Summary

This article provides a practical 10-step framework for moderating qualitative usability tests. Each step, from welcoming participants to thanking them at the end, addresses specific actions and communication strategies. The framework helps researchers avoid forgetting essential items and ensures consistent participant experience. A facilitator's guide—a document used only by the researcher containing scripts and checklists—helps standardize the moderation process across multiple sessions.

## Key Takeaways

- **Avoid the word test** — Describe the session as "research" or a "study" to avoid making participants feel they are being judged on their abilities; the design is being tested, not the user.
- **Build rapport early** — Check pronunciation of participant names, welcome them warmly, and put them at ease; nervous participants may not provide authentic feedback.
- **Get informed consent** — Have participants sign a consent form with time to read and ask questions; check for proper completion and note any opted-out conditions before proceeding.
- **Conduct brief interviews if needed** — A short interview about participant background can help tailor tasks, but avoid priming questions that might bias later behavior.
- **Set clear expectations** — Explain that tasks will be given one at a time, you will take notes rather than help, and you want honest feedback both positive and negative.
- **Use followup questions** — After each task, start with broad open-ended questions before asking specific ones; this captures natural reactions before priming specific elements.
- **Involve observers appropriately** — Reserve the last 5 minutes for observer questions; observers can ask directly or give questions to the facilitator.
- **Thank and compensate** — Thank participants for their time, provide compensation before the session ends (ideally), and handle recordings and materials appropriately.

## Quotes

> Unlike a research plan or test plan, a facilitator's guide is a document that is produced for and used solely by the researcher. It usually includes an introduction script, as well as checklists of items the researcher should not forget to do or say.

> It's important to put the participant at ease, since some participants may feel nervous and not sure what to expect. Avoid the word "test", since it can make participants think they are being tested.

> Just to let you know, we're not testing you, we're testing the design. If something doesn't make sense to you or is difficult, that's something we need to know so we can make the design easier to use.

## Concepts

- [[Usability Testing]] — A research method requiring the facilitator's careful moderation to capture authentic user behavior and feedback without bias or intimidation, achieved through creating a comfortable environment, following a structured process, and gathering meaningful data through carefully timed questions.
- [[Research Methods]] — Usability testing is one method among many; successful execution depends on proper participant setup, clear instructions, and thoughtful questioning.
