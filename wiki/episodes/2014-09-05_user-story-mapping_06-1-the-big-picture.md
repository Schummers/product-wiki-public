# User Story Mapping: 1. The Big Picture

## Summary

This chapter introduces story mapping as a solution to a central problem in Agile development: how to maintain visibility of the whole product while working in iterative cycles. Patton begins by acknowledging that while Agile teams see progress every few weeks through working software, many team members lose the big picture. He then walks through the history and principles of user stories, starting with their original intent under Kent Beck's Extreme Programming and how the practice evolved. The chapter uses Gary Levitt's experience building Mad Mimi to illustrate story mapping in action: how Patton helped Gary externalize his product vision from a flat prioritized backlog into a spatial map that revealed the true size and complexity of his product. The chapter introduces core practices like "talk and doc" (externalizing thinking onto cards), framing the product idea, describing customers and users, and telling the users' stories from left to right, then filling in details vertically. It concludes with a contributed sidebar by Ceedee (Clare) Doyle, Agile project manager and coach at Assurity Ltd in Wellington, on The Learning Connexion, a New Zealand art college, showing how story mapping helped a diverse team build shared understanding of their business process and prioritize what was truly vital.

## Key Takeaways

- **Stories are for telling, not writing** — Stories get their name from how they should be used: as conversation starters that drive shared understanding, not as static written requirements.
- **The flat backlog problem** — Prioritizing a flat list of features can lead teams to build features that don't solve the real user problem or deliver value, as happened with Gary's initial Mad Mimi development.
- **Talk and doc externalize thinking** — Writing ideas on cards as a team tells stories prevents ideas from "vaporizing"; it lets people refer back and reorganize, and pointing to cards helps everyone recall the conversation.
- **Story maps reveal the big picture** — Organizing stories spatially—left to right for sequence, top to bottom for details—helps teams see the breadth of the entire user journey before diving into details.
- **Framing and customers come first** — Before telling stories about features, teams must frame why they're building the product, who will use it, and which users matter most.
- **Story mapping is a pattern, not an invention** — Patton says he did not coin the term until 2007 and then kept meeting people already doing much the same thing; he uses his friend Linda Rising's definition of a pattern: when you describe a great idea and the other person says "yeah, we do something like that, too," it is a pattern rather than an invention.
- **Shared understanding emerges from mapping** — The act of building a story map together surfaces gaps in thinking, reveals where team members disagree, and builds collective ownership of the product vision.

## Quotes

> Stories get their name from how they should be used, not what should be written.

> Talk and doc: write cards or sticky notes to externalize your thinking as you tell stories.

## Concepts

- [[User Story]] — Patton traces the concept back to Kent Beck and Extreme Programming, emphasizing that stories are meant to be told as conversations, not documented comprehensively; the size of what one card represents (a couple of hours, days, weeks or a month of development) is unknowable until the team talks about it.
- [[Shared Understanding]] — The entire chapter emphasizes that the primary value of story mapping is building shared understanding among team members, customers, and stakeholders about what the product does and why.
- [[Story Mapping]] — The core technique: organizing user stories spatially on a surface (cards, sticky notes, or paper) to show sequence left to right and decomposition top to bottom, creating a visual representation of a user journey.
- [[Product Backlog]] — Patton critiques the traditional flat, prioritized backlog as a flawed starting point; story mapping serves as an alternative structure that makes the product's shape visible and helps teams minimize scope.
- [[Workshop Facilitation]] — Patton describes his facilitation practices in detail: framing discussions, writing down ideas quickly, stopping to let teams finish the big story before diving into details, and creating spatial relationships that communicate meaning without words.
