# UX Research: Chapter 7: Recruiting

## Summary

Chapter 7 addresses the critical risk of finding appropriate research participants. The chapter guides teams through identifying target participants, creating recruitment screeners, and selecting from multiple recruitment methods. Recruitment is presented as a risk management problem: poor participant selection undermines all downstream research, while proper screening ensures cohesive datasets. The chapter also covers common excuses teams use to avoid recruiting, such as "we don't have users yet" or "everything is a secret," and reframes them as solvable problems rather than blockers.

## Key Takeaways

- **Participant Identification** — start with existing sources such as past qualitative research, current analytics reports, customer segments from marketing, or write baseline user profiles to guide recruitment; this ensures recruitment criteria align with research goals and product strategy.
- **Recruitment Screeners** — are often surveys of multiple-choice questions, which allow a binary decision: certain responses disqualify a candidate, others confirm their desirability. A screener should take no longer than 10–15 minutes, and shorter is always better. Skipping screening to research a friend, family member or someone off the street exposes you to risks and gaps in your findings.
- **Behavioral Demographics** — beyond age and income, researchers should screen for behaviors relevant to the product, such as online shopping habits, how participants resolve issues, or where they use a product.
- **Quotas** — assuming participant availability is not an issue, qualitative research typically targets 3–5 participants per user profile; quotas help avoid skewed participant pools and ensure recruitment matches research importance.
- **Recruitment Methods** — options range from self-recruiting (DIY, slow, minimum 2 weeks lead time) and in-person intercepts to online intercepts, cold calling, and outsourcing to third-party firms or client-based recruiters.
- **Backups and No-Shows** — scheduling backup participants is expected practice because no-show rates are common; backup participants may cover multiple sessions and are compensated similarly to regular participants.
- **Handling Mismatched Participants** — occasionally screeners fail and participants arrive who do not fit profiles ("duds"); these sessions still provide data and may require adjusting tactics during the session.

## Quotes

> While it can be tempting to skip screening and conduct research with a friend, family member, or someone off the street, such an approach will expose you to risks and gaps in your findings.

> Assuming participant availability is not an issue, qualitative research generally targets three to five users per user group. These numbers shift based on how important it is to hear from a specific user profile and how difficult it might be to find people that align with one of your profiles.

> Just because a user pool is small, however, doesn't mean it should be a challenge to reach. In fact, it's the opposite. Small user groups are a focused audience.

## Concepts

- [[Participant Recruitment]] — identifying, screening, and scheduling research participants through methods ranging from DIY to outsourced recruiting.
- [[Study Design]] — defining participant quotas, behavioral demographics, and screening criteria to ensure the participant pool matches research goals.
- [[Research Planning]] — documenting target profiles and recruitment screeners before recruiting begins, informed by existing data and stakeholder input.
- [[Bias in Research]] — avoiding bias in recruitment by using appropriate screeners rather than assuming "everyone is a user" or recruiting convenient contacts.
- [[User Research]] — participant selection as a foundational step to any user research effort, whether qualitative or quantitative.
