# How and Why to Recruit Backup Participants (aka \"Floaters\") in User Research

## Summary

No-show participants and last-minute cancellations disrupt project schedules, waste money, and reduce research insights. Intentional overrecruiting (recruiting extra "floater" participants) protects against these disruptions. Three overrecruiting models exist: one floater per participant (most expensive, least risk), one floater per two participants (moderate cost and risk), and scheduling extra research sessions (least expensive). Floaters should be honestly recruited using same screening criteria as main participants, clearly informed of their role, and professionally dismissed if not needed.

## Key Takeaways

- **When to recruit floaters** — Floaters are valuable when project schedules allow only small time windows, external factors (equipment rental, lab availability) impose time limits, participant background requires on-site screening, or observers must see particular sessions for buy-in and credibility.
- **Model 1: One-to-one** — Recruit two people per time slot. If scheduled participant arrives, floater is released and paid honorarium (e.g., $100). Most expensive ($1800 for 5 users with floaters vs. $900 without) but lowest risk. Floaters paid same as regular participants.
- **Model 2: One floater per two participants** — Floater covers two sessions, paid double honorarium (e.g., $200) if covering two sessions. Less expensive ($1640 vs. $900) but higher risk. Floater released if both scheduled participants show.
- **Model 3: Extra sessions** — Schedule additional research sessions (e.g., 5 needed + 2 extra for 7 total participants). Least expensive ($1260 vs. $900) but slowest (1.5 days vs. 1 day). For unmoderated remote research, recruit 1 extra for every 3 sessions. Cancel unneeded sessions but pay participants.
- **Recruitment and dismissal** — Be honest with floaters about their role. Frame it as ensuring full sessions through insurance against cancellations. Dismiss professionally without implying floater did something wrong. Can use floaters to select best-fit participants if both show up.
- **Insurance trade-off** — Deciding whether to overrecruit is like deciding whether to buy insurance. Consider whether guaranteed research results at specified time justify the extra cost to your team.

## Quotes

> When I run research projects, I try to spend enough money on recruiting and incentivizing participants, to ensure that (1) I find the right kind of users and (2) that they are motivated enough to show up for the sessions (and are also fairly compensated for it).

> Don't forget the reduced research insights stemming from fewer research participants than the plan called for. If you follow our recommendations for small sample sizes for most qualitative studies, each lost participant represents a fairly substantial proportion of your potential insights, and thus reduces the profitability of your design project.

> Deciding to use these overrecruiting methods is a lot like deciding whether to buy optional insurance.

## Concepts

- [[Participant Recruitment]] — Strategic overrecruiting using floaters or extra sessions protects research schedules against no-shows and cancellations, ensuring adequate data collection while balancing costs.
- [[Research Budgeting]] — Overrecruiting adds costs but prevents expensive waste from unused research slots (facilitator time, lab space, team members' time, opportunity cost). Cost-benefit analysis depends on project constraints and budget.
- [[Study Design]] — Different overrecruiting models suit different research scenarios. Time-constrained projects favor one-to-one floaters; flexible schedules favor extra sessions. Remote unmoderated studies can use lower overrecruit ratios than moderated studies.
