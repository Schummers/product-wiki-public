# Complex Application Design: A 5-Layer Framework

## Summary

Complex applications support broad, unstructured goals and nonlinear workflows for specialized users with expertise. Beyond the applications themselves, complexity arises from five distinct layers: integrative (underlying technology and systems), information (data volume and interaction behaviors), intention (unstructured goals and variable workflows), environmental (physical surroundings and real-time context), and institutional (organizational culture and power structures). Understanding these five layers helps UX practitioners break down the sources of complexity and design more effectively rather than being overwhelmed by the entire picture.

## Key Takeaways

- **Definition of complexity** — Complex applications support specialized users navigating broad, unstructured goals in nonlinear workflows. They often require specialized domain knowledge, manage large data sets, enable advanced sensemaking, and involve high-stakes tasks where errors have significant consequences.
- **Integrative complexity** — Stems from interconnection of multiple legacy systems and databases. Poor UX manifests as slow, manual processes like tedious data transfer and data entry as users reference different systems to make comparisons or inquiries.
- **Information complexity** — Refers to data volume and the interaction behaviors needed to navigate and analyze large data sets. When data is not organized to support domain workflows, clutter and confusion result. Expert users often perform sustained waiting periods during data analysis.
- **Intention complexity** — Associated with supporting unstructured goals and broad tasks. Workflows are nonlinear with no known ideal path. Designers often don't fully understand the range of use cases or workflows their application must support, particularly in open-box enterprise software.
- **Environmental complexity** — Relates to physical surroundings of users, such as medical practitioners caring for patients while using an application or pilots managing multiple environmental aspects simultaneously. Understanding environmental context requires firsthand, real-time field work and in-situ user testing.
- **Institutional complexity** — Concerns organizational culture, norms, attitudes, roles, and institutionalized ways of doing things. Legacy approaches and resistance to innovation can limit design improvements, and UX practitioners may struggle for credibility among domain experts.

## Quotes

> We define a complex application as any application supporting broad, unstructured goals or nonlinear workflows.

> The cleanliness and the consolidation of the data is probably the most challenging thing…because you can imagine if a record set began being captured 15 or 20 years ago, it's in a very different place than a record set that we capture today, right? It's in an old legacy system, old legacy database. It's not structured; it's not containerized.

> They[the medical practitioners] have so many things flashing in front of their face at all times...in addition to having to care for the person who's right next to that computer…Making sure that it [the information on the application] is distilled down to exactly what somebody needs, exactly where they need it, is extremely important.

## Concepts

- [[Complex Applications]] — A framework of five layers of complexity helps designers understand the sources of difficulty in designing for specialized domains and specialized users navigating unstructured goals.
- [[Application Architecture]] — Integrative complexity arising from legacy systems and technical debt requires careful management to reduce manual processes and create unified interfaces.
- [[Specialized Domains]] — Designing for specialized domains requires understanding not just the information and intention layers, but also the environmental and institutional contexts in which domain experts work.
