# Mixed-Methods Research: Combining Qualitative and Quantitative Data

## Summary

Mixed-methods research intentionally integrates qualitative and quantitative methods within a single research project to answer the same overarching research question. Rather than using each method in isolation, effective mixed-methods research plans both approaches together from the start, aligning them under shared goals and analyzing data in relation to one another. Qualitative methods provide context and explain the "why," while quantitative methods show measurable patterns at scale. The article describes three common research designs (explanatory sequential, exploratory sequential, and convergent parallel) and provides guidance on planning, aligning methods to goals, and thinking about data integration rather than mere addition.

## Key Takeaways

- **Intentional Planning is Essential** — Mixed-methods research must be deliberately designed from the start, with qualitative and quantitative methods aligned under the same goal; ad-hoc addition of methods later differs from intentional mixed-methods design.
- **Balance Methodological Strengths** — Combining methods addresses blind spots of single-method approaches; qualitative research uncovers the "why" that quantitative methods cannot explain, while quantitative research reveals patterns and trends that small qualitative samples might not generalize.
- **Three Research Design Patterns** — Explanatory sequential starts with quantitative to identify patterns then qualitative to explain them; exploratory sequential starts with qualitative to generate hypotheses then quantitative to test them; convergent parallel conducts both simultaneously for independent but complementary evidence.
- **Integration Matters More Than Addition** — The value comes from how data works together, not simply from having both types; researchers should consider how results connect, whether one method explains or informs the other, and whether findings triangulate.
- **Resource and Timeline Tradeoffs** — Mixed-methods research requires more time and resources than single-method studies, including managing multiple protocols, participant groups, and data types; teams must plan for longer timelines and closer coordination.
