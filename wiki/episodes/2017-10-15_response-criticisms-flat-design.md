# Response to Criticisms of Flat-Design Eyetracking Study

## Summary

This article responds to criticisms of Nielsen Norman's eyetracking study on flat design by clarifying the research methodology and findings. The core finding is that weak or absent visual signifiers — a common feature of flat design — attract less attention than strong, traditional visual cues. The article addresses common misunderstandings: that the study only tested 2D vs. 3D, that comparisons were unfair, that sample sizes were too small, and that flat design can be done well when signifiers are strong enough.

## Key Takeaways

- **Triangulation across two years** — findings stem from two years of research using multiple methods, not just eyetracking; this multi-method approach increased confidence in conclusions and showed representative site selection.
- **Signifiers, not flatness itself** — the problem is not flat design inherently but weak or absent visual signifiers that flat design frequently employs; good flat design with strong signifiers works fine.
- **Statistical significance** — results at p<0.05 level provide 95% confidence that findings are real, far better than guessing; the study tested 9 sites across 6 domains, more breadth than most practitioners do.
- **Context of findings** — the study's limitations are acknowledged (small tasks, only 70 users, fine-grain metrics); coarser measures like task time may not show differences, but fine-grain analysis reveals hidden costs.
- **Real design impact** — every comparison came from real flat websites; many well-funded companies do design pages exactly like the test stimuli, showing the findings apply to actual practice.

## Quotes

> flat design can be done well. Flat design isn't the enemy — weak or absent signifiers are.

> they sometimes struggle more to find what they're looking for
