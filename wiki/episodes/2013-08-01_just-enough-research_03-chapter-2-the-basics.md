# Just Enough Research: Chapter 2: The Basics

## Summary

The chapter provides a practical operational framework for conducting research. It starts with the principle that entire design teams should participate in research to shift thinking and enable better application of findings, but designates one person as research lead. Hall then addresses how to define research purpose by connecting activities to specific project decisions. She categorizes research into four types: generative (exploratory, before the problem is defined), descriptive (understanding a defined problem's context), evaluative (testing proposed solutions iteratively), and causal (understanding why implemented solutions behave as they do). The chapter details eight research roles—author, interviewer, coordinator, notetaker, recruiter, analyst, documenter, observer—that can be distributed across team members. A substantial section addresses common organizational objections to research (no time, no budget, CEO will dictate, need to be a scientist, need infrastructure, etc.) noting that at the root of most of them lies a mix of laziness and fear. Hall then situates research across different contexts: freelance, agency, in-house at established companies, startups, and agile teams. She closes with guidance on managing bias (design, sampling, interviewer, sponsor, social desirability, Hawthorne effect), ethical research conduct (consent, safety, privacy, project integrity), and determining "just enough" by identifying high-risk assumptions and listening for the "satisfying click" when data patterns emerge.

## Key Takeaways

- **Team participation shifts thinking** — ideally everyone on the design team also participates in the research rather than receiving a report, because people who have a hand in collecting the insights look for opportunities to apply them, and because being the smart person is more fun than obeying the smart person, which is how the dynamic feels when designers are merely the recipients of the analysis.
- **Research purpose drives method selection** — Research activities must support specific project decisions. Generative research helps define the problem; descriptive research understands an identified problem's context; evaluative research tests proposed solutions; causal research explains post-launch behavior.
- **Eight roles, one lead** — Hall identifies author (plans), interviewer (interacts with participants), coordinator (schedules), notetaker (captures data), recruiter (screens participants), analyst (finds patterns), documenter (reports), and observer (watches). A research lead keeps everyone on protocol; roles are clusters of tasks, not people.
- **Objections root in laziness and fear** — Common obstacles ("no time," "no budget," "need to be a scientist") mask, for most of them, three actual reasons: not wanting to be bothered, being afraid of being wrong, and being uncomfortable talking to people. Hall's counter is that you do not have time to be wrong about your assumptions, and that upfront research makes the rest of the work go much faster.
- **Context determines approach** — Freelancers must cost research explicitly into fees. Agencies can bundle research into scoping. In-house teams can tap customer service. In a startup the approach and biases of the founder and investors might dominate, so anyone else has to be very clear about the value of research and savvy about making the case. Agile teams can decouple research planning from development cycles and recruit continuously every three weeks.
- **Six types of bias to watch** — Design bias (personal goals in study structure), sampling bias (unrepresentative participants), interviewer bias (inserting opinions), sponsor bias (favorable to the funding organization), social desirability bias (participants want to appear good), and Hawthorne effect (behavior changes when observed).
- **Ethics as professional responsibility** — Hall calls it "our responsibility as professionals to proceed without deceiving or injuring any of the participants", and gives a starter set of concerns: informed consent as the rule, safety and privacy, honesty about the study, and the project as a whole (if success leads to harm, do not participate).
- **Just enough is when patterns emerge** — there is no answer to the question of enough other than the point at which you feel sufficiently informed and inspired, but one way to know is to listen for the "satisfying click" of the pieces falling into place. Identifying the assumptions that carry the biggest risk is how to avoid unnecessary research.

## Concepts

- [[Research Planning]] — Hall argues research activities must be chosen to support specific project decisions and situated in the researcher's professional context (freelance, agency, in-house, startup, agile), with explicit purpose statements and expected outcomes.
- [[Research Methods]] — The chapter distinguishes generative (exploratory), descriptive (understanding), evaluative (testing), and causal (post-hoc explanation) research, and emphasizes matching method to the specific questions the project needs answered.
- [[Stakeholder Engagement]] — Hall advocates team participation in research execution, not just consumption of reports, to shift thinking and enable application. She also addresses how to secure organizational buy-in before starting.
- [[Research Ethics]] — The chapter outlines informed consent, confidentiality, safety, honesty about study purpose, and the principle that the project itself must be ethical—good methodology does not justify harmful outcomes.
- [[Bias in Research]] — Hall catalogs design bias, sampling bias, interviewer bias, sponsor bias, social desirability bias, and the Hawthorne effect, arguing that acknowledging bias allows appropriate weighting of results without claiming impossible objectivity.
