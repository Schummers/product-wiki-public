# Product Management for UX People: CHAPTER 1: What Exactly Does a Product Manager Do?

## Summary

Chapter 1 answers the title question with a single word, value, then spends the rest of the chapter separating the role from its neighbours and tracing where it came from. The product manager is responsible for value "through the coordination and delivery of customer experiences," making sure the experience delivered to customers and other stakeholders is valuable enough to be "hired" by the user and developed as a sustainable concern. Crumlish borrows one dimension of sustainability from LinkedIn product lead B. Pagels-Minor ("Something the user values and repeatedly uses") and a fuller definition of value from Jay Zaveri, his chief product officer at the start-up CloudOn and later head of a product incubator at Social Capital.

The chapter then distinguishes product manager from project manager (a specialist whose core expertise is keeping projects on track, on time and on budget, quoting Matt LeMay of Sudden Compass: product managers have "both the opportunity and the responsibility to ask 'why?'") and from product owner (defined for the book as a tactical, engineer-centric role focused on tracking tasks, "invented in the absence of true product managers"). It then walks five archetypes the role descends from: business manager, marketing manager, engineering manager, experimental explorer and creative artist, naming Marty Cagan, Rich Mironov, Eric Ries, Jeff Gothelf and Josh Seiden along the way. It closes on three shared personality traits and defers the "what does a PM do all day" question to Chapter 2.

## Key Takeaways

- **Responsibility for value** — Crumlish's one-line definition is that the product manager is responsible for value, through the coordination and delivery of customer experiences, so that the experience is valuable enough to be "hired" by the user and sustainable as a concern, ideally in service of a broader vision. Sustainability, he adds, means finding repeatable cycles of inputs and outcomes, with the people and money inputs at least steady; sustaining value to the organization is "a fair share of the value created for the customer."
- **Value defined by Jay Zaveri, not by the author** — Asked what value is, Zaveri answers that it is "something special that a person or customer experiences that never existed in the same way for them in the past," useful, usable and desirable, apparent when something is technologically differentiated, abundantly available, and changes human behavior. He also warns against treating financial and growth metrics as value metrics: "No true value is created by just financial and growth metrics."
- **Not a project manager, not a product owner** — Project management can be one of a PM's responsibilities, but Crumlish says it should be "only a sliver of their time and attention"; a project manager's core expertise is keeping projects on track, on time and on budget rather than defining value and driving strategy. A product owner, in the definition the book adopts, shapes and guides the engineering team, is primarily focused on tracking tasks, and originated from the engineering pool; the chapter notes that in practice the title is used many other ways, including for a primary business stakeholder or, in some government contexts, for a role closer to head of product.
- **Five archetypes, not one origin** — The chapter sets out the product manager as business manager (responsible for business case, strategy and financial viability), as marketing manager (the historic 20th century origin, source of the obsession with customer needs and product-market fit), as engineering manager (technical PM interviews, Google's Associate Product Manager program pioneered by Marissa Mayer), as experimental explorer (Marty Cagan of Silicon Valley Product Group and *Inspired*, Rich Mironov, and the "Build, Measure, Learn" cycle from Eric Ries's *The Lean Startup*, brought to the UX community by Jeff Gothelf and Josh Seiden), and as creative artist (design thinking, the Apple mythology, UX leads moving onto the product track).
- **You do not start with "Build"** — On the lean cycle, Crumlish notes that despite the name and the fact that it is a cycle, "you generally do not start with building": you start by learning or measuring something, then build.
- **Three traits: curious, connecting, courageous** — Great PMs are curious to the point of feeling like a busybody and being "high context"; connecting, in the sense of providing "the glue or lubrication" of emotional intelligence and soft skills that let teams thrive; and courageous, brave enough to take risks, evaluate failures coldly and learn from them, which sets a tone that encourages others.

## Quotes

> The product manager is responsible for value, through the coordination and delivery of customer experiences, and for making sure that the experience being delivered to customers (and other stakeholders) provides enough value to be "hired" by the user and developed as a sustainable concern, ideally in service of a broader vision.

> Product consultant and author Matt LeMay, co-founder of Sudden Compass, put it this way: "Product managers have both the opportunity and the responsibility to ask 'why?'"

> They are curious almost to the extent of feeling like a busybody, being nosy, wanting to "know all the things," keeping tabs on everything, and being incredibly "high context" and thorough about understanding.

## Concepts

- [[Product Management]] — The chapter's definition: responsibility for value through the coordination and delivery of customer experiences, plus the five historical archetypes (business, marketing, engineering, experimental, creative) the role is assembled from, and the three traits Crumlish says great PMs share.
- [[Project Management]] — Distinguished sharply: a project manager's core expertise is keeping projects on track, on time and on budget, not defining value or driving strategy. Project management is a legitimate part of a PM's work but should be only a sliver of it, and both roles being abbreviated "PM" is a source of confusion the book resolves by reserving PM for product manager.
- [[Agile Development]] — The product owner is presented as an Agile scrum role, engineer-centric and tactical, "invented in the absence of true product managers," sometimes paired with a certified scrum master; some businesses treat it as an entry-level product management title.
- [[Value Proposition]] — Jay Zaveri's definition of value is quoted at length (special, previously non-existent for that person, useful, usable and desirable, technologically differentiated, abundantly available, behavior-changing) along with his warning that financial and growth metrics are necessary but not sufficient, and B. Pagels-Minor's shorter test, "Something the user values and repeatedly uses."
- [[Product Strategy]] — The chapter frames strategy as what a PM defines and drives, in contrast with executing a plan, and traces how the strategic repertoire absorbed market sizing from marketing, technical depth from engineering, the "Build, Measure, Learn" loop from lean, and problem reframing from design.
