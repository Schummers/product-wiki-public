# Storytelling in Design: Chapter 1. Why Storytelling Matters

## Summary

Chapter 1 traces the historical and contemporary significance of storytelling, grounding the book's central argument in the human need for stories. Dahlström identifies three core elements that distinguish good stories: capturing imagination, maintaining dynamic development through causal relationships and a unifying theme (which she calls the "red thread," from the Swedish *röd tråd*), and introducing surprise. She then surveys storytelling's evolution from oral tradition to modern digital media, examining how stories have served purposes ranging from passing information and instilling moral values to building community identity and creating early forms of branding. The chapter culminates in discussing storytelling today—from interactive media to the experience economy—and its persuasive power, showing why mastering storytelling is increasingly important for product designers, business leaders, and anyone seeking to connect emotionally with an audience and drive action.

## Key Takeaways

- **Three elements define a good story** — According to Dahlström's conversation with her father (a writer), a good story must (1) capture imagination by creating vivid mental images, (2) have dynamic development driven by causal relationships and a unifying theme (the *red thread*), and (3) include elements of surprise that keep the audience engaged without being fully predictable.
- **Storytelling was one of the finest professions of the Middle Ages, and its skills persist in another form** — Troubadours and bards travelled from town to town as entertainers and educators; being a storyteller no longer takes that form, but the equivalent skill shows up in formats such as PechaKucha nights and in the ability to present and to bring a room together.
- **Each communication medium reshapes how stories are told** — From oral narratives and rock art to the printing press invented by Johannes Gutenberg around 1440, radio, television, and the internet, the medium through which stories travel fundamentally alters their form, audience reach, and the role they play in society.
- **Personal storytelling—dreams, daydreams, visualizations—shapes how we process and communicate** — These everyday forms of storytelling help us consolidate learning, test new ideas, create emotional responses, and practice communicating our experiences and aspirations to others.
- **Stories persuade more effectively than facts or argument** — Research cited by Jonathan Gottschall shows that stories lower our critical guard and influence attitudes, fears, and values more powerfully than factual or persuasive writing; this is why purposeful storytelling is becoming essential in business and product design. Hollywood producer Peter Guber defines a purposeful story as “a story you tell with a specific purpose in mind”; in *Tell to Win* he reports that we are increasingly told that “without a compelling story, our product, idea, or personal brand is dead on arrival” and that “if you can’t tell it, you can’t sell it.”
- **Storytelling helps designers understand users and communicate with stakeholders** — Each user journey is itself a story the user is in the middle of; turning to traditional storytelling helps designers understand the people they design for and align the product story they tell with the user's own story.

## Quotes

> Being able to capture the imagination is also key for creating empathy in the users for whom we're designing, as well as for getting buy-in from clients and internal stakeholders.

> These studies have revealed that when we read dry, factual arguments, we read with our guard up. In other words, we're skeptical and critical. When we're presented with a story, on the other hand, something happens to us.

> When you look at these journeys and break down what a user may think, feel, need, and worry about, and how they may have ended up right there, and where they may go next, then each and every journey also becomes a story.

## Concepts

- [[Storytelling]] — Dahlström traces storytelling from its historical role in passing information and instilling moral values, through its evolution across media, to its modern function in business and product design as a tool for persuasion, emotional connection, and driving intentional action.
- [[Red Thread]] — Introduced as the Swedish concept *röd tråd*, this is the unifying theme or common thread that runs through a story, connecting all events and details into a coherent whole; Dahlström emphasizes that without this thread, a story lacks the dynamic structure that holds an audience.
- [[Emotional Design]] — The chapter establishes that stories persuade and move people to action primarily through emotional connection; without emotional resonance, there is no compelling story, and this emotional power is what makes storytelling so effective as a tool in design and business.
- [[Empathy]] — Dahlström ties empathy to the ability to capture the imagination: capturing it is key both to creating empathy in the users we're designing for and to getting buy-in from clients and internal stakeholders.
