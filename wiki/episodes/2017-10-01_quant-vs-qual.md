# Quantitative vs. Qualitative Usability Testing

## Summary

Usability testing research collects two types of data: qualitative data from observational findings about which design features are easy or hard to use, and quantitative data from metrics like task completion rates and success rates. The article explains how these methods serve different purposes in the iterative design cycle: qualitative methods are best for formative research during redesign to identify specific problems, while quantitative methods are suited for summative evaluation and comparison. Both are essential because they answer different questions and protect against different biases.

## Key Takeaways

- **Qualitative research** — provides direct assessment through observation, allowing researchers to identify which UI elements are problematic or effective, and to ask follow-up questions to probe deeper.
- **Quantitative research** — offers indirect assessment through metrics like task times and success rates, best used for evaluation and comparison with baselines or competitors rather than identifying specific design problems.
- **Statistical significance** — quantitative studies come with formal assurance of confidence (often 95%) through statistical analysis, while qualitative findings lack this mathematical protection but work well with small sample sizes.
- **Benchmarking** — the quantitative process of measuring design performance to compare versions and track usability improvements over time, requiring large sample sizes and strictly controlled conditions.
- **Iterative design** — formative research early in redesign (qualitative) and summative research at beginning and end (either method) together drive the design cycle; one quick qualitative study can uncover 85% of problems with only 5 users.
- **Methodological tradeoffs** — quantitative studies require many participants and strict controls but enable statistical claims; qualitative studies are flexible and cheap but lack objectivity guarantees.
