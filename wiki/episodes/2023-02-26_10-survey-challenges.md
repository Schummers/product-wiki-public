# 10 Survey Challenges and How to Avoid Them

## Summary

Despite their popularity in UX research, surveys have fundamental limitations that researchers must understand to avoid collecting flawed data. This article identifies ten common biases and methodological issues that distort survey responses: recall bias (forgetting details), recency bias (overweighting recent events), social-desirability bias (portraying oneself favorably), prestige bias (exaggerating achievements), acquiescence bias (tendency to agree), order effects (question sequence impact), mood bias, central-tendency bias, demand characteristics, and random-response bias. For each bias, the article explains the problem and provides preventive strategies. Crucially, surveys measure attitudes and perceptions, not actual behaviors — a distinction often overlooked by practitioners.

## Key Takeaways

- **Surveys measure attitudes, not behaviors** — surveys capture what users think and feel, not what they actually do; observational methods are required to study actual user behavior.
- **Recall bias threatens accuracy** — participants forget details over time, so surveys should be distributed as soon as possible after the relevant experience to capture fresher memories.
- **Response filtering is unavoidable** — unlike observational methods, surveys allow participants to filter and self-censor their responses before sharing, introducing systematic distortion.
- **Social desirability causes misreporting** — people distort answers to conform to social norms, making confidentiality/anonymity assurances, indirect questioning, and ranges instead of exact numbers more effective.
- **Question order matters significantly** — the sequence of survey questions influences responses through order effects and recency bias, requiring careful consideration during design.
- **Researchers cannot probe for understanding** — surveys generate ratings and multiple-choice selections without opportunity to ask clarifying questions, limiting insight into why participants responded as they did.

## Quotes

> It is very easy to write a bad survey that gathers flawed data.

> Use surveys to learn what users think and feel, not what they do.

> Unlike observational methods, which reveal real-time behaviors that cannot be easily faked or filtered (such as the ease or difficulty of navigating a new mobile app), surveys are self-reported – which means that respondents get to filter everything they share with researchers before they share it.

## Concepts

- [[Survey Design]] — effective surveys must account for recall limitations, social desirability, question ordering, and the fundamental constraint that surveys measure attitudes rather than behaviors.
- [[Research Methods]] — surveys are best used to complement observational methods, with timing (post-task), wording (ranges vs. exact numbers), and question ordering all affecting data quality.
- [[Response Bias]] — systematic biases in how people respond to surveys include recency, social desirability, prestige, acquiescence, and mood effects, each requiring specific preventive strategies.
