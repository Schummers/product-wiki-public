# Iterative Design of a Survey Question: A Case Study

## Summary

This case study demonstrates how survey wording affects responses through four rounds of pilot testing. A seemingly straightforward open-ended question about important online decisions was tested iteratively; changes intended to clarify actually introduced bias through priming. Adding an explanatory sentence constraining "significant" biased respondents toward change-related answers. A multiselect question before the main question primed research-related responses. The final version added reassurance that respondents could pick just one important instance. The article illustrates that small wording changes, order effects, and priming require testing with target participants; surveys, like UI designs, are instruments requiring rigorous iteration.

## Key Takeaways

- **Small wording changes significantly alter responses** — Simply changing "World Wide Web" to "online" required only modest modification, but explanatory additions, priming questions, and reassurance language all shifted what participants reported.

- **Explanatory additions introduce bias rather than clarity** — Defining "significant action or decision" prompted respondents to focus narrowly on changes, excluding other types of significant decisions; clarifications can constrain rather than expand interpretation.

- **Preceding questions prime responses through recency and anchoring** — A multiselect question listing activities (especially "Researched a topic" last) biased subsequent open-ended responses toward research-related answers through priming effects.

- **Context matters: pervasiveness of the internet changed interpretation** — In 1998, "finding online information" was unusual and specific; by 2019, it's mundane and frequent, requiring respondents to narrow down among countless instances.

- **Reassurance about multiple valid responses reduces confusion** — Adding "If you can recall several such instances, please describe the one that was the most important to you" helped respondents feel confident selecting any instance without overthinking.

- **Run pilot testing with target demographic, not just colleagues** — 5-10 participants per iteration with think-aloud protocols reveal interpretation issues; testing only with coworkers misses how actual respondents understand language.

## Quotes

> Survey questions are delicate things. Even small details in wording can affect how your respondents interpret and answer them. A carelessly written question can ruin a study, so it's worth a little extra time to perfect your survey.

> People are social animals who can interpret subtle clues and try to behave as (they assume) researchers want them to, even subconsciously.

> Like user-interface designs, surveys need to be tested. In fact, a survey instrument is a design, so treat it as such.

## Concepts

- [[Survey Design]] — Survey questions are sensitive to wording, order, and priming; iterative pilot testing is essential to ensure neutral language and valid responses.

- [[Qualitative Research]] — Open-ended survey questions require careful framing to avoid biasing respondents; small linguistic choices (definitions, preceding questions, reassurance) shape what participants report.

- [[Pilot Testing]] — Surveys benefit from multiple rounds of pilot testing (3-5 people per round) with think-aloud protocols to catch interpretation issues before full-scale data collection.

- [[Question Wording]] — Neutral language, minimal explanation, and strategic reassurance help respondents answer authentically; priming effects and question order significantly influence data quality.
