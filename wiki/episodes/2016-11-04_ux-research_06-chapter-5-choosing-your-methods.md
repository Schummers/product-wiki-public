# UX Research: Chapter 5: Choosing Your Methods

## Summary

This chapter addresses how to select between quantitative and qualitative research methods by emphasizing that no single approach works for all projects. Selection depends on answering several key questions: What questions are you trying to answer? What are stakeholders' goals and what will resonate with them? What sample size is available? Where are participants located? What is the budget? And what is the timeline? The chapter frames these constraints as a "project triangle" where time, budget, and scope compete, and practitioners should identify which two matter most while allowing the third to vary. It then explores combining quantitative and qualitative methods effectively, noting that the best research initiatives borrow from both approaches. The chapter identifies what works well (combining methods that address the same root question, like analytics with contextual inquiries for understanding workflow) and what doesn't work (combining totally disparate methods or expecting the same method to serve different research goals). It emphasizes maintaining focused research sessions while remaining open to new questions that emerge, and stresses that once practitioners understand how methods work and have practiced them, they should feel comfortable adapting methods to their specific needs.

## Key Takeaways

- **Question First** — understanding what questions need answering is the most important factor in determining research approach, requiring reflection on both the design team's questions and stakeholder goals
- **Stakeholder Needs Matter** — understanding whether stakeholders are looking to validate assumptions, define new markets, or prove organizational value helps determine whether quantitative reports, firsthand observation, or another approach will best inform them
- **Sample Size Considerations** — while quantitative methods typically require large data sets rooted in statistics, qualitative methods work better with limited participants; however, this is a general guideline rather than a strict rule, as quantitative studies can work with small data sets and large-scale qualitative studies are common for international products
- **Location Affects Method Choice** — qualitative methods excel when researchers can travel to participants in their environment, but global products may make travel cost-prohibitive, making unmoderated remote validation or other location-flexible methods necessary
- **Budget and Timeline Trade-offs** — site analytics provide low-cost retroactive review once enabled, while contextual inquiries (requiring 90–120 minutes each) demand more time investment; quantitative measures can work in "set-and-forget" mode while qualitative requires ongoing researcher involvement
- **Project Triangle** — when balancing time, budget, and scope constraints, choose the two that matter most and allow the third to be more variable
- **Mixing Methods Best Practices** — the best research combines qualitative and quantitative approaches; identify a qualitative and quantitative measure for the same root question, and use data from one piece of research to inform questions for other research
- **Avoiding Method Mismatches** — don't combine totally disparate methods or expect the same method to serve different research goals; keep research sessions focused on specific goals, and if interested in multiple distinct questions, the authors recommend running separate research tracks rather than forcing them into one method

## Quotes

> Having an approach to selecting the right method is critical. Because no two projects are alike, there is no simple, one-size-fits-all way to choose a method, but the following questions will help you define what methods are most appropriate for your given needs.

> The best way to combine methods is to understand the root of your question and identify a qualitative and quantitative measure for it.

> Research is a muscle and, like all muscles, it gets stronger through practice and repetition.

## Concepts

- [[Research Methods]] — the chapter's primary focus, providing a systematic approach to selecting appropriate methods rather than seeking a one-size-fits-all approach, emphasizing that research methodology is a learnable skill
- [[Research Planning]] — planning is central to method selection, requiring practitioners to clarify their questions, understand stakeholder needs and goals, assess available sample size and location, evaluate budget and timeline constraints, and balance competing priorities using the project triangle
- [[Quantitative Research]] — addressed as one category in the selection equation: rooted in statistics, it often requires large data sets and suits a "set-and-forget" measurement mode like ongoing analytics, though the chapter notes quantitative studies can also be performed with small data sets
- [[Qualitative Research]] — addressed as the complementary category in the selection equation: better suited to limited participant pools and exploring a specific workflow, especially when researchers can travel to participants in their environment, though the chapter notes large-scale qualitative studies are common for international products
- [[Study Design]] — emphasized as an important consideration in research planning, recognizing that once methods are understood, practitioners should feel comfortable adapting approaches to their specific project needs while keeping sessions focused on specific goals
