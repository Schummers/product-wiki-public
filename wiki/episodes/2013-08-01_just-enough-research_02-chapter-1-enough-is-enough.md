# Just Enough Research: Chapter 1: Enough Is Enough

## Summary

The chapter opens with the Segway as a case study in context failure: a highly engineered device that ignored the conventions and real needs of existing transportation systems and collapsed despite massive investment and hype. From this, Hall argues that context is everything for design. She then frames research as a practical tool for overcoming fear of admitting knowledge gaps and breaking free from myth-driven cultures that celebrate individual genius over systematic inquiry. The chapter distinguishes between personal research (finding information), pure research (advancing knowledge in a field), applied research (serving real-world goals), and design research (understanding users within the product design process). It closes by clarifying what research is not: it is not asking people what they like, not a political tool, and applied research is not science, and positions the book as a practitioner's guide for doing research faster and better.

## Key Takeaways

- **Context determines success** — Hall uses the Segway as evidence that superior engineering alone cannot overcome context failure: the device violated transportation conventions, solved no real problem, and could not fit into existing infrastructure or needs.
- **Research as antidote to genius myth** — The chapter argues that design cultures celebrating individual mastery make it hard to say "I don't know." Research is a tool offering a "periscope" to see surroundings clearly and save time and effort by reducing reliance on untested assumptions.
- **Design research is distinct** — Hall separates two senses of the term: in academia, design research is the study of design itself, its purpose and processes; when practicing designers use it, they mean inquiries that are part of designing rather than about design, focused largely on understanding the people for whom they are designing, "often referred to by the dehumanizing but instrumental term end users," and a core part of user-centered design.
- **Research is not liking** — The chapter debunks the idea that asking people what they like yields useful data: "like" is subjective and unmoored from behavior. Hall argues to "quash all liking, and hating too" because behavior and actual usage matter.
- **Applied research has different standards** — Hall clarifies that applied research, used for real-world design goals, does not require the statistical rigor of pure science. Methods can be more relaxed; the research succeeds to the extent it serves the stated goal.
- **Practical framing for practitioners** — The book targets non-researchers, providing a sampler of techniques Hall has found useful at the design agency Mule, unlike the good books on applied qualitative research, which run many hundreds of pages and were mostly written by professional researchers for professional researchers.

## Quotes

> What does the failure of the Segway have to teach design research? That where humans are concerned, context is everything.

> Research is a tool—a periscope offering you a better view of your surroundings. It can be very powerful if applied thoughtfully.

> For a design to be successful, it must serve the needs and desires of actual humans. Strangely, simply being human is insufficient for understanding most of our fellows.

## Concepts

- [[User Research]] — Hall defines design research as inquiry integral to the design work itself, focusing on understanding the people for whom designers build, distinct from academic design research about design theory; The chapter argues research saves time and effort by validating assumptions, reducing risk of solving the wrong problem, and providing stronger arguments for design decisions.
- [[Problem Framing]] — The chapter uses the Segway example to argue that understanding context—how the system fits into existing conventions, infrastructure, and real needs—is essential before designing solutions.
- [[Research Methods]] — Hall distinguishes personal research (finding existing information), pure research (advancing a field through experimentation), and applied research (serving real-world goals with relaxed methods).
- [[Context of Use]] — Hall emphasizes that design success depends on understanding the environment, conventions, and constraints within which the product must operate, not on engineering alone.
