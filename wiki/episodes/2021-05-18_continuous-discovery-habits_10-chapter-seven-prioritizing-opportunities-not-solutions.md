# Continuous Discovery Habits: Chapter Seven: Prioritizing Opportunities, Not Solutions

## Summary

This chapter shifts teams away from the "build trap"—obsessing over shipping features—toward prioritizing customer opportunities that drive outcomes. Product strategy emerges from decisions about which outcomes to pursue, which customers to serve, and which opportunities to address; most teams skip these strategic decisions and jump straight to solutions and roadmaps. The chapter teaches how to use a well-structured opportunity solution tree to assess and prioritize opportunities across four dimensions: opportunity sizing (which affects most customers, most often?), market factors (competitive position, trends), company factors (vision, mission, organizational strengths), and customer factors (importance, satisfaction with alternatives). Teams should embrace the messiness and subjectivity of these comparisons rather than mathematizing decisions, treating opportunity prioritization as reversible, two-way-door decisions that can be course-corrected.

## Key Takeaways

- **Prioritize in the opportunity space, not the solution space** — Strategy is made by choosing which opportunities to pursue, not which features to build; teams that obsess over roadmaps and feature releases are stuck in the build trap.
- **Assess top-level opportunities first, then focus on one branch** — Compare parent opportunities against each other using the four assessment dimensions; once you identify the priority branch, ignore other branches and focus assessment on its children.
- **Use four lenses to assess opportunities** — Opportunity sizing (reach and frequency), market factors (competitive position, external trends), company factors (mission, strategic fit, organizational strengths), and customer factors (importance, current satisfaction); use all four to avoid blind spots.
- **Make subjective, data-informed decisions, not quantitative formulas** — Don't score or stack-rank; instead, compare and contrast siblings, have healthy debate across dimensions, and make the best decision for this moment; leave room for doubt.
- **Frame prioritization as reversible decisions** — Choosing a target opportunity commits only to exploring it further over the next few days or weeks, not to building it; this framing keeps teams mentally flexible and willing to course-correct if new information emerges.

## Quotes

> "The build trap is when organizations become stuck measuring their success by outputs rather than outcomes. It's when they focus more on shipping and developing features rather than on the actual value those things produce."

> "When people create maps of an unknowable, unpredictable world, they face strong temptations toward either overconfident knowing or overly cautious doubt. Wisdom consists of an attitude toward one's beliefs, values, knowledge, and information that resists these temptations through an ongoing balance between knowing and doubt."

> "You are never one feature away from success…" The crowd erupts with cheers. But I'm not done. I wait patiently for the crowd to quiet down. I continue, "…and you never will be."

## Concepts

- [[Prioritization]] — The chapter defines prioritization as strategic comparison of opportunities (not features) across four dimensions, using relative judgments between siblings rather than absolute scoring; it is a skillfully subjective, reversible decision.
- [[Decision Making]] — Teams make strategic decisions in the opportunity space by comparing sets of opportunities at each level of the tree, using a two-way-door mindset that allows course-correction as new evidence emerges during discovery.
- [[Product Outcome]] — A product outcome (distinct from a business outcome) focuses on customer value; prioritization of opportunities is guided by which opportunities will drive the product outcome, ensuring customer needs remain central to strategy.
