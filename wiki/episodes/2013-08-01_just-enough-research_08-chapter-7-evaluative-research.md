# Just Enough Research: Chapter 7: Evaluative Research

## Summary

Chapter 7 covers evaluative research: assessing whether a design actually works for its intended users and purpose. Hall presents two primary methods for evaluation. Heuristic analysis involves having experts review a design against established usability principles (Nielsen's ten heuristics) without recruiting users—a quick, cheap method useful for catching obvious issues in early prototypes, though it may miss real-world problems. The second and more comprehensive approach is usability testing, which Hall positions as a core design practice throughout a project's lifecycle, from testing sketches and competitor products to analyzing live product data.

The chapter emphasizes that usability is a baseline standard, not a luxury, and that the best evaluation combines both qualitative methods (observing users perform tasks) and quantitative data (analytics showing actual behavior). Hall provides detailed guidance on planning tests, recruiting participants, facilitating sessions, documenting observations, and analyzing results. She ranks the costly cases: the second most expensive usability testing is the kind put off until very late in the process, and the most expensive of all is the kind customers do for you after launch, by way of customer service.

## Key Takeaways

- **Evaluation timing and types** — Early evaluation takes the form of heuristic analysis and usability testing of prototypes and competitors. After launch, quantitative analytics reveal actual user behavior. The best assessment combines both qualitative and quantitative methods: numbers show what is happening, and individual observations reveal why.
- **Heuristic analysis essentials** — Based on Nielsen and Molich's work from 1990, heuristic analysis uses a checklist of ten usability principles (system status visibility, match between system and real world, user control and freedom, consistency, error prevention, recognition over recall, flexibility of use, aesthetic design, error recovery, help and documentation) to identify potential issues quickly with minimal cost. Ideally at least two or three evaluators go through the system individually; the downsides are that it is simplistic, that it might not catch issues that come up in context, and that some expert evaluators flag issues that do not trouble actual users.
- **Usability as quality baseline** — Citing Nielsen, the chapter presents usability as a quality attribute defined by five components: learnability (accomplishing basic tasks first time), efficiency (speed once learned), memorability (reestablishing proficiency after period away), errors (count, severity, and recovery ease), and satisfaction (pleasantness of use). It amounts to basic manners: as a designer or developer, in Hall's words, "you either care about usability, or you're a jerk."
- **Test progression and cost** — Test paper prototypes before moving to digital prototypes, test in the office before field studies, test general audiences before specific ones. Start by testing competitors' products and sketches, then continue testing at every stage. The second most expensive testing is the kind put off until very late in the process; the most expensive of all is the kind customers do for you after launch, through customer service.
- **Usability test planning** — Create a test plan around tasks derived from personas and core user goals. Define deal-breaker tasks (e.g., online shopping cart or ticket purchase) where failure carries high risk. Document number of participants (four to eight per target user type), scenarios, success metrics (completion rate, error-free rate), and methodology.
- **Recruiting and facilitating** — Participants must share key goals with target users to immerse themselves in test scenarios. The facilitator must be personable and patient, avoiding leading questions and hints while embracing uncomfortable silences. The greatest danger when the system's own designer or developer facilitates is that they cannot sit by while their creation fails, and small hints and leading questions creep in.
- **Documentation methods** — Audio recording is valuable and practical; video is data-heavy and requires significant editing effort. Screen capture with audio is efficient. For mobile testing the chapter cites MailChimp's solution, detailed on their blog: the user sets up a video chat on a MacBook and hugs it from behind so the iSight camera catches the interaction on the phone and the microphone catches the audio. The note-taker should record the participant's reaction, task duration, failures, and terminology stumbles, ideally with approximate time codes.
- **Severity and frequency rating** — Rate each problem on two scales: severity (high prevents task completion, moderate causes difficulty but completion possible, low is minor) and frequency (high is 30%+ of participants, moderate is 11–29%, low is 10% or fewer). Combine severity and frequency into three tiers: Tier 1 (high-impact, high risk) demands immediate fixes; Tier 2 is either moderate problems at low frequency or low problems at moderate frequency; Tier 3 (low-impact problems affecting a small number of users) carries a low risk if left unresolved. How core the related task is weighs in too.
- **Competitive benchmark testing** — Beyond testing your own product, conduct benchmark usability studies across your website and competitors' sites using the same set of tasks and scoring system. This reveals overall usability and per-task performance gaps and can be repeated post-redesign to verify improvement.

## Quotes

> Usability is the absolute minimum standard for anything designed to be used by humans. If a design thwarts the intended users who attempt the intended use, that design is a failure from the standpoint of user-centered design.

> Don't use expensive testing—costly in money or time—to find out things you can find out with cheap tests. Find out everything you can with paper prototypes or quick sketches before you move to a prototype.

> As long as you have an open mind, nothing is more interesting and valuable than seeing your precious theories of how people will interact with a design crash against the rocky shoals of reality.

## Concepts

- [[Heuristic Evaluation]] — The chapter introduces heuristic analysis based on Nielsen and Molich's 1990 framework: expert evaluators assess a design against ten usability principles (visibility, system-world match, control, consistency, error prevention, recognition, flexibility, aesthetics, error recovery, help). It is quick and cheap but may miss real-world problems and works best as a sanity check before usability testing.
- [[Usability Testing]] — Hall presents usability testing as a core practice, not a late-stage gate: test competitor products, sketches, and prototypes at every decision point. Tests revolve around task-based scenarios with four to eight participants per user type. Success is measured by completion rate and error-free rate. Results are rated by severity (does it prevent task completion?) and frequency (what percentage of users hit it?), then triaged into three tiers of priority.
- [[Participant Recruitment]] — Effective recruitment requires that participants share key goals with target users, so they can immerse themselves in test scenarios. Maintain an ongoing database of potential participants. Participants are single-use (bringing them back risks tainting from prior experience), so a good supply is essential. Recruiting for usability testing is substantively the same as recruiting for ethnographic interviews.
- [[Analytics]] — Once a product is live, analytics provide quantitative data on how users actually interact with the system. Combined with qualitative usability testing observations, analytics reveal both what users do and why. The chapter emphasizes that understanding actual behavior through data is essential to effective evaluation alongside user observation.
