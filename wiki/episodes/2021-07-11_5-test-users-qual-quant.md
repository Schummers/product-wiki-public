# Why 5 Participants Are Okay in a Qualitative Study, but Not in a Quantitative One

## Summary

The 5-user guideline for usability testing applies only to qualitative studies aimed at identifying issues, not quantitative studies measuring population metrics. This distinction resolves the apparent contradiction between Nielsen's 5-user recommendation and the recommendation for 40+ participants in quantitative studies. Qualitative studies identify usability problems (absolutely valid when any user encounters them) through iterative testing and fixing. Quantitative studies measure how many users will succeed, how long tasks take, and satisfaction levels, requiring large samples to produce narrow confidence intervals for population prediction. The 5-user guideline rests on three assumptions: identifying issues is the goal, any encountered issue is worth fixing, and 31% is the probability of encountering an issue. These assumptions can be questioned: using Nielsen and Landauer's formula, if the probability is lower (10-20%), more users are needed; the real goal is maximizing research ROI, which typically peaks around 5 users even with variability.

## Key Takeaways

- **No Contradiction Exists** — 5-user guideline applies to qualitative issue identification; 40+ users needed for quantitative metrics; these are different study types with different goals.
- **Qualitative Identifies Issues** — Goal is to find usability problems, not to predict population behavior; any issue identified is valid and worth fixing regardless of frequency.
- **Quantitative Predicts Behavior** — Goal is to measure and generalize metrics (success rates, task time, satisfaction) to the population; small samples produce wide, useless confidence intervals.
- **Three Assumptions Behind 5 Users** — (1) goal is identifying issues, (2) any issue someone encounters is valid to fix, (3) probability of encountering an issue is 31%.
- **Probability Varies by Design Maturity** — The 31% number is from 1990s average; better interfaces have lower encounter probability; using Nielsen-Landauer formula, 10-20% probability requires 9-18 users, not 5.
- **ROI Peaks at 5 Users** — Even with parameter variation, peak gain-to-cost ratio (ROI) typically occurs around 5 participants in iterative testing cycles; ROI remains robust to reasonable assumptions.
- **Iterative Testing Strategy** — Test with 5, fix issues found, then repeat; if few insights after first test, add users; if plenty of issues found, fix first then test again.

## Quotes

> There is no contradiction between the 5-user guideline for qualitative user testing and the idea that you cannot trust metrics obtained from small studies, because you do not collect metrics in a qualitative study.

> To make an analogy for this assumption: if one person falls into a pothole, you know you need to fix it. You don't need 100 people to fall into it to decide it needs fixing.

> Qualitative user testing simply gives you a list of problems. It is the researcher job's to prioritize among the different issues and move on.

## Concepts

- [[Qualitative Research]] — Studies identifying usability issues through iterative testing; 5 users sufficient because any issue found is valid, not dependent on frequency.
- [[Quantitative Research]] — Studies measuring population metrics; 40+ users necessary to produce narrow confidence intervals and reliable generalization.
- [[Sample Size]] — Different studies require different sizes based on goals; issue identification needs fewer users than metric prediction.
- [[Usability Issues]] — Problems identified in qualitative studies are valid regardless of how many participants encounter them; prioritization uses judgment, not frequency.
- [[Research Validity]] — Quantitative studies require statistical validity through narrow confidence intervals; qualitative studies require logical validity in issue identification.
