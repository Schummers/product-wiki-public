# Antipersonas: What, How, Who, and Why?

## Summary

Antipersonas represent user groups that could misuse products in ways that harm target users or the business, complementing regular personas by identifying and mitigating risks. Unlike personas designed to build empathy with target users, antipersonas focus on threat prevention. They are necessary when products deal with sensitive information (fraud, identity theft, harassment), create physical or emotional threats (injury, violence, sexually explicit content), or when misuse consequences are severe. An antipersona should include name, goal, motivations, actions, tools, contextual needs, and consequences. Common threat types include thieves, creators of illegal content, spreaders of disinformation, and children accessing inappropriate content. Validation requires research with people similar to the antipersona or experts who understand the threat behavior. The Tide Pods case exemplifies how antipersona research could have prevented catastrophic brand damage and user harm through advance design safeguards.

## Key Takeaways

- **Antipersona definition** — A representation of a user group that could misuse a product, threatening target users' safety, security, confidence, loyalty, or retention.
- **When to create them** — Antipersonas make sense when products deal with sensitive data, pose physical/emotional threats, or involve misuse with severe consequences; weigh likelihood against severity, as even low-probability misuse may justify an antipersona.
- **Essential components** — Include name and face for memorability, goal and motivations (root causes), actions and tools (implementation methods), contextual needs (what enables misuse), and consequences (impact on users and business).
- **Four common threat types** — Thieves (information or physical), creators of illegal content, spreaders of disinformation, and children; these provide starting points for threat identification.
- **Validate through research** — Conduct discovery with people similar to antipersonas or experts in the behavior; direct research is ideal but interviews with security guards, lawyers, or social workers can provide insights.
- **Balances empathy with realism** — Antipersonas aren't about hating users; they're about realistic risk assessment and designing safeguards that don't punish target users unnecessarily.

## Quotes

> An antipersona is a representation of a user group that could misuse a product in ways that negatively impact target users and the business. Product misuse may threaten regular users' safety, security, confidence, loyalty, and retention.

> If there is an opportunity for these harms to occur as the direct result of anyone using the product, there should be one or more antipersonas to represent the risk. Always balance the chance of such a misuse with its consequences in order to determine if an antipersona is worth creating.

> Antipersonas help businesses to anticipate and mitigate risks associated with the misuse of their products. If you are considering an antipersona associated with a possible type misuse, weigh how likely that misuse is with its consequences.

## Concepts

- [[Persona]] — While regular personas build empathy with target users, antipersonas identify threats from misuse; both types inform comprehensive design that serves target users safely.
- [[Risk Management]] — Antipersonas are risk-management tools; they help teams identify and mitigate threats proportional to their severity, from low-probability catastrophic harms to predictable moderate harms.
- [[Security Design]] — Products with sensitive data must consider thieves and unauthorized access; security architecture should be informed by antipersona research on attacker motivations and tools.
- [[Product Safety]] — Physical and emotional harms from misuse require advance consideration; the Tide Pods example shows how advance antipersona thinking could have prevented brand damage and health risks.
- [[User Research]] — Understanding antipersonas requires research similar to persona research; direct studies with threat actors or expert interviews provide evidence for designing effective safeguards.
