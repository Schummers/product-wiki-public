# Product Management for UX People: CHAPTER 2: Do You Want to Be a Product Manager?

## Summary

Chapter 2 is the decision chapter: should a UX practitioner move to product at all? Crumlish sorts the reasons into three families (professional and career, ambition, evolving interests), then tests them. Career pressure is external: at Yahoo, both Larry Cornett, VP of UED for the search product team, and senior director of UED Luke Wroblewski moved onto the product track, which he labels the "if you can't beat 'em, join 'em" approach, while noting the other option is to fight for better opportunities on the UX track. Ambition is legitimate but carries two possible unintended consequences he names: perpetuating an inferior approach to product management, and losing what makes work meaningful. One side effect is not a risk but a guarantee, the burden of decision-making. John Cutler, head of product research and education at Amplitude, supplies a long "From the Trenches" sidebar, his essay "44 Signs You Are Becoming a Product Manager," reprinted with permission.

The chapter then describes the work. Crumlish poses three questions to ask before switching: do I love spreadsheets, am I a good writer, am I deeply curious about interpersonal dynamics. Product management "is not a design job"; the day goes into Jira and Confluence, spec writing, backlog grooming, sprint planning, email, Slack and calendars. Two practitioners qualify the quantitative requirement in a sidebar: Clement Kao says truly amazing B2B PMs can get away without numbers because they are so locked in with each customer, while Matt LeMay says flatly that he does not like numbers, math, analysis, spreadsheets or databases and still has the three qualities described. The "typical day" that follows is Crumlish's own generic composite, from a 4:30 a.m. wake-up to checking data one more time before sleep, and he closes it by saying "that's just me" and promising other PMs' days later in the book. A final section argues that even a reader who now firmly wants to stay in UX benefits from understanding product management.

## Key Takeaways

- **Three families of reasons, all legitimate, all with caveats** — Professional and career (career progression planning, professional interests), ambition (realities of the current org structure, "lust for power"), and evolving interests (waning interest in UX practitioner work, fascination with the business side). Crumlish says all these reasons are legitimate and that you do not need a good reason to explore a growth opportunity, but urges weighing the trade-offs and opportunity costs of pursuing product against sticking with UX.
- **The grass-is-greener test** — He warns against a fallacy that romanticizes the road not taken "and minimizes the mowing, weeding, and manure spreading that produced your neighbor's green lawn," and insists anyone considering the fork understand the day-to-day realities first.
- **Two named unintended consequences of ambition** — Getting more powerful by wearing a product hat may mean perpetuating an inferior approach to product management that dominates UX without using its value wisely, or losing what makes the work meaningful. Crumlish says neither necessarily happens, but the risk is a lot higher if ambition is the sole reason.
- **Three questions, and two practitioners who qualify them** — The chapter's screening questions are whether you love spreadsheets, whether you are a good writer, and whether you are deeply curious about interpersonal dynamics. Crumlish then writes that you "really do have to like numbers, math, analysis, spreadsheets, and databases to be a great product manager" — a claim the sidebar immediately softens: Clement Kao (Principal PM at Blend, cofounder of Product Manager HQ) says truly amazing B2B PMs can get away without numbers since they are so deeply locked in with each customer's priorities, though there is no escaping numbers in B2C; Matt LeMay of Sudden Compass says this is "sometimes but not always true" and that he dislikes all of those things while having the three qualities.
- **What the day actually contains** — Not pushing pixels: writing specification docs, decomposing epics into user stories, grooming the backlog, planning sprints, answering developer questions on tickets, reviewing demos and automated test data, reading burn-down charts, running retrospectives, plus email, Slack, calendar work, biweekly Agile rituals and roadmap reporting to leadership quarterly if not monthly.
- **Data as the PM's material** — Crumlish extends the UX analogy: as a potter develops a feel for clay and a UX practitioner for digital materials, a PM's material is data, requiring "deep immersion for sustained periods," and a PM develops "very sensitive antennae" that react when a morning North Star report shows an unexpected dip in daily active users or a spike in sales.
- **John Cutler's 44 signs** — The sidebar reprints Cutler's list of 44 signs you are becoming a "real" product manager, opening on his question about what actually signals progress and answering "No, it's these 44 signs": being pressured for estimates you know are worthless, building a roadmap everyone buys into before everything changes in an instant, shipping a dud feature nobody uses and an awesome one nobody notices, being "the single wringable neck," being empowered on paper while taking orders.

## Quotes

> "Product management is not a design job. It does require some familiarity with design and a deep sensitivity to user experience concerns, but the day-to-day tasks and craft work of product management rarely involve pushing pixels."

> And Matt LeMay of Sudden Compass raises a good nuanced point: "This is sometimes but not always true. I don't like any of those things ('numbers, math, analysis, spreadsheet, or databases'), but I have the three qualities you described earlier."

> A product manager develops very sensitive antennae (or if you're a pop culture enthusiast, call it a spidey sense) that react fast when the morning report on key North Star metrics heralds an unexpected dip in daily active users or a spike in sales.

## Concepts

- [[UX Career]] — The chapter is a career-decision framework: three families of motivation, the grass-is-greener fallacy, the named risks of moving for ambition alone, and the Yahoo precedents of Larry Cornett and Luke Wroblewski moving from UED leadership to product. It also argues the value of understanding product management for someone who decides to stay in UX.
- [[Product Management]] — A concrete inventory of the daily work (specs, epics and user stories, backlog grooming, sprint planning, ticket answering, demo review, retrospectives, messaging, reporting) and Crumlish's own generic typical day, explicitly labelled as his and not as the role's.
- [[Business Metrics]] — Data is described as the PM's raw material, requiring sustained immersion to acquire a feel for; North Star metrics and daily active users are the morning read. The chapter's own claim that you must love numbers is contradicted in its sidebar by Matt LeMay and qualified by Clement Kao for B2B.
- [[Trade-Off]] — Crumlish frames the whole decision as a trade-off and opportunity-cost calculation rather than an upgrade, and notes what is given up: time in Figma, Sketch or Swift, the creative flow of long uninterrupted design days, and possibly the meaning of the work.
- [[Agile Development]] — The PM's tooling and cadence are Agile scrum: Jira with Confluence, epics decomposed into user stories, biweekly planning, daily stand-ups, demos, retrospectives, burn-down charts, and functioning as a de facto product owner at stand-up.
