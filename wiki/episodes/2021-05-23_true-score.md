# Why You Cannot Trust Numbers from Qualitative Usability Studies

## Summary

Statements like "70% of users completed the task" from qualitative studies are misleading because they report observed scores without acknowledging measurement error. True-score theory explains that the relationship between an observed score (obtained from a small study sample) and the true population score includes measurement error. Small sample sizes produce large measurement errors that make observed scores unreliable predictors of population behavior. Protocol variability in qualitative studies (different facilitator intervention, varying participant behavior, sometimes different tasks) adds additional noise. Researchers should report confidence intervals and statistical significance when presenting numbers from sample-based studies to communicate the true range and reliability of findings.

## Key Takeaways

- **True-Score Theory** — The observed score from a study differs from the true population score by a measurement error; observed scores only predict true scores reliably when measurement error is small.
- **Small Sample Size Produces Large Error** — When studies include only few participants (5-10), personal context and variation cause large measurement error; noise overwhelms the signal making observed scores poor predictors of population behavior.
- **Confidence Intervals Show True Range** — A confidence interval quantifies the likely range for the true population score; for example, 50% of 10 participants means 50% ± 26% (24-76% range), but 50% of 100 means 50% ± 10% (40-60% range).
- **Statistical Significance Compares Observations** — P-values below 0.05 indicate differences are real and not due to chance; without this test, observed differences may be measurement noise rather than actual differences in true scores.
- **Protocol Variability Adds Noise** — Qualitative studies intentionally allow facilitator flexibility, different interventions, and protocol adjustments, which introduce additional noise beyond sample-size limitations.
- **Always Report Statistics** — Never report numbers from sample-based studies without confidence intervals and statistical significance; explicitly state whether results can be generalized to the population or are specific to the study.

## Quotes

## Concepts

- [[True-Score Theory]] — Framework explaining observed scores include measurement error, with error size determined primarily by sample size and methodology consistency.
- [[Research Validity]] — The difference between observed scores and true population scores, larger in small-sample studies and qualitative studies with protocol variability.
- [[Sample Size]] — Small samples (5-10 people) produce large measurement errors where personal context and noise overwhelm signal; larger samples allow noise to cancel out.
- [[Statistical Significance]] — P-values indicate whether observed differences reflect real true-score differences or are just measurement noise; p < 0.05 indicates statistical significance.
- [[Confidence Intervals]] — Range statistics showing the likely bounds for true population scores, directly influenced by sample size with wider intervals for smaller samples.
