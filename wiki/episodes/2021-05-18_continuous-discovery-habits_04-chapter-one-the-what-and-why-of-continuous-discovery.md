# Continuous Discovery Habits: Chapter One: The What and Why of Continuous Discovery

## Summary

This opening chapter introduces continuous discovery as a structured and sustainable approach to customer research integrated into product development. Torres distinguishes discovery (deciding what to build) from delivery (building and shipping), noting that many companies over-invest in delivery while under-investing in discovery. The chapter traces the evolution of product discovery from annual business-driven planning through Agile practices to today's continuous discovery cadence, where product teams engage customers regularly on a weekly basis.

## Key Takeaways

- **Discovery vs Delivery** — A fundamental distinction between deciding what to build and actually building it; many companies focus heavily on delivery while neglecting discovery, leading to building products customers don't want.
- **Product Trio** — A cross-functional team of product manager, designer, and software engineer responsible for making product decisions together, leveraging each role's expertise while balancing speed of decision-making with inclusiveness.
- **Six prerequisite mindsets** — Teams adopting continuous discovery must cultivate outcome-oriented, customer-centric, collaborative, visual, experimental, and continuous mindsets; a focus on value created rather than features shipped is essential.
- **Product Outcome** — Success measured by impact on customers' lives and business sustainability rather than by code shipped; the chapter emphasizes defining success as value created, not outputs delivered.
- **Weekly Customer Touchpoints** — Continuous discovery is defined as minimum weekly touchpoints with customers by the building team, conducting small research activities in service of a desired outcome.
- **Evolution through Agile** — Agile practices shortened delivery cycles from annual to weekly releases and enabled more customer feedback, but teams still struggled with deciding what to build until discovery practices became integrated into daily decision-making.

## Quotes

> As you'll see, many companies put a heavy emphasis on delivery—they focus on whether you shipped what you said you would on time and on budget—while under-investing in discovery, forgetting to assess if you built the right stuff.

> A digital product is never done. It can and should continue to evolve. As we learn more about our market, as our customers' needs change, as new technology becomes available, good products adapt.

> Rather than defining your success by the code that you ship (your output), you define success as the value that code creates for your customers and for your business (the outcomes).
