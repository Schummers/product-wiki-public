# Fitts's Law and Its Applications in UX

## Summary

Fitts's Law is a foundational principle in human-computer interaction that quantifies the relationship between movement time, distance to target, and target size. Developed in the 1950s by psychologist Paul Fitts, it establishes that movement time increases with distance and decreases with target size. The law is particularly valuable for UX designers because it provides concrete guidance for optimizing interface elements to reduce user interaction time and errors. The article explores both the mathematical foundations and practical applications of Fitts's Law across various UI design scenarios.

## Key Takeaways

- **Mathematical relationship** — Fitts's Law states that movement time is proportional to distance and inversely proportional to target size, with the relationship expressed logarithmically so time increases at a slower rate than linear growth.
- **Two-component movement** — Human movement consists of an initial rapid phase that covers distance and a final slower phase that ensures accuracy; smaller targets require proportionally more time in this deceleration phase.
- **Screen-edge advantage** — Targets placed on screen edges act as infinite targets because the screen boundary prevents overshooting, allowing faster acquisition; this principle explains why operating systems place menus and taskbars at edges.
- **Target size matters** — Increasing target size reduces interaction time more effectively than proximity; labels should accompany icons because the combined area is larger and faster to acquire than icons alone.
- **Crowding creates problems** — Placing targets too close together increases accidental overshooting errors, particularly for small targets; adequate spacing is necessary even beyond the active click area.
- **Menu organization** — Menu type affects average distance to items; rectangular menus are more efficient than linear ones, and pie menus offer equal distance to all options, though users find them unfamiliar.

## Quotes

> The bigger the distance to the target, the longer it will take for the pointer to move to it.

> In MacOS, application menus are shown at the very top of the screen, on the horizontal edge. This enables the user to reach that area very easily. In Windows (at least until Windows 8) the start button was placed in the bottom left corner of the screen.

> The second movement phase is, however, driven mostly by size of the target — the pointer needs to slow down in order to not overshoot and click beyond the target.

## Concepts

- [[Movement Time]] — The core principle that designers can minimize user action time by managing distance and size variables in interface design.
