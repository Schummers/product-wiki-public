# Deceptive Patterns in UX: How to Recognize and Avoid Them

## Summary

Deceptive patterns are designs that force users to take actions against their best interests through deception, misdirection, or obstruction. Coined "dark patterns" in 2010, these designs are prolific on the web because they effectively boost conversions, though they are unethical and increasingly illegal under data protection regulations. Deceptive patterns particularly harm vulnerable users like those with lower literacy. Distinguishing deceptive patterns from persuasive design requires examining whether information presented is factually accurate, whether the design respects user autonomy, and whether information is easily accessible. Designers must actively argue against and avoid deceptive patterns.

## Key Takeaways

- **Deceptive patterns manipulate through obstruction, visual tricks, nagging, emotional manipulation, or sneaking** — making it harder to make beneficial choices, exploiting cognitive limitations, pestering users after they decline, shaming users into choices, or adding items without permission.
- **Mild deceptive patterns often go unnoticed** — obvious patterns trigger user anger in testing, but subtle ones escape detection, especially affecting users with lower education; designers must scrutinize designs systematically.
- **Distinguish persuasive from deceptive design** — persuasive design using social proof and scarcity is ethical if information is accurate and exchange is fair; deception appears when designs hide information or mislead about choices.
- **Cognitive walkthroughs identify deceptive patterns** — structured questioning about whether users might spend more than intended, misinterpret choices, miss options, or feel rushed or manipulated reveals problematic designs.
- **Designers must balance business needs with ethics** — the role is to straddle both needs but actively call out deceptive patterns and seek fairer ways for businesses and users to achieve goals.

## Quotes

> A deceptive pattern is a design pattern that prompts users to take an action that benefits the company employing the pattern by deceiving, misdirecting, shaming, or obstructing the user's ability to make another (less profitable) choice.

> Deceptive patterns harm users by causing financial loss, loss of privacy, and legal control. Furthermore, deceptive patterns are more likely to be successful with vulnerable users, such as time-poor users or users with lower literacy and digital literacy levels, making this practice even more troubling.

> As UX practitioners, our job is to straddle the needs of the business that employs us with our users' interests. Designers should call out deceptive patterns when proposed or implemented and seek fairer, more ethical ways of helping businesses and users achieve their goals.
