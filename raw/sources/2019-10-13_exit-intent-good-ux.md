I’m sure you’ve come across them: You try to close a browser tab or move to a different one, and a popup window comes up with some last-minute message to catch your attention. This is an exit-intent popup.

Exit-intent popups are often used by websites to retain visitors that are about to leave the site. Users’ mouse movements are tracked, and when their cursor moves outside the upper page boundary, the popup is triggered.

Most conversations around exit-intent popups are about how companies can use them to their advantage — for example, in order to get people to subscribe to the company’s email newsletter. Gobs of conversion-optimization services tout their effectiveness in generating leads, newsletter subscriptions, and making sales.

Although these techniques may show small increases in conversion metrics, they can also [negatively impact users](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/needy-design-patterns/). Most exit-intent popups ask something from the users, such as their names and email addresses or to like the company on social channels. [Manipulative wording](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/shaming-users/) is often used in exit-intent popups to shame people into taking the desired action. Users see through these tactics, and the result comes off as a one-sided interaction: the company is the only party that benefits from it.

In this article, we’re not going to talk about how to convert and manipulate users with these popups. We’re going to discuss how to use exit-intent popups as a tool for improving the customer experience. The main idea is to increase the [perceived value](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/perceived-value/) of the exit-intent popup and ensure that users get a benefit from the interaction that keeps them on the site. Ideally, the provided benefit should balance the cost of not pursuing their original intent (leaving the site).

### 1. Give an Instant Discount

Our research on [ecommerce promotions](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/applying-discounts/) shows users regularly leave the checkout page when they see an open field for a coupon code and they don’t have a coupon. In this situation, users search the web for a discount code. Instead of making users search the web for a code and possibly losing that sale if they don’t find one (or find a better deal elsewhere), offer the code when it’s contextually relevant: that is, when you notice that a person prepares to leave the site to find one. The user has already added items to the cart, so save her time and pleasantly surprise her with a little incentive to complete the purchase.

![BeautiesLTD exit intent](https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2019/10/07/beautiesltd-exit_intent.jpg)

*Beauties LTD offered free shipping when users tried to leave the checkout process. This popup was good because it provided a benefit without asking the user to do anything in return.*

Note: The language employed (Not interested. I’d rather pay for shipping) is [manipulative](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/shame-users-to-convert/). (Image source: [https://targetbay.com/blog/shopping-cart-abandonment-strategy/](https://targetbay.com/blog/shopping-cart-abandonment-strategy/) )

![Casper exit intent](https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2019/10/07/casper-exit-intent.png)

*Casper.com, an online mattress retailer, anticipated users’ intention to park the page to pull up competitors’ offerings and caught users with an exit-intent popup regardless of the page they’re on. The popup gave users a way to earn a discount by filling out a short survey.*

![Sivana exit intent](https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2019/10/07/sivana-exit-intent.jpg)

*Sivana provided a coupon code (and no manipulink) in an exit-intent popup if the user tried to leave the checkout page. (Image source: https://www.omnikick.com/exit-pop-up-vs-exit-intent-pop-up/ )*

An exit-intent popup doesn’t have to show a coupon to be beneficial to users. Sale items or other useful sales-related information can also deter users from leaving the site.

![sale items exit intent](https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2019/10/07/sale-items-exit-intent.jpg)

*This exit intent popup showed items on sale. The headline,* Don’t Miss Out on These *, suggests that the company cares about the user. (Image source: https://beeketing.com/blog/coupon-popups-dos-donts-to-get-more-clicks/ )*

### 2. Prevent a Mistake

How many times have you thought that you had purchased something online, only to realize later that you did not fully complete the checkout process? This has happened to me more than a handful of times: I thought I had placed an order, but my item never arrived, and when I logged in to my account I discovered that the order didn’t exist.

Of course, in many such situations, the culprit is the interaction flow. Sometimes several confirmation pages appear before the transaction is processed and users lose their place or assume they are finished. Sometimes, the review page looks like a confirmation page, signaling to users that the transaction is complete when it is not. But, occasionally, users may get distracted and try to leave the process too early.

![Pricefalls exit intent](https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2019/10/07/pricefalls-exit-intent-mistake-prevention.png)

*Pricefalls caught users as they tried to leave the browser tab, letting them know that the order had not been placed. The heading* You’re not done yet!*, while fairly polite, is, however, a little too presumptive:  after all, it is still possible that some users intended to abandon their cart. A better heading would be* Are you sure you want to leave without completing your purchase?

### 3. Save Progress

Sometimes users leave a page because they want to finish their task at another time — for example, if the process is too time-consuming and they’re not able to complete it in that moment. Any time users leave a process, there’s a risk that they won’t come back to finish it later, even if they intend to do so. Help them continue their journey and pick up where they left off without losing their progress. Saving progress is useful for any time-consuming task, not just ecommerce purchases.

![KB Authority exit intent](https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2019/10/07/kbauthority-exit-intent-save-progress.jpg)

*KBauthority.com provided an exit-intent popup offering to save unauthenticated users’ shopping carts for later. The wording was polite and helpful. Rather than asking people to sign up for a newsletter, which usually comes off as self-serving, this approach felt as if the company genuinely wanted to help users continue their task later. In this case, it’s to the user’s benefit to provide an email address.*

![99 designs exit intent](https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2019/10/07/99designs-exit-intent.png)

*99designs.com, a crowdsourcing graphic-design service, used an exit-intent popup to remind users to save the progress they made filling out a long creative brief so they don’t have to start over later.*

### 4. Offer Something Else of Value

Before users leave, consider calling their attention to something on the site that might be useful to them — a piece of content or something else they might not have noticed. Take it as an opportunity to show visitors that your site is a helpful resource. Perhaps this final interaction will help build a relationship that will turn into business later.

![greatskinkc exit intent](https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2019/10/07/aestheticare-exit-intent-providing-content.jpg)

*GreatskinKC.com, a medical spa, intercepted users with an exit-intent popup that advertised an online-consultation tool providing a personalized approach to delivering education on the clinic’s medical services, which could resonate with some users.  However, the headline* Before You Go! *sounds like the company wants something from the user; it could be improved by using customer-centric copy such as* Did you try our 1-minute virtual consultation tool?

![Modern met exit intent](https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2019/10/07/mymodernmet-exit-intent-offer-something-valuable.png)

*MyModernMet.com offered a free download of a creative-resource guide in an exit-intent popup. However, the offer asked users to subscribe to its newsletter and could be perceived as an unscrupulous ploy to get email addresses. It would have been better if visitors could download the content without providing an address. Or, the site could ask for the email address only for the delivery of the material, with an option to subscribe to the newsletter as a part of this interaction.*

### 5. Gather Feedback to Serve Them Better

Some websites use exit-intent popups to ask for feedback when users leave the checkout process. Although the feedback ultimately benefits the company, it may genuinely be used to help users in the long run. That said, the request for feedback should appear as user-centric rather than company-centric.

![Lowes exit intent](https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2019/10/07/lowes-exit-intent-feedback.jpg)

*Lowes.com asked for feedback in and exit-intent popup when a large purchase was abandoned in the cart.  Lowe’s language was matter-of-fact and did not indicate that the feedback would benefit the customer in any way.*

![Hilfiger exit intent](https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2019/10/07/tommy-hilfiger-exit-intent-feedback.jpg)

*Tommy Hilfiger also asked for feedback when a user tried to leave the shopping-cart page. The language it used was customer-centric: the company indicated that the feedback would be used to improve the customer experience (* Sharing your experience will help us serve you better *).*

Although the examples above are from ecommerce websites, asking for feedback could be used on many other types of websites.

### 6. Offer to Remind Them Later

Not all users are ready to buy when they’re browsing a website. People often research and compare options with the intent to purchase later. This pattern is common on sites that sell goods and services for a specific occasion or season.

![wedding reception ideas exit intent](https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2019/10/07/myweddingreceptionideas-exit-intent-reminder.png)

*The exit-intent popup on myweddingreceptionideas.com offered to remind the user to come back at a time closer to the date of the wedding. This site knew that users are inundated with options when researching wedding favors and, by offering to provide a reminder, it lightened the user’s burden of keeping track of ideas discovered online.*

### 7. Offer Support

If your offering is complex or if customers might benefit from talking to a human, consider calling attention to your [contact](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/contact-us-pages/) channels, especially if you have [live chat](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/chat-ux/). Users may not come up by themselves with the idea of contacting customer service or they may think that the effort to do so is too big. But, if you can catch users on their way out with a quick path to easy answers, they may just take you up on that offer. If your customer support is easy to use and real-time, this interaction may secure a customer. They’ll likely remember their positive experience with your company.

![sumo exit intent](https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2019/10/07/sumo-exit-intent.jpg)

*Sumo.com called attention to its online chat feature before users left the site. The popup was simple and friendly. It made it easy to initiate a conversation with a knowledgeable staff member. Happily, it was also not pushy: users could simply say* No Thanks *. (Image source: https://blog.useproof.com/exit-intent-popup/ )*

### 8. Direct Users to a Different Channel or to the Next Step of the Customer Journey

A static website experience may not resonate well with every potential customer. Some may benefit from interacting with other media to learn about your offerings, but they may not take the steps to do so on their own. If there are other settings or channels where you can educate customers about your services, alert users about them when they are leaving your site.

![Zendesk exit intent](https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2019/10/07/zendesk-exit-intent.jpg)

*Zendesk.com, a customer-service–software company, offered to schedule a demo of its product to users leaving the page. This suggestion could be helpful to some users who hadn’t considered requesting a live demo on their own.*

This type of popup lowers the [interaction cost](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/interaction-cost-definition/) of searching and locating an alternative channel or supplementary information. It can work well in situations where users need to take action to initiate your services or find information about the options. For example, a box-subscription service may ask users to fill out a short survey in order to recommend a subscription box for them. Presenting the path to that questionnaire may be a quick way to get users into the workflow and to receive benefits immediately.

### 9. Make Users Aware of New Content or Features.

If your website is long established, it can be difficult to communicate new offerings to returning visitors who know your site for old functionalities and use it only for those. If you roll out new features, consider using exit-intent popups to communicate their benefits to users in relevant scenarios.

For example, TripAdvisor has been known over the years as a destination for researching and reviewing travel-related items such as hotels and tourist destinations. In 2015, the site added support for booking hotels. When a user researched hotels on TripAdvisor but left the site without making a booking, the site used an exit-intent pop-up to let her know of the new feature.

![trip advisor exit intent](https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2019/10/07/trip-advisor-exit-intent.jpg)

*TripAdvisor.com used an exit-intent popup to make users aware of a new booking feature. (Image source: https://maxtraffic.com/blog/13-simple-and-surprisingly-effective-exit-pop-up-examples/ )*

### 10.Mention Your User-Friendly Policies

When customers are researching products and services online, they’re [considering multiple options across multiple sites](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/users-interleave-sites-and-genres/). If you have outstanding customer-service policies that differentiate you from your competitors, draw attention to them.

![price match exit intent](https://media.nngroup.com/media/editor/2019/10/07/price-match-gurantee-exit-intent.jpg)

*This popup caught customers on their way out to let them know that the site had a price-match guarantee. Image source: https://www.barilliance.com/exit-intent-popups-increase-conversion-rates-cart-recovery-ability/*

User-friendly policies such as price-match guarantees, money-back guarantees, free samples, and free trials may just be the reason a user chooses your offerings over a competitor’s. Not all customers will search for these policies as part of their research process, so calling special attention to a valuable policy during the [customer journey](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/customer-journeys-omnichannel/) can be a benefit to users.

**Conclusion**

Popups have long been considered [annoying to users](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/most-hated-advertising-techniques/). Often, it’s because they [interrupt users](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/overuse-of-overlays/) during an important task or block out the page content with self-serving messaging. Yes, there have been plenty of examples of [problematic popup](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/popups/) usage. However, exit-intent popups are different, due to the timing of their delivery: they act much like a final message as users are on their way out the door. For this reason, they are less disruptive than [traditional popups](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/modal-nonmodal-dialog/). And when designed correctly, they can be an effective way to deliver a critical message or provide something useful to a site visitor. They are also low-risk: those for whom the message is irrelevant can continue on their way without issue.
